## 1. Satellites — beyond the FM birds (E2A)

*Linear transponders, Doppler craft, keps, and the grid-chasing culture.*

- FACT — **Mode letters, uplink first** (pool E2A05): the letter pair names uplink band then downlink band. The letters as the pool and AMSAT use them: **V = 2 m, U = 70 cm, L = 23 cm, S = 13 cm** (pool E2A09 pins L/S; the V/U convention is documented across study guides, e.g. hamstudy.org's E2A browse page and the ARRL-band-plan satellite subbands below). "Mode" here is a band pair, not FM-vs-SSB and not an orbit class (pool E2A04). `[pool-supported: E2A04, E2A05, E2A09]`
- FACT — Where those bands live in the US band plan (arrl.org/band-plan, verified 2026-07-30): **145.80–146.00 OSCAR subband** (2 m), **435–438 satellite only** (70 cm), **1260–1270 satellite uplinks** (23 cm = L), **2400–2410 amateur satellite** (13 cm = S). QO-100's uplink on 2.4 GHz and the pool's S-band question are the same 13 cm allocation seen from two sides. `[pool-supported: E2A09]` `[practice]`
- FACT — LEO Doppler is a few kHz on VHF and tens of kHz on UHF: about **±3.5 kHz on 2 m, ±10 kHz on 70 cm** (orbitalradar.com Doppler explainer, 2026-07-30). Field habit: start the downlink HIGH and tune down through the pass — one club presentation's rule of thumb is "VHF start 3–5 kHz high, UHF start 10 kHz high, keep tuning down" (McHenry County Wireless Assn satellite deck, 2026-07-30). `[practice]`
- FACT — The FM-bird Doppler rule, from N8HM's AMSAT deck "Getting Started on FM Satellites" (2026-07-30): **on U/v birds adjust the UPLINK** so your signal arrives at the satellite on its design frequency; **on V/u birds (e.g., SO-50) adjust the DOWNLINK** so what you hear stays on frequency. Tune the link with the bigger Doppler — the higher-frequency one. AMSAT's hints page calls this "The One True Rule for Doppler Tuning" (KB5MU) and links a follow-up on computer-driven Full Doppler Tuning. `[practice]`
- A linear transponder is a bent-pipe frequency translator: uplink mixed with a local oscillator, the difference product retransmitted (pool E2A03), so it relays CW, SSB, and data alike (E2A07). On an INVERTING transponder the sidebands swap, band positions reverse, and **uplink and downlink Doppler move in opposite directions and partially cancel** (E2A02) — the operator on an inverting bird has less net tuning to do through a pass than the raw Doppler figures suggest. `[pool-supported: E2A02, E2A03, E2A07]`
- Downlink power discipline: a linear transponder's total downlink power is shared, and an overdriven uplink captures the AGC and steals power from every other user (pool E2A08). The on-air calibration every Tech canon reader already knows: run uplink power so **your downlink is about as strong as the satellite's beacon** (Technician pool, pinned in `/home/kasm-user/your-first-ham-license/canon/pool-technician.json`). `[pool-supported: E2A08]` `[practice]`
- FACT — **QO-100 (Es'hail-2)**, the pool's geostationary-satellite concept (E2A10) in the flesh: launched 2018-11-15, parked at **25.9°E**, the first amateur transponders in geostationary orbit — **2.4 GHz uplink / 10 GHz downlink**, one narrowband (SSB/CW/FT8) and one wideband DATV transponder, footprint "Brazil to Thailand" (radarc.org "Operating on QO-100"; amsat-uk.org QO-100 tag page; 2026-07-30). Aim the dish once and you're done — but the minimum round trip is ~71,600 km, a **~240 ms delay** you hear in your own headphones (g8pqh.uk, 2026-07-30). See Watch item 6 on US access. `[pool-supported: E2A10]` `[practice]`
- FACT — **Keplerian elements** = the orbit-defining parameter set (pool E2A06) distributed as TLEs; "with the TLEs and your station location, you can compute when the satellite will be in view and where to point your antennas" (amsat.org/keplerian-elements-resources, 2026-07-30). Tracking software eats them and predicts passes: Gpredict (free; real-time tracking and pass prediction on NORAD SGP4/SDP4 algorithms, per NASA's Small Spacecraft Technology report) and SatPC32 (AMSAT's hints page links an IC-9700 setup guide). Pass vocabulary: **AOS** (acquisition of signal, bird rises) and **LOS** (loss of signal, bird sets); an **ascending pass** travels south→north (pool E2A01). `[pool-supported: E2A01, E2A06]` `[practice]`
- Circular polarization at the ground station fights spin modulation and Faraday rotation (pool E2A11); AMSAT's hints page hosts I8CVS's two-part article on **switchable** circular polarization — because the sense that works at AOS can be wrong by LOS. `[pool-supported: E2A11]` `[practice]`
- Store-and-forward in practice (pool E2A12): the orbiting mailbox, flown literally by **FalconSAT-3** — AMSAT announced it "open for amateur radio use as a digital store-and-forward system" at 9600 baud, 145.840 up / 435.103 down (amsat.org, 2017): upload a message packet on one pass, someone else pulls it down a continent away on the next. It re-entered in January 2023 (arrl.org news, 2023-01-20); AMSAT's hints page still hosts the how-to guide. Fleet churn is real — Watch item 1. `[pool-supported: E2A12]` `[practice]`
- Roving color: satellite rovers haul an Arrow yagi and a dual-band rig to rare Maidenhead grids, sign **/R**, post planned passes to **hams.at**, and get listed in AMSAT News Service's weekly "Upcoming Satellite Operations" (amsat.org ANS bulletins, 2024–2025 issues checked 2026-07-30 — one rover writes of "another satellite roving expedition" and "pushing past the 100 grids-roved milestone"; another posts passes "to hams.at"). AMSAT's hints page links "How to log Satellite QSOs in Logbook of the World" — rovers log each grid as a separate LoTW location. `[practice]`
- FM vs linear bird etiquette differs in kind: an FM bird passes one QSO at a time (capture effect — loudest wins), so exchanges are short and call-sign-first; a linear transponder passes everyone at once across its passband, so you find your own downlink, drift with Doppler, and share. The pool's ERP question (E2A08) is a linear-bird question; the FM-bird crowding problem is different. See Watch item 2. `[pool-supported: E2A08]` `[practice]`

## 2. EME — the moon as a passive reflector (E3A01–A03, E2D03, E2D06)

*The ultimate weak-signal discipline; where the pool's perigee question earns its keep.*

- FACT — **Round-trip delay ≈ 2.5 seconds** at the Moon's average 384,400 km distance; the delay varies with lunar distance, about **2.4–2.8 s** (moonbounce.dk "What is EME"; bobatkins.com Q65-15 EME notes; 2026-07-30). You can literally listen for your own echo — WSJT-X's **Echo mode** exists for exactly that (WSJT-X 2.7 User Guide §1, fetched 2026-07-30). `[practice]`
- FACT — Path loss: **≈252 dB at 144 MHz, ≈271 dB at 1296 MHz**; the lunar surface reflects only about **6%** of the power that reaches it (electronics-notes.com EME page, 2026-07-30; the 1296 figure independently in a Chalmers EME thesis, 2026-07-30). `[practice]`
- FACT — **Perigee vs apogee is worth ~2 dB** round-trip — "path loss to the moon and back is roughly 2 dB less at perigee than at apogee" (N1BUG EME notes via g1ogy.com mirror, 2026-07-30; electronics-notes, ibid; Ham Radio magazine Feb 1981 computes 1.14 dB one-way → 2.28 dB round trip). WSJT-X's Astronomical Data window reports it as **DGRD** (degradation), running 0 to about −2.1 dB (bobatkins.com EME_101, 2026-07-30). This IS the practice behind the pool's "least path loss at perigee." `[pool-supported: E3A03]`
- FACT — Geometry: any two stations with the Moon mutually visible can try it, up to **~12,000 miles apart** (pool E3A01); about half the globe sees the Moon at once (AB1OC/N1FD "Getting Started in EME" deck, 2024 edition, 2026-07-30). `[pool-supported: E3A01]`
- FACT — Libration fading (pool E3A02) = the fluttery, irregular fading caused by multipath off the Moon's rough, curved, wobbling face — "the received signals become fluttery and distorted because of the multiple reflections from the Moon's curved and irregular surface" (Barron, "Amsats and Hamsats," 2026-07-30). Polarization rotation adds slow deep QSB on top (N1BUG, ibid). `[pool-supported: E3A02]`
- FACT — Who's on the moon: **2 m is the most active EME band**, then (in activity order per AB1OC/N1FD) 6 m, 23 cm, 70 cm. The ARRL band plan reserves EME segments: **144.00–144.05 (CW), 432.00–432.07, 1295.8–1296.08** (arrl.org/band-plan, 2026-07-30). The ARRL International EME Contest runs as **four 48-hour weekends, two for 50–1296 MHz and two for 2.3 GHz and up**, grid square as exchange/multiplier (local: `~/arrl-calendar/src/data/rules.ts` eme entry). `[practice]`
- FACT — Modes: **JT65 was designed for EME** on VHF+ "and is mostly used for that purpose today"; **Q65** is effective for EME and fast-fading paths (tropospheric scatter, rain scatter, ionospheric scatter, TEP) — all from the WSJT-X 2.7 User Guide §1 (fetched 2026-07-30), matching pool E2D03/E2D05. Bob Atkins' Q65 EME notes call Q65 "currently the mode of choice for EME," with JT65 keeping a following on lower bands (2026-07-30). CW EME survives but "requires BIG antennas and LOTS of power" (AB1OC/N1FD). `[pool-supported: E2D03, E2D05]`
- FACT — The time-synchronous alternation the pool tests (E2D06) is the WSJT slow-mode discipline: JT4/JT9/JT65 use **1-minute T/R sequences, so a minimal QSO takes 4–6 minutes**, one station on odd UTC minutes, the other on even (WSJT-X 2.7 User Guide §1, 2026-07-30). Q65 offers 15/30/60/120/300-s sequences (bobatkins.com, 2026-07-30). `[pool-supported: E2D06]`
- Station concept at concept level: the preamp goes **at the antenna, not in the shack** — feedline loss ahead of the preamp sets your system noise figure (QST, July 1985 EME station article via worldradiohistory.org; Barron book: mast preamp with ~10 dB margin over coax loss, very low NF for EME vs medium NF acceptable for satellites). The classic QST arrangement runs separate TX and RX lines with relays energized-to-receive so you can't transmit into the preamp. Big-gun ceiling: a 1985 Central States VHF Society newsletter describes the ever-present 2 m EME stations as running **16 or more very good yagis and high power**. `[practice]`
- Sky-noise color: when the Moon crosses the galactic plane (twice a month) the sky behind it gets noisy and EME systems are sensitive enough to hear it — EME scheduling software carries the data (N1BUG, ibid). Worst at lower frequencies. `[practice]`
- Why digital won: "on VHF bands and higher, QSOs are possible (by EME, scatter, and other propagation types) at signal levels **10 to 15 dB below those required for CW**" (WSJT-X 2.7 User Guide §1, 2026-07-30). Q65's edge over JT65 — averaging multiple receive cycles (pool E2E07) — is how you dig a decode out of libration flutter. `[pool-supported: E2D05, E2E07]`

## 3. Weak-signal VHF+ — SSB/CW territory and the calling frequencies (E2C06)

*The bottom-of-band world where "weak signal" is a place, not a complaint.*

- FACT — The national calling frequencies (arrl.org/band-plan, verified 2026-07-30): **50.125 (6 m SSB), 144.200 (2 m), 222.100, 432.100**; the 70 cm plan reads "432.10 — 70-cm calling frequency." This is the geography behind pool E2C06: in a VHF/UHF contest the CW/SSB crowd sits **in the weak-signal segment, near the calling frequency**. `[pool-supported: E2C06]`
- FACT — The weak-signal segments themselves, same source: 6 m — 50.0–50.1 CW/beacons, 50.1–50.3 SSB/CW; 2 m — 144.00–144.05 EME, 144.05–144.10 CW/weak, 144.10–144.20 EME/weak-signal SSB; 1.25 m — 222.0–222.150 weak-signal; 70 cm — 432.00–432.10 EME/CW, 432.10–432.30 weak-signal work. Calling-frequency custom: raise a station there, then move up the band — that's why activity clusters *near*, not *on* the calling frequency. `[pool-supported: E2C06]` `[practice]`
- FACT — Propagation beacons, same source: **50.060–50.080, 144.275–144.300, 222.05–222.06, 432.30–432.40 MHz**. Beacons are the band-open alarm: you tune the beacon segment, not the white space, to know a band is open. `[practice]`
- FACT — VHF contests exchange **grid squares, not signal reports**: ARRL January/June/September VHF contests — 4-character grid, all legal modes on 50/144/222/432 and up; the **222 MHz and Up** distance contest and the **10 GHz and Up** contest use **6-character** grids and distance-based scoring (local: `~/arrl-calendar/src/data/rules.ts`, entries carrying arrl.org rules URLs). This is exactly the pool's FT8/FT4-in-a-VHF-contest answer (grid square replaces SNR, E2D02). `[pool-supported: E2D02]` `[practice]`
- Rover class exists in ARRL VHF events ("entry categories include single operator, multioperator, rover, and club" — rules.ts 222-Up entry): a rover drives grid to grid re-activating each one, and everyone else chases them for multipliers. `[practice]`
- Big-gun anatomy (concept level): tall tower, long yagis or stacks, legal-limit amplifier, **mast-mounted low-noise preamp** — the preamp belongs at the antenna because coax loss before it degrades noise figure dB-for-dB; but keep gain modest (~10 dB over line loss) or the preamp overloads and costs you dynamic range (Barron book, 2026-07-30). `[practice]`
- The activity rhythm: make contact on the calling frequency, then QSY up into the weak-signal segment to chat; contest weekends the action clusters near the calling frequency and spreads upward through the segment — exactly the pattern pool E2C06 describes. `[pool-supported: E2C06]` `[practice]`

## 4. Exotic-propagation operating — meteors, aurora, TEP, ducts, Es (E2D01, E3A06–A13, E3B)

*Where the physics questions meet someone actually pointing a beam.*

**Meteor scatter (E2D01, E3A08, E3A09)**

- FACT — Burst nature, pinned by the protocol paper (Franke & Taylor, "The MSK144 Protocol for Meteor-Scatter Communication," QEX Sept/Oct 2017, arrl.org, fetched 2026-07-30): modern digital MS works **pings shorter than 0.1 s**, any day of the year, out to ~1,300 miles; the rare long "blue whizzer" trails last **several seconds**; ping duration scales as **1/f²**, so pings at 144 MHz are about **1/8 as long** as at 50 MHz. Trails ionize at E-region height (pool E3A08); the pool's best-scatter range 28–148 MHz (E3A09) is why 6 m and 2 m dominate. `[pool-supported: E3A08, E3A09]`
- FACT — MSK144 mechanics (same QEX paper): 144-bit frames at 2000 baud → a complete message every **72 ms**, repeated through a **15 s T/R sequence**; effective rate 250 chars/s; decodes to **−8 dB SNR in 2500 Hz**; a 20 ms short format (reports, RRR, 73) exists for the shorter 2 m pings. 6 m "calling frequency" **50.260 MHz by informal convention**; 100 W and a small yagi works meteor scatter on 6 m. This is pool E2D01 in operation. `[pool-supported: E2D01]`
- Lineage color (same paper): European HSCW operators ran 10–40 chars/s Morse off slowed cassette tapes in the 1960s–70s, up to 150 cps by ~2000; K1JT's FSK441 (147 cps, 2001) was the first digital MS mode; MSK144 (2016) replaced it. K5ND's getting-started piece adds the operator reality: a 6 m MSK144 QSO can take 16 minutes of mostly silence, a 2 m one an hour; skeds get arranged on pingjockey.net (n1nc.org newsletter, 2026-07-30). `[practice]`

**Aurora (E3A12, E3A13)**

- FACT — **Beam north first**: "Regardless of your station location, beam north initially whenever auroral activity is suspected. When stations are heard, swing the beam either side of north to maximize the signals" (VHF/UHF DX Book, qsl.net mirror, 2026-07-30); both stations point at the curtain, not at each other (IVARC propagation talk, 2026-07-30). `[practice]`
- FACT — Why CW wins (pool E3A13): the moving curtain imposes fast, erratic Doppler that spreads a pure CW carrier into a **buzz a few kHz wide** and turns SSB into "mushy, unintelligible garble" (WorldRadio, June 1999, worldradiohistory.org; ARRL Handbook propagation chapter, msr.zapto.org mirror, 2026-07-30). Distortion grows with frequency — **6 m is easier than 2 m, 2 m easier than 70 cm** (IVARC); typical paths 500–2,000 km on 2 m (IVARC), ~800 km and more per electronics-notes (2026-07-30). `[pool-supported: E3A12, E3A13]`
- FACT — Trigger and watch: auroral propagation follows **severe geomagnetic storms** (pool E3A12); operators watch for M/X-class flares and CME arrivals on NOAA/spaceweather (G4MCU aurora guide, 2026-07-30) — same skill as reading the A/K indices in E3C02–E3C03. Bonus mode: auroral E, where the Doppler distortion vanishes and signals behave like sporadic E — typical max 2,300 km, sometimes 5,000 km on 28/50 MHz (ARRL Handbook ch. 21, ibid). `[pool-supported: E3A12, E3A13]` `[practice]`

**Transequatorial (E3B01–E3B03)**

- FACT — Discovery and shape: first amateur TEP was **August 1947, Mexico–Argentina on 50 MHz**, late afternoon/early evening (johnsonfrancis.org TEP article, 2026-07-30; Ham Radio magazine, July 1984, worldradiohistory.org). Two flavors: **afternoon TEP** (~1500–1900 local, strong steady signals, MUF to ~60 MHz, paths 5,000–6,500 km) and **evening TEP** (~2000–2300 local, Doppler spread, rapid fading) with contacts on 2 m and sometimes 70 cm (amateur-radio-wiki.net TEP page; johnsonfrancis.org, ibid; 2026-07-30). `[pool-supported: E3B01, E3B03]`
- FACT — Geometry (Ham Radio 1984 / N5DUX VHF-UHF primer, 2026-07-30): stations sit **~1,500–2,500 miles north and south of the geomagnetic equator** with the path crossing it near right angles (±20°); signals duct between the two equatorial-anomaly electron peaks with **no intermediate ground reflection**. Better near equinoxes and high sunspot numbers. Pool numbers — 2,000–3,000 mi separation, 5,000 mi max, afternoon/early evening (E3B01–B03) — sit inside these practice figures. `[pool-supported: E3B01, E3B02, E3B03]`

**Ducts & sporadic E (E3A07, E3A11, E3B09, E3B11)**

- Tropospheric ducting is the most common VHF enhancement — old hands call a duct opening a **"lift"** (IVARC, 2026-07-30); the pool pins ducts forming over large bodies of water with 100–300 mile typical microwave range (E3A07, E3A11). Weather-reading habit: watch for high-pressure temperature inversions settling in over water paths. `[pool-supported: E3A07, E3A11]` `[practice]`
- Sporadic-E season is the VHF operator's summer: peaks around the solstices, especially the summer solstice, and it's a daylight mode (pool E3B09, E3B11) — which is why the **June ARRL VHF Contest (second full weekend of June) lands in prime Es season** and 6 m earns its "magic band" name (rules.ts junVhf entry; K5ND "Guide to 6 Meter DXing," 2026-07-30). `[pool-supported: E3B09, E3B11]` `[practice]`
- Night fallback: when darkness drops the MUF below your band, move DOWN (pool E3A06) — HF contesters plan the evening migration 20→40→80/160 the same way VHF operators watch for the evening tropo build. `[pool-supported: E3A06]` `[practice]`

## 5. Digital weak-signal practice — FT8/FT4/MSK144/WSPR/Q65 (E2D02, E2E02–E2E07, E2E10)

*Clock discipline, sequence discipline, and where to point the dial.*

- FACT — Sequence lengths as the operator lives them (WSJT-X 2.7 User Guide §1, fetched 2026-07-30): **FT8 15 s** (pool E2E06), **FT4 7.5 s** built for contesting, JT65/JT9 1 minute, MSK144 5/10/15/30 s selectable. FT4's contest build shows in WSJT-X itself: a **"Best S+P"** button that picks the best decoded CQ — new multiplier first, new call second — and answers it (OnAllBands "FT8 vs. FT4," 2026-07-30). `[pool-supported: E2E03, E2E06]`
- FACT — Clock discipline (pool E2E02): synchronized computer clocks are the mode; WSJT-X system requirements say **sync to UTC within ±1 second** and warn the built-in Windows time sync is usually not adequate (User Guide §2, 2026-07-30). Waterfall full of signals and zero decodes = check the clock. `[pool-supported: E2E02]`
- FACT — 6 m digital dial: **FT8 on 50.313, with 50.323 the intercontinental/Europe-to-USA convention; FT4 on 50.318** (OnAllBands "FT8: Frequencies, Decibels, and Message Meanings," 2026-07-30; K5ND's 6 m DXing guide monitors both 50.313 and 50.323; 50.318 per hamdeck.com digital-frequency reference, 2026-07-30). In a VHF contest the exchange is your **grid square** in place of the SNR report (pool E2D02). See Watch item 4. `[pool-supported: E2D02]`
- FACT — WSPR is beacon-only, no keyboard-to-keyboard (pool E2E05): 2-minute sequences carrying **call sign, grid locator, and power in dBm**, decodable to **−31 dB SNR in 2500 Hz**, reports auto-uploaded to WSPRnet's map (WSJT-X 2.7 User Guide §1, 2026-07-30). Practice: run it overnight to A/B two antennas — the map tells you which one won. See Watch item 3 on the older −28 dB figure. `[pool-supported: E2E05]` `[practice]`
- FACT — Q65 = the EME/scatter mode (pool E2D03): "particularly effective for tropospheric scatter, rain scatter, ionospheric scatter, TEP, and EME on VHF and higher bands" (WSJT-X 2.7 Guide §1); Q65-30A (30-second) is the 6 m standard submode (K5ND Q65 article, 2026-07-30); WSJT-X 2.7 added **Q65 Pileup** mode aimed at 6 m EME DXpeditions and the QMAP companion receiver (User Guide §1.1, fetched 2026-07-30). Where JT65 doesn't, **Q65 averages multiple receive cycles** (pool E2E07) — the decoder stacks repeats until the message emerges. `[pool-supported: E2D03, E2E07]`
- FACT — The decoder's payoff, worth quoting to students: weak-signal QSOs on VHF+ are possible "at signal levels **10 to 15 dB below those required for CW**" (WSJT-X 2.7 Guide §1). JT65 itself was built for EME (pool E2D05) using 65-tone FSK (pool E2D09). `[pool-supported: E2D05, E2D09]`
- Narrowest-of-the-list color (pool E2E10): an FT8 signal occupies ~50 Hz, so dozens of stations share one waterfall at different audio offsets — on a crowded 6 m opening you watch the whole 3 kHz window at once instead of tuning station to station. `[pool-supported: E2E10]` `[practice]`
- Duty-cycle caution for VHF operators new to digital: FT8/FT4/MSK144 transmit at ~100% duty for whole sequences — VHF brick amplifiers and finals appreciate half power. (Same caution the General note pins for HF; applies doubly to fan-less VHF amps.) `[practice]`

## 6. Contest & DX craft at depth (E2C)

*The techniques the pool names and the ones it only hints at.*

- FACT — Split pileup dimensions, from N7NG's ARRL/INDEXA "DXpeditioning Basics" (arrl.org/files/file/DXCC/dx-basics.pdf, fetched 2026-07-30): keep the listening range tight — "no more than **30 kHz** will ever be necessary on SSB while **10 kHz** is more than enough on CW." Same source documents the tradition of the DX station transmitting "just outside of the U.S. phone band" — a tactic intended, N7NG notes, to minimize callers transmitting on the DX frequency, though in his experience it has almost no effect because most such transmissions are inadvertent. This is the practice under pool E2C10's three true reasons for split. `[pool-supported: E2C10]`
- FACT — **Cut numbers, the full set**: digits shortened in contest exchanges are **1=A, 5=E, 9=N, 0=T** — and 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8 "are usually not abbreviated" (Ethics and Operating Procedures for the Radio Amateur §II.9.22, wr4cc.org mirror, 2026-07-30). So "5NN" = 599 and a serial "ANT" parses as 190. Radio keyers optionally extend the map (Yaesu manuals offer U=2, V=3, B=7, D=8 formats, 2026-07-30). `[practice]`
- FACT — **Super Check Partial**: a callsign database "built from Cabrillo logs contributed by contesters" (supercheckpartial.com, 2026-07-30) that lets the logger suggest full calls as you type a partial; it began with K1EA's CT and is now supported by all major loggers (K4RO, OnAllBands contesting-101 article, 2026-07-30). The coach's rule from the Contest University RTTY deck: "Log what is communicated to you!" — SCP is a hint, not the contact. `[practice]`
- FACT — **SO2R** (single operator, two radios): one radio "runs" (calls CQ) on one band while the second hunts multipliers on a different band; an interlock ensures only one signal transmits at a time, since most rules allow a single operator just one signal (qsl.net/zs1an Contesting FAQ, 2026-07-30). It's a technique, not an entry class — you still enter as single operator. `[practice]`
- FACT — The **multi-single 10-minute rule** that shapes big-station band strategy (CQ WW rules, cqww.com/rules.htm, 2026-07-30): only one transmitted signal on one band during any 10-minute period (the "run" station); one additional "multiplier" signal may transmit only on a different band and only to work a new multiplier; run and multiplier transmitters have independent 10-minute clocks starting with the first QSO on the band. `[practice]`
- Pileup discipline the pool tests (E2C11: send your full call once or twice): the DX station often comes back to a partial — "the Alpha-Bravo station only" — and everyone not matching goes silent. The General-book note covers caller-side manners; N7NG adds the DX-side view that a well-managed pileup mirrors the DX operator's rhythm. `[pool-supported: E2C11]` `[practice]`
- FACT — 30 m is contest-free by long convention (pool E2C03); the ARRL VHF/UHF events fill the calendar instead — January, June, September VHF contests (grid-square exchange, rules.ts), the August 222-Up and 10 GHz-Up distance contests, and the EME contest weekends (§2). `[pool-supported: E2C03]` `[practice]`
- Paper trail the pool tests: ADIF for log interchange, Cabrillo for contest-log submission, LoTW for confirmations of every flavor (E2C02/C07/C08). Satellite flavor: AMSAT's hints page links the how-to for logging satellite QSOs — and rover /R logs — in LoTW (2026-07-30); QSL managers (E2C05) exist so a DX station's confirmations get handled without burying the operator. `[pool-supported: E2C02, E2C05, E2C07, E2C08]` `[practice]`

## 7. Extra-segment customs — what's actually down there (E1A cross-reference)

*The slices you upgraded for, and what lives in them.*

- FACT — The Extra-only HF segments, exactly (canon `r1-part97.md` §1, §97.301(b) vs (d), verified 2026-07-28): **3.500–3.525, 3.600–3.800, 7.000–7.025, 7.125–7.175 (R2), 14.000–14.025, 14.150–14.225, 21.000–21.025, 21.200–21.275 MHz** — Extra-exclusive spectrum exists only on 80, 75, 40, 20, and 15 m. `[pool-supported: E1A cross-ref]`
- FACT — The ARRL Extra Class License Manual's own sales pitch (12th ed., qsl.net/wb4bxw mirror, 2026-07-30): the Extra slices "are prime real estate… Traditional operating practice is for DX contacts to take place closer to the bottom edge of the bands… nearly all serious DXers and contest operators hold Extra class licenses because that's where the contacts are most likely to be made… the Extra class segments are less crowded." `[practice]`
- FACT — Band-plan DX windows adjacent to those slices (arrl.org/band-plan, verified 2026-07-30): **3.790–3.800 MHz** on 75 m, plus RTTY-DX spots **3.590 and 7.040 MHz** — voluntary stay-clear segments where locals keep quiet so weak DX can be heard, enforced socially like every band-plan custom. `[practice]`
- Historical color: the bottom-25-kHz Extra telegraphy slices are a late-1960s **incentive-licensing** artifact — QST's August 1969 coverage of the docket already refers to "the Extra Class telegraphy allocations at the low end" of 40 and 20 m at their "present 25 kHz" width (worldradiohistory.org, 2026-07-30). `[practice]`
- How it plays on the air: a rare-DX CW operation camped at 3.505 or 14.005 MHz can only be answered by US Extras (and non-US stations) — thinner pileups, exactly the ARRL manual's point; on phone, N7NG documents the mirror-image habit of the DX transmitting just outside the US phone edge. Frame as custom and geography, not a rule of the airwaves. `[practice]`
- 60 m is NOT an Extra prize: channels are shared by General and above under the 100 W ERP cap (canon `r1-part97.md` §2 and its W1 watch item — current rules are four channels plus the contiguous segment at 9.15 W ERP). `[practice]`

## Watch items (pool framing vs. common practice; time-sensitive pins; unverified claims)

1. **Satellite fleet churn.** Every named bird in this note (SO-50, FalconSAT-3, QO-100) is a time-sensitive operational fact; LEO satellites fail and re-enter without ceremony — FalconSAT-3 itself re-entered in January 2023 — and FM/linear availability shifts month to month. Before print, check the AMSAT status page (amsat.org/status/) and drop or demote any bird that has gone quiet. The pool itself tests only concepts (E2A), never specific satellites — keep the book that way.
2. **Linear vs FM discipline is two different etiquettes.** Pool E2A08 (limit ERP to protect the shared downlink) is a linear-transponder question; on FM birds the failure mode is capture-effect hogging, not AGC theft. Don't let a chapter merge them into one "satellite power" rule.
3. **WSPR sensitivity figure drifted.** The WSJT-X 2.7.0 User Guide says −31 dB in 2500 Hz; older WSPR documentation (e.g., the classic wsprnet-era description mirrored at qsl.net/dl2wb and k7uv.com) says −28 dB. This note pins −31 from the current guide; if a chapter quotes the older number, attribute it to "earlier WSPR documentation."
4. **6 m digital frequencies are convention, not plan.** 50.260 (MSK144 calling, "informal convention" per the QEX paper), 50.313/50.323 (FT8), 50.318 (FT4) all drift with usage and differ regionally — re-verify against the current WSJT-X default frequency table and ARRL band plan before print. The pool pins none of these (E2D02 tests the grid-square exchange, not a frequency).
5. **Pool-vs-practice on Es timing.** Pool E3B11 says sporadic E is most likely between sunrise and sunset; 6 m operators also work plenty of evening Es (r3 already flags this for E3B11). This note's "summer Es season" color (§4) is compatible — but don't print "Es ends at sunset."
6. **QO-100 is not a US operating opportunity.** Its footprint runs Brazil-to-Thailand; of the Americas only northeast Brazil is inside (jeremyclark.ca QO-100 planning notes, 2026-07-30). Use it in the book as the geostationary *example* (E2A10), explicitly noting continental-US readers can't reach it — otherwise expect mail.
7. **EME figures are band-specific.** The 252/271 dB path-loss pins are 144 MHz and 1296 MHz respectively; the ~2 dB perigee/apogee swing is round-trip and frequency-independent. Don't let a chapter generalize "EME path loss is 252 dB" without the band.
8. **Doppler magnitudes vary with source and orbit.** ±3.5 kHz (2 m) / ±10 kHz (70 cm) are typical-LEO figures (orbitalradar.com); club decks say "3–5 kHz / 10 kHz" for the same idea. Present as order-of-magnitude practice, not constants.
9. **UNVERIFIED items: none.** Every FACT above carries a pool ID, a canon pin, or a URL content-checked 2026-07-30. Deliberately excluded for lack of a citable source: the MSK144/Q65 regional "who transmits first period" convention; the famous California–Hawaii tropo duct path specifics; any claim about which big-gun station holds which record; exact auroral ranges on bands other than 2 m.
