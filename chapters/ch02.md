## 2. Operating beyond the General Bands: Satellites, TV, Digital, DX

Aim your beam at the horizon and you know the game: skip, ducting, the occasional surprise opening. Now tilt that beam up. On a good pass, a linear-transponder satellite drifts overhead with its whole passband alive at once — a dozen QSOs stacked side by side, CW low in the passband and SSB above, all of it relayed by one bent pipe hundreds of miles up, all of it sliding with Doppler while you tune. The hobby continues straight up, and satellites are only the start. There are operators who bounce signals off the Moon, who complete 2 meter QSOs on meteor pings that live for milliseconds, who send live television on 70 centimeters, and who run VHF contests with grid squares instead of signal reports. In this chapter you'll learn the operating craft behind all of it — how a satellite hears and talks back, what the mode letters really name, how EME and weak-signal VHF work, what amateur television involves, and how the digital modes move their bits. This is pool subelement E2 — Operating Procedures: 60 questions in five groups, E2A through E2E, five of which will be on your exam.

### How a satellite hears and talks back

The FM birds are single-channel repeaters in the sky, and you may know them already. A **linear transponder** is something else entirely: a bent-pipe frequency translator. The satellite takes a slice of uplink spectrum, mixes it with a local oscillator, and retransmits the difference product on the downlink band (E2A03). Nothing is demodulated and nothing is repeated channel by channel — whatever lands in the passband comes back out, frequency-shifted. Because the translator is linear, it relays any mode you put into it — FM, CW, SSB, SSTV, PSK, or packet (E2A07) — and it relays all of them at once, each signal holding its place in the passband. That is why one linear passband can carry a dozen simultaneous QSOs while an FM bird carries exactly one.

{{fig:ch02-linear-transponder}}

Many transponders are built **inverting**, and the pool asks what that means with an all-of-the-above answer (E2A02): USB on the uplink becomes LSB on the downlink, and vice versa; your signal's position in the band is reversed — tune up on the uplink and your downlink moves down; and the uplink and downlink Doppler shifts run in opposite directions, so they partially cancel. That last truth is the operator's gift: on an inverting bird you do less net retuning through a pass than the raw Doppler numbers suggest. Learn the three as a set, because the distractors offer them one at a time.

A transponder's downlink power is a shared resource, and that drives the central etiquette of the linear birds (E2A08). Run too much uplink ERP and your signal captures the transponder's AGC — the gain drops for the whole passband, and your loud signal steals downlink power from every other user. The calibration rule is the one you learned as a Technician: adjust uplink power so your own downlink comes back about as strong as the satellite's beacon. File that discipline under linear transponders, where it belongs. On an FM bird the failure mode is different — the capture effect lets the loudest signal own the single channel — and the pool's AGC-theft question is a linear-bird question. Never merge the two etiquettes.

Then there is the geometry. An **ascending pass** travels south to north — crossing the equator going up (E2A01) — and a descending pass comes down the other way. **AOS**, acquisition of signal, is the moment the bird rises above your horizon; **LOS**, loss of signal, is when it sets. Tracking software predicts both from **Keplerian elements**, the parameter set that defines the orbit (E2A06), distributed as two-line element sets — TLEs. Feed the TLEs and your station location to the software and it tells you when the satellite will be in view and where to point. A **geostationary** satellite is the special case: parked over the equator at the altitude where the orbital period matches Earth's rotation, it appears to stay in one position in the sky (E2A10) — aim the dish once and you are done. The living example is QO-100, launched in November 2018 and parked at 25.9°E: 2.4 GHz up, 10 GHz down, one narrowband transponder for SSB, CW, and FT8 and one wideband transponder for digital amateur TV, with a footprint running Brazil to Thailand. Admire it as *the* geostationary example — and note that the continental US sits outside that footprint, so it is not an operating opportunity for you. Two more craft items: a rotating satellite flutters as its antennas turn (spin modulation), and the ionosphere rotates the signal's polarization on the way through (Faraday rotation). A circularly polarized ground-station antenna minimizes the effects of both (E2A11), because a circular wave stays circular no matter how the far end has twisted.

Last, the orbiting mailbox. A **store-and-forward** satellite holds digital messages for later download (E2A12): upload a packet on one pass, and an operator a continent away pulls it down on the next. FalconSAT-3 flew exactly that service at 9600 baud until it re-entered in January 2023 — the cautionary tale in miniature. Every named satellite is a time-sensitive fact; fleets fail and re-enter without ceremony, so the pool tests concepts, never specific birds, and this chapter follows the same rule. The rover culture is the durable part: satellite rovers haul a small dual-band yagi and a dual-band rig to rare grid squares, sign /R, post their planned passes to hams.at, and log each grid as a separate location so the chasers get their confirmations.

One bookkeeping note before the exam questions: E2A ends at E2A12 — a thirteenth question was withdrawn by the pool's second errata in November 2024, and because it was the group's last, its removal leaves no numbering gap. Transponders, orbits, and passes are pool group E2A.

### The mode letters: uplink first

Ask a satellite operator what mode a bird is and the answer comes back as a pair of letters — U/V, V/U, L/S. Here is the pinned law of the section: a satellite's "mode" is its uplink/downlink **band pair** — not FM versus SSB, and not an orbit class (E2A04) — and the mode letters specify the uplink and downlink frequency ranges, **uplink letter first** (E2A05). The letters are microwave band names: **V = 2 m, U = 70 cm, L = 23 cm, S = 13 cm** (E2A09 pins the L and S pair). Read them as band letters, not words — L is not "long" and S is not "short." So "mode U/V" means you transmit up on 70 centimeters and listen down on 2 meters; "mode V/U" runs the other way.

{{fig:ch02-satellite-modes}}

Those bands are real places in the US band plan. On 2 meters the OSCAR subband is 145.80–146.00 MHz. On 70 centimeters, 435–438 MHz is satellite-only. On 23 centimeters, 1260–1270 MHz holds the satellite uplinks. On 13 centimeters, 2400–2410 MHz is the amateur-satellite segment — QO-100's 2.4 GHz uplink and the pool's S-band question are the same 13 cm allocation seen from two sides.

The letters also tell you where the Doppler work is. A low-Earth-orbit pass slides every frequency on the link: a few kHz on VHF, tens of kHz on UHF — about ±3.5 kHz on 2 meters and ±10 kHz on 70 centimeters as typical-LEO magnitudes. Treat those as order-of-magnitude practice, never constants; orbits differ. The rule that follows is the one satellite operators quote chapter and verse: tune the link with the bigger Doppler, which is always the higher-frequency one. On a U/v bird — 70 cm up, 2 m down — you adjust the **uplink** as the pass progresses so your signal keeps arriving at the satellite on its design frequency, and the 2 m downlink barely moves. On a V/u bird you hold your 2 m uplink steady and adjust the **downlink** you are listening to. The field habit that goes with it: start the downlink high and tune down through the pass. The capital letter in the mode pair is doing the telling — whichever side carries the U gets the tuning hand.

The mode designators close out E2A: what "mode" means (E2A04), which letter comes first (E2A05), and which bands L and S name (E2A09).

### EME: the Moon as a passive reflector

Long before anyone put a transponder in orbit, operators were bouncing signals off the Moon. **Earth–Moon–Earth** — EME, moonbounce — treats the Moon as a passive reflector: any two stations with the Moon mutually visible can attempt a contact, up to about 12,000 miles apart measured along the Earth's surface (E3A01; chapter 3 owns the propagation physics, this section owns the operating discipline).

Start with what the path costs. The round trip takes about 2.5 seconds at the Moon's average 384,400 km distance — roughly 2.4 to 2.8 seconds as lunar distance varies — and WSJT-X ships an Echo mode so you can hear your own signal come back. Then the power budget: the lunar surface is a poor mirror, reflecting only about 6 percent of the power that reaches it, and the path loss runs ≈252 dB at 144 MHz and ≈271 dB at 1296 MHz. Pin each figure to its band — those are band-specific numbers, and "EME path loss is 252 dB" without the band attached is wrong. The Moon's orbit hands you one knob: distance varies through the month, and perigee versus apogee is worth about 2 dB round trip — the least EME path loss occurs at perigee (the practice behind E3A03). And the Moon wobbles. **Libration fading** is the fluttery, irregular fading caused by multipath off the rough, curved, wobbling face (E3A02) — the reason a moonbounce signal can shimmer and vanish mid-transmission.

> **Worked example:** Your own voice comes back from the Moon: the round trip is 2 × 384,400 km at 300,000 km/s ≈ 2.56 s — the two-and-a-half-second delay that makes EME QSOs feel like correspondence (WSJT-X's Echo mode exists to let you hear it). At 144 MHz the path costs ≈252 dB and the lunar surface returns only about 6 percent of what arrives — which is why EME stations run big antennas, low-noise preamps at the antenna, and digital modes that decode 10–15 dB below CW.

The counterattack has three parts. First, antenna gain on both ends — the serious EME stations run long yagis or arrays of them. Second, a low-noise preamp **at the antenna**, not in the shack: every dB of feed line loss ahead of the preamp adds directly to your system noise figure, so the preamp lives on the mast. Third — and this is the pool's point — digital modes. **JT65 was designed for EME** (E2D05), a multitone-AFSK mode whose name carries its 65 tones (E2D09), and **Q65** — which averages multiple receive cycles where JT65 does not (E2E07) — is effective for EME and for fast-fading scatter paths (E2D03). On VHF and up, these modes decode 10 to 15 dB below what CW needs, which is why a modest station with good software now works the Moon.

The discipline the pool actually tests is timing (E2D06): EME runs on **time-synchronous transmissions alternating between stations**. In the JT slow modes the sequences are one minute long — one station transmits on odd UTC minutes, the other on even, and a minimal QSO takes four to six minutes of patient alternation. Q65 offers 15-, 30-, 60-, 120-, and 300-second sequences, trading decoding depth against speed. Nothing about this is conversational; it is choreography, and both computers keep the beat. Where to point the dial: 2 meters is the most active EME band, and the ARRL band plan reserves EME segments at 144.00–144.05, 432.00–432.07, and 1295.8–1296.08 MHz. Twice a month the Moon crosses the galactic plane and the sky behind it gets noisy — EME systems are sensitive enough to hear the difference, and scheduling software carries the data.

EME's exam presence sits in E2D — Q65 for EME (E2D03) and the synchronous alternation (E2D06) — with the propagation numbers cross-pointed to chapter 3's E3A.

### Weak-signal VHF+ and the contest/DX craft

On the VHF and UHF bands, "weak signal" is not a complaint — it is a place. The bottom of each band belongs to CW and SSB operators working far beyond line of sight, and the geography is worth learning exactly as the pool pins it. Each band has a national calling frequency: 50.125 MHz on 6 meters, 144.200 on 2 meters, 222.100 on 1.25 meters, and 432.100 on 70 centimeters. Custom says you raise a station on the calling frequency and then both of you move up the band to talk — which is exactly why, when a contest or an opening brings the crowd out, the highest level of SSB/CW activity sits in the weak-signal segment with most of the activity near the calling frequency, not on it (E2C06). The band-open alarm is the beacon segment, not the white space: 50.060–50.080, 144.275–144.300, 222.05–222.06, and 432.30–432.40 MHz hold automated propagation beacons, and a quick tune across them tells you whether the band is open before you call anyone.

The contest craft differs from HF in one exam-tested way: **VHF contests exchange grid squares, not signal reports** — the four-character Maidenhead locator on 50 MHz and up, six characters in the distance contests — because the multipliers come from geography, not readability. That is why FT8 and FT4 in a VHF contest exchange a grid square in place of the SNR report (E2D02): the software's contest mode swaps the field. The culture has its own athletes, too — rovers drive grid to grid, re-activating each one while everyone else chases them for multipliers. One HF-side convention also lands here: 30 meters is contest-free. Say it the pool's way every time — the WARC-band truce is long-standing custom, **convention, not regulation**: no FCC rule excludes contests from 30 meters, and the pool's own wording is "generally excluded" (E2C03).

The DX craft carries over from your HF life with three pinned answers. Split operation serves all three of its purposes at once (E2C10, another all-of-the-above): it keeps the callers off the DX station's transmit frequency so the rare one stays audible, it improves efficiency for everyone, and it lets stations call where they are licensed — a General who may not transmit on the DX station's Extra-segment frequency can still call up the band. In the pileup itself, send your full call sign once or twice (E2C11) — not your last two letters, and not a continuous stream. And when the confirmations matter, a QSL manager handles the receiving and sending of a DX station's confirmations (E2C05).

The paper trail has its own vocabulary, and the pool shuffles two of the terms as distractors: **ADIF** is the format for exchanging amateur radio log data — moving contacts between logging programs — while **Cabrillo** is the standard for submitting electronic contest logs to the sponsor (E2C02, E2C07). Logbook of The World sits above both: LoTW confirms contacts of every flavor — special-event, DX, and WAS alike (E2C08, keyed all-of-the-above).

Two modern operating questions round out the group, and both are taught flatly. A US-licensed operator running a US-located transmitter by remote control needs **no additional station-identification indicator** beyond normal Part 97 ID (E2C01) — §97.119(c) permits indicators but does not require them, and none is prescribed for remote control. Many Generals remember otherwise; pin it. The network under that remote link has a name for its sluggishness: **latency** is the delay between a control operator action and the corresponding change in the transmitted signal (E2C12) — the distractors (jitter, hang time, anti-VOX) are real terms with other meanings. Last, mesh: amateur mesh networks run on frequencies shared with unlicensed wireless data services (E2C04), built from wireless routers running custom firmware such as AREDN (E2C09), with nodes forming the network through discovery and link-establishment protocols — a different animal from packet digipeating.

That is pool group E2C in full — contest and DX practice, log formats, mesh, and remote control — plus E2D02's grid-square exchange.

### Amateur television

Television is the oldest of the exotic modes, and its pool group is nearly pure standards knowledge: learn the vocabulary and the questions are free.

Fast-scan ATV is full-motion television on the old broadcast standard: NTSC paints **525 lines per frame** (E2B02), **interlaced** — the odd-numbered lines are scanned in one field, the even-numbered lines in the next (E2B03), and the two fields lock together into each frame. The modulation is **vestigial sideband**: amplitude modulation in which one complete sideband and a portion of the other are transmitted (E2B06). The point of the vestige is bandwidth with fidelity — VSB reduces the transmitted bandwidth while increasing the fidelity of the low-frequency video components (E2B05), the part of the signal that carries the picture's structure. The pleasant hack: commercial analog TV receivers can watch 70-centimeter fast-scan ATV because amateurs transmit on channels shared with cable TV (E2B08).

Slow-scan television trades motion for spectrum: a still picture sent over an ordinary voice channel, one line at a time. In analog SSTV, **brightness is encoded by tone frequency** (E2B10) — brighter pixels are higher tones — and color is sent as sequential color lines (E2B04), the picture transmitted once per color component. Two tone conventions run the protocol. A **VIS code** — vertical interval signaling — sent at the start identifies the SSTV mode being used, so the receiving software can auto-select the right decoder (E2B11); it names the mode, not the station, and despite the name it is not the vertical sync. Then specific tone frequencies signal the receiving software to begin each new picture line (E2B12). There is a digital branch as well: SSTV using the Digital Radio Mondiale (DRM) protocol can be received and decoded on an ordinary SSB receiver (E2B09).

Digital amateur TV borrows broadcast standards wholesale. A **coding rate** of 3/4 means 25 percent of the data sent is forward-error-correction data (E2B01) — three payload bits out of every four transmitted, the fourth buying correction. And amateur DVB-T, the European digital-broadcast standard hams adopted, uses QAM and QPSK modulation (E2B07).

That is E2B: twelve questions of standards vocabulary, deliberately carrying no operating color — the reservoir here is the standards themselves.

### Digital modes in the field: MSK144, APRS, JT65

Three digital mode families do the field work of this chapter, and the pool's anchor is the mode-to-job map: **MSK144 → meteor scatter** (E2D01), **Q65 → EME** (E2D03, from the moonbounce section), **APRS → real-time balloon tracking** (E2D04), and **JT65 → very-low-SNR decodes** (E2D05) using multitone AFSK (E2D09). The distractors shuffle the MSK144/Q65/JT65 triplet constantly, so anchor each mode to the physics it was built for: meteor pings live for milliseconds, so the meteor mode packs a complete message into a blink; EME drowns in noise, so the moon mode averages patiently.

> **Worked example:** Why MSK144 wins the meteor game: a usable meteor trail lives for milliseconds. MSK144 packs a complete message into 144 bits sent at 2000 baud — $144 / 2000 = 0.072$ seconds, a full frame every 72 ms, repeated through a 15-second T/R sequence until one ping carries it through (E2D01). It decodes down to −8 dB SNR in 2500 Hz, and a 20 ms short format exists for the shorter pings on 2 meters — ping duration scales as 1/f², so 144 MHz pings run about an eighth as long as 50 MHz pings.

The operating reality matches the math: a 6 meter MSK144 QSO can take many minutes of mostly silence, waiting for the trail that carries the missing fragment, which is why meteor operators arrange schedules on public sked boards instead of relying on luck. The rare long trails last several seconds and feel like a gift.

APRS is the other protocol with internals on the exam, and they are all plumbing. APRS runs on **AX.25**, the amateur packet protocol (E2D07). Its position beacons are **Unnumbered Information frames** — UI frames, connectionless broadcasts with no acknowledgment expected, which is exactly right for a beacon (E2D08). Range comes from relays, and APRS stations relay data by packet **digipeaters** (E2D11). The path you attach to your beacon is a hop budget: **WIDE3-1** designates three digipeater hops requested with one remaining (E2D10). Read "3-1" as "of three, one left": each digipeater that relays the packet decrements the counter, and the packet dies after its third relay. The distractors make the numbers count stations or subcarriers; they count hops.

The mode-to-job map and the APRS plumbing are pool group E2D, minus the two EME questions you already own.

### Digital mechanics: how the modes move their bits

Close the chapter with what the knobs and names actually mean — pool group E2E, the subelement's biggest at thirteen questions.

First, how the bits get on the air at all. Below 30 MHz, data emissions use **FSK** — the transmitter's radio frequency itself shifts between mark and space (E2E01). There are two ways to make that happen, and the exam wants them separated: **direct FSK** modulates the transmitter's VFO, while **AFSK** feeds audio tones into an SSB rig and lets the rig's linear transmit chain turn the two audio tones into two RF frequencies (E2E11).

The WSJT-X family runs on a discipline stricter than any net control: **synchronized computer clocks are the mode** (E2E02). Every station's clock must sit within ±1 second of UTC — the operating system's built-in time sync is often not adequate — because the decoder only listens inside the expected window. Memorize the diagnostic: a waterfall full of signals with zero decodes means check your clock. The cycle times are pinned numbers. An **FT8** transmission cycle is **15 seconds** (E2E06); **FT4** runs 7.5-second cycles, built for contesting. FT4's "4" names its **four-tone continuous-phase frequency shift keying** (E2E03). **FST4** stacks three true clauses into one all-of-the-above answer (E2E04): four-tone Gaussian FSK, variable T/R periods, and seven tone spacings. Q65 averages multiple receive cycles where JT65 does not (E2E07) — you met that in the EME section. **WSPR** is the beacon-only member: it does not support keyboard-to-keyboard operation (E2E05) — just call sign, grid locator, and power in dBm, sent in two-minute sequences and decodable to −31 dB SNR in 2500 Hz per the current WSJT-X guide; if you ever meet a −28 figure, it comes from earlier WSPR documentation. And the bandwidth champion of the listed modes is FT8 at about 50 Hz (E2E10) — narrow enough that dozens of stations share one waterfall at different audio offsets, which is the whole point of the waterfall.

The keyboard modes close the group. **PSK31** uses variable-length character coding — a varicode in which common letters get short codes (E2E09), the same economy Morse exploits with E and Q. **PACTOR** is the HF mode that transfers binary files (E2E08), and **PACTOR IV** has the highest data throughput of the listed modes under clear conditions (E2E13). Notice the trap: E2E10 and E2E13 share an option set — FT8 wins narrowest, PACTOR IV wins fastest, and the pool asks both directions. **ALE** — automatic link establishment — constantly scans a list of frequencies and activates the radio when it receives its designated call sign (E2E12): the HF set answering its own page.

That is E2E's thirteen: FSK mechanics, WSJT-X internals, keyboard modes, and throughput.

### Exam Focus

Subelement E2 — Operating Procedures — is 60 pool questions in five groups (E2A through E2E), and your exam draws five of them, one per group. The nine below are quoted verbatim from the pool, answer key included, with a one-line why for each — the mode-letter anchor and the two convention traps (E2C01, E2C03) are the highest-value confusables.

> **E2A02** Which of the following is characteristic of an inverting linear transponder?
> A. Doppler shift is reduced because the uplink and downlink shifts are in opposite directions
> B. Signal position in the band is reversed
> C. Upper sideband on the uplink becomes lower sideband on the downlink, and vice versa
> D. All these choices are correct
> **Answer: D** — all three hold at once on an inverting transponder — the sidebands swap, band positions reverse, and the opposing Doppler shifts partially cancel.

> **E2A05** What do the letters in a satellite’s mode designator specify?
> A. Power limits for uplink and downlink transmissions
> B. The location of the ground control station
> C. The polarization of uplink and downlink signals
> D. The uplink and downlink frequency ranges
> **Answer: D** — the letters name the uplink and downlink frequency ranges, uplink first — a band pair, not power, polarization, or a control station.

> **E2B10** What aspect of an analog slow-scan television signal encodes the brightness of the picture?
> A. Tone frequency
> B. Tone amplitude
> C. Sync amplitude
> D. Sync frequency
> **Answer: A** — analog SSTV encodes brightness as tone frequency — brighter pixels are higher tones.

> **E2C01** What indicator is required to be used by US-licensed operators when operating a station via remote control and the remote transmitter is located in the US?
> A. / followed by the USPS two-letter abbreviation for the state in which the remote station is located
> B. /R# where # is the district of the remote station
> C. / followed by the ARRL Section of the remote station
> D. No additional indicator is required
> **Answer: D** — no additional indicator is required — §97.119(c) permits indicators but prescribes none for remote control.

> **E2C03** From which of the following bands is amateur radio contesting generally excluded?
> A. 30 meters
> B. 6 meters
> C. 70 centimeters
> D. 33 centimeters
> **Answer: A** — 30 meters is contest-free by long convention — the pool's "generally excluded" is custom, not an FCC rule.

> **E2D01** Which of the following digital modes is designed for meteor scatter communications?
> A. WSPR
> B. MSK144
> C. Hellschreiber
> D. APRS
> **Answer: B** — MSK144 was designed for meteor scatter — a complete 144-bit frame every 72 ms fits inside millisecond pings.

> **E2D10** What does the packet path WIDE3-1 designate?
> A. Three stations are allowed on frequency, one transmitting at a time
> B. Three subcarriers are permitted, subcarrier one is being used
> C. Three digipeater hops are requested with one remaining
> D. Three internet gateway stations may receive one transmission
> **Answer: C** — "3-1" reads "of three hops requested, one remains" — each digipeater decrements the counter.

> **E2E06** What is the length of an FT8 transmission cycle?
> A. It varies with the amount of data
> B. 8 seconds
> C. 15 seconds
> D. 30 seconds
> **Answer: C** — an FT8 transmission cycle is 15 seconds.

> **E2E10** Which of these digital modes has the narrowest bandwidth?
> A. MFSK16
> B. 170 Hz shift, 45-baud RTTY
> C. FT8
> D. PACTOR IV
> **Answer: C** — FT8 is the narrowest of the listed modes at about 50 Hz — dozens of stations share one waterfall.

### Key Takeaways

- A satellite's "mode" is a band pair, uplink letter first: V = 2 m, U = 70 cm, L = 23 cm, S = 13 cm — "mode U/V" means 70 centimeters up, 2 meters down.

- A linear transponder is a bent pipe that relays every mode at once and shares its downlink power — run uplink power so your downlink matches the beacon, and never be the AGC thief.

- EME trades antenna gain and patience for the ultimate weak-signal path: a 2.5-second round trip, band-tagged path loss, and time-synchronous sequences, with the preamp at the antenna.

- Weak-signal VHF contests exchange grid squares, not signal reports — which is why FT8/FT4 contest mode swaps the SNR report for a grid.

- 30 meters is contest-free by convention, not regulation, and remote control of a US transmitter needs no additional ID indicator.

- Anchor the mode-to-job map: MSK144 → meteor scatter, Q65 → EME, APRS → balloon tracking, JT65 → deep noise; WIDE3-1 means "of three hops, one remains."

- FT8 runs 15-second cycles in about 50 Hz, WSPR is beacon-only, and PACTOR IV is the throughput champion of the listed modes.
