## 4. The Workbench: Instruments, Receivers, Interference

Somewhere between General and Extra, an operator stops trusting the S-meter and starts measuring. The meter says the band is noisy tonight; a spectrum-analyzer sweep says the "noise" is a switch-mode supply radiating carriers at regular intervals across two bands, and the same sweep across your own transmitted signal finds a spur your ear never caught. This is the workbench chapter — the instruments that see what ears cannot, the receiver specifications that decide whether a weak signal survives your front end, and the interference-hunting craft that traces a buzz in the speaker to a corroded gutter two houses away. In this chapter you'll learn how oscilloscopes, spectrum analyzers, frequency counters, and network analyzers actually work, what noise figure, dynamic range, and intercept points really mean, how to close a link budget with nothing but dB arithmetic, and how to track down and kill the noise — all 63 questions of pool subelement E4.

One anchor from Book 3 carries over unchanged: 3 dB doubles power, 10 dB is ten times, an S unit is about 6 dB, and the formula behind all of it, $dB = 10 \log_{10}(P_2/P_1)$, stops being background knowledge and becomes first-class exam math here. Everything else in this chapter is new.

### Time versus Frequency: Scopes, Analyzers, and Counters

Two instruments dominate the workbench, and the exam's oldest trap is swapping their axes. An oscilloscope displays amplitude versus time — the waveform as it actually happened, left to right across the screen. A spectrum analyzer displays amplitude versus frequency (E4A02) — the energy in a signal sorted by where it sits on the dial. When the pool asks which instrument displays spurious signals and intermodulation products generated by a transmitter, the answer is the spectrum analyzer (E4A03), because spurs and intermod products are things you see in frequency: little towers of energy where no towers should be. On the bench that makes the analyzer your transmitter's judge — key up into a dummy load, sweep, and every harmonic and spur stands up as a pip above the noise, its height measured against your carrier in dB. The distractor that swaps the axes — amplitude and time — is the classic miss, so say it once and keep it: scope, time; analyzer, frequency.

A digital scope's highest accurately displayed frequency is set by the sampling rate of its analog-to-digital converter (E4A01). Sample too slowly for the signal and the scope doesn't just go fuzzy — it lies. Undersampling produces aliasing: a false, jittery low-frequency version of the waveform appears on screen (E4A06), a phantom that isn't in your circuit at all. The alias is a sampling artifact, not a calibration error, which is why no amount of knob-turning cures it.

Probe craft gets its own questions because it ruins its own measurements. A ×10 probe carries a small compensation trimmer, and you adjust it while watching the scope's calibrator square-wave output until the flat tops are genuinely flat (E4A04) — peaked, overshooting corners mean too much compensation, rounded corners too little. Keep the probe's ground connection as short as possible (E4A09): a six-inch ground lead is an inductor that rings on fast edges and picks up whatever RF is in the room. And when you're hunting ripple in a linear power supply, trigger on line (E4A10) — locking the sweep to the AC line stabilizes the display at exactly the frequency the ripple rides on.

The frequency counter answers one question — how many cycles per second — and answers it superbly until the signal outruns the counter's range. The fix is a prescaler, a divider that reduces the signal frequency to within the counter's operating range (E4A05). The "multiply" distractor inverts the whole idea: you divide the high frequency down, count the divided result, and scale the reading back up.

Then there's the instrument that replaced the SWR bridge on most benches. An antenna analyzer computes SWR and impedance automatically (E4A07) — no fiddling with a bridge's knobs — and the same box measures velocity factor, cable length, and tuned-circuit resonance, which makes E4A11 a legitimate "all these choices are correct." In practice that means one handheld box at the feedpoint tells you the resonant frequency and the R and X of the mismatch, a pulse down the coax finds the open or short at 63 feet instead of "somewhere in the run," and a sweep of a parallel LC marks where it peaks. SWR itself is measurable with a directional wattmeter, a vector network analyzer, or an antenna analyzer — all three (E4A08), another all-of-the-above that earns its key.

That's E4A's eleven: the two domain axes, scope craft, the prescaler, and the analyzer family.

### Accuracy, S Parameters, and Power

Instruments don't just measure — they load, drift, and confuse, and E4B is the group that teaches you to read them skeptically. Start with accuracy. A frequency counter is only as good as its clock: time-base accuracy is the factor that most affects a counter's accuracy (E4B01). An oven-stabilized 10 MHz reference is why a good counter reads 14.030000 MHz while a cheap one argues about the last two digits.

A voltmeter touches the circuit and changes it. The ohms-per-volt rating tells you how hard it touches: multiply the rating by the full-scale voltage of the range you're on and you have the meter's input impedance (E4B02). A 20 kΩ/V meter on its 10 V range presents 20 kΩ/V × 10 V = 200 kΩ to the circuit under test — fine across a power rail, ruinous across a high-impedance oscillator tank. That loading math is why a 10 MΩ digital meter or a ×10 scope probe is the right tool anywhere the circuit is fragile.

Now the vocabulary that runs modern RF measurement: S parameters. They describe a two-port network — a box with an input and an output, port 1 and port 2 — and the subscripts name the ports at which the measurement is made (E4B07). Read the subscripts as "into the first number, from the second": S21 is the forward gain, the signal arriving at port 2 from port 1 (E4B03); S11 is the input return loss or reflection coefficient — the fraction of the incident signal that port 1 sends back — and it is equivalent to VSWR (E4B04), the same mismatch described in network language instead of transmission-line language. S12 is reverse isolation and S22 the output match, but the pool lives on S21 and S11.

A vector network analyzer is the instrument that measures S parameters, and a two-port VNA measures filter frequency response (E4B09) — sweep a filter and watch its passband and skirts drawn on screen — plus input and output impedance and reflection coefficient, another earned "all these choices are correct" (E4B11). Port 1 connected to an antenna reads S11 across the band, which is the SWR curve you used to guess at with a bridge and a knob; port-to-port through a filter reads S21, which is the insertion loss and shape. Before any of it means anything, you calibrate: the three test loads are a short circuit, an open circuit, and a 50 Ω load (E4B05), the SOL ritual that teaches the analyzer where perfect reflection and perfect match live. Skip it and you have not measured the device — you have measured your own test cables.

**FACT:** S parameters describe a two-port network with subscripts naming the ports measured: S21 is forward gain (into port 2 from port 1), and S11 is input return loss/reflection coefficient, equivalent to VSWR.

Power measurement is older and simpler, and the exam's one wattmeter question is pure bookkeeping. A directional wattmeter between transmitter and load reads forward power one way and reflected power the other; what the load actually absorbs is the difference.

> **Worked example:** Your directional wattmeter reads 100 W forward and 25 W reflected. The load absorbs 100 − 25 = 75 W (E4B06) — the 125 W distractor adds what physics subtracts. The same pair of readings gives the reflection coefficient: $|\Gamma| = \sqrt{25/100} = 0.5$, from which SWR = (1 + 0.5)/(1 − 0.5) = 3:1 and the return loss is −20·log₁₀(0.5) ≈ 6.0 dB. One meter, three answers — and that chain, |Γ| to SWR to return loss, is exactly why S11 is "equivalent to VSWR."

Two more measurement methods close the group. The Q of a series-tuned circuit comes from the bandwidth of its frequency response (E4B08): sweep the circuit, measure the bandwidth between the half-power points, and Q = f/BW. You'll meet the same relation turned around in chapter 5, where BW = f₀/Q sizes a resonator's selectivity — the VNA version measures Q instead of computing it.

And the transmitter intermodulation test: modulate the transmitter with two non-harmonically related audio-frequency signals and observe the RF output on a spectrum analyzer (E4B10). The load-bearing word is audio. The distractor that injects RF tones describes a receiver test — a transmitter IMD test exercises the whole transmit chain from mic jack to antenna, and the analyzer shows you the intermod products standing beside your two-tone signal where only two tones should be.

That's E4B's eleven: time bases and meter loading, S parameters and the VNA, one wattmeter subtraction, and the two-tone test.

### The Receiver Numbers: Noise, Selectivity, Overload

Open a receiver's spec sheet and three families of numbers stare back: how weak a signal it can hear, how loud a signal it can survive, and how well it separates what you want from what you don't. E4C makes those numbers mean something.

Noise first, with three terms the exam keeps distinct. Noise figure is the ratio in dB of the noise generated by the receiver to the theoretical minimum noise (E4C04) — how much noisier your receiver is than a perfect one. The theoretical minimum is not a spec somebody chose; it is physics: −174 dBm in a 1 Hz bandwidth at room temperature (E4C05), the thermal noise floor every receiver on Earth starts from. And MDS — minimum discernible signal (E4C07) — is the practical bottom line: the weakest signal the receiver can dig out of its own noise. Because noise power scales with bandwidth, widening the filter raises the floor.

> **Worked example:** Widen the receiver filter from 50 Hz to 1,000 Hz and the noise floor rises by $10 \log_{10}(1000/50)$ = 13 dB (E4C06). Check it against the floor: a perfect receiver hears −174 dBm in 1 Hz, so in 50 Hz it hears about −174 + 17 = −157 dBm and in 1,000 Hz about −174 + 30 = −144 dBm — the same 13 dB. Every narrowing of the filter buys back signal-to-noise you can hear.

{{fig:ch04-noise-cascade}}

Why does the first stage dominate a receiver's noise? Because noise added early is amplified by everything after it, while a later stage's noise, referred back to the input, is divided by all the gain in front of it. The low-noise budget belongs in the preamp — which is why the weak-signal VHF operator buys the preamp with the lowest noise figure and mounts it at the antenna, ahead of the feedline loss that would otherwise add directly to the system noise. That picture also explains a counterintuitive exam answer: on the lower HF bands, input attenuation reduces overload with little impact on the received signal, because atmospheric noise is generally greater than internally generated noise even after the attenuation (E4C11). At 7 MHz the band itself is louder than your receiver, so a 10 dB pad quiets the atmosphere and the signal together while your receiver's own hiss stays buried below both. On 2 meters the same trick costs real sensitivity, because there the receiver's noise is the limitation.

{{fig:ch04-receiver-performance}}

Overload next, and the exam's highest-value confusable pair. Reciprocal mixing is local-oscillator phase noise mixing with a strong adjacent signal and dumping noise onto the desired one (E4C13): one strong signal, plus an imperfect LO. Every oscillator's output wears a noisy skirt, a spread of close-in frequencies of its own; mix that skirt with a powerhouse a few kilohertz away and some of it lands right on your frequency. The distractor — two out-of-band signals mixing to make an in-band product — defines intermodulation, not reciprocal mixing: IMD needs nonlinearity and two signals, reciprocal mixing needs phase noise and one. The same mechanism is why excessive phase noise in an SDR's master clock is dangerous: it combines with strong signals on nearby frequencies to generate interference (E4C01). And SDR overload has a hard digital edge — the receiver overloads when input signal peaks exceed the reference voltage of the analog-to-digital converter (E4C08). Past that voltage the converter clips, and everything downstream is garbage.

Selectivity is architecture, and the exam walks the whole chain. A high first IF makes it easier for front-end circuitry to eliminate image responses (E4C09) — the image lives twice the IF away from your signal, so a higher IF pushes it out where cheap filters kill it. A front-end filter or preselector removes strong out-of-band signals before they reach the stages where they could intermod (E4C02). A choice of bandwidths lets you match the filter to the modulation, maximizing signal-to-noise ratio (E4C10) — the 13 dB worked example above is the reason. A narrow roofing filter sits early in the IF and improves blocking dynamic range by attenuating strong signals near the receive frequency (E4C12), before they can drive later stages into compression. And IF Shift slides the passband sideways to reduce interference from stations on adjacent frequencies (E4C14) — the roofing filter narrows early, IF Shift moves late. One FM peculiarity finishes the group: the capture effect, the suppression in an FM receiver of one signal by a stronger one on the same frequency (E4C03). Two co-channel FM stations don't mix in your speaker; the stronger simply wins. You have heard it on every repeater: the mobile fluttering at the fringe doesn't fade in under the base station — it vanishes, and only the stronger signal exists.

**FACT:** Receiver noise scales with bandwidth: widening from 50 Hz to 1,000 Hz raises the noise floor by 10·log₁₀(1000/50) = 13 dB.

That's E4C's fourteen: the noise vocabulary, the overload mechanisms, the selectivity architecture, and capture.

### Dynamic Range, Intermodulation, and the Link Budget

E4D opens with two different dynamic ranges the exam deliberately blurs. Blocking dynamic range is the difference in dB between the noise floor and the level of an incoming signal that causes 1 dB of gain compression (E4D01) — one signal, growing until the receiver's gain sags. Its distractors define the other dynamic range, the third-order IMD version, which needs two signals and a nonlinear stage. Keep the owners straight: blocking is compression, IMD is third-order products. When dynamic range runs short, the symptoms are spurious signals from cross-modulation and desensitization caused by strong adjacent signals (E4D02). Desensitization is the quieter killer — a reduction in receiver sensitivity caused by a strong signal near the receive frequency (E4D06), one that never has to appear in your passband to hurt you — and the taming move is to insert attenuation before the first RF stage (E4D07), the same E4C11 trick from the last section.

Intermodulation itself is born in nonlinear circuits or devices (E4D08) — any stage whose output is not a faithful scaled copy of its input manufactures new frequencies from old. Two repeaters in close proximity intermod when their output signals mix in the final amplifier of one or both transmitters (E4D03): energy from repeater A's antenna couples into repeater B's transmitter, finds the nonlinear final, and mixes there. The cure respects the direction of the problem — the trouble is energy coming back into the transmitter — so a properly terminated circulator at the output of the repeater's transmitter (E4D04) routes the returning energy into a dummy load instead of into the final amplifier. On the receive side, the preselector increases rejection of signals outside the band being received (E4D09), starving the nonlinear stages of the raw material for intermod.

Why does the pool single out odd orders? Because of where the products land. Two signals inside the band being received produce odd-order intermodulation products that are also likely to be in-band (E4D11): the third-order product 2f1 − f2 of two close neighbors sits right beside them, inside your roofing filter, untouchable. Even-order products land far away and filters eat them. The third-order intercept point compresses all this into one number: an IP3 of 40 dBm means a pair of 40 dBm input signals would theoretically generate a third-order product equal in level to the inputs (E4D10). That point is an extrapolation — the place where two trend lines drawn from sane measurements meet — never a working level, because the receiver would be destroyed long before 40 dBm reached its input.

Then comes the math the exam drills three ways, and the good news is that it is bookkeeping, not calculus. A link budget works in dBm — decibels relative to 1 milliwatt, so 0 dBm is 1 mW and 30 dBm is 1 W — and in dB land every gain adds and every loss subtracts. That is the whole trick: decibels turn the multiplication and division of ratios into addition and subtraction, so a 10 W transmitter, two antennas, a hundred dB of path loss, and a noise floor combine in one line of arithmetic you can do in your head at the operating table.

> **Worked example:** A receiver MDS of −100 dBm — what power is that? $P = 10^{((-100-30)/10)}$ = 10⁻¹³ W = 0.1 picowatt (E4D14). The dBm-to-watts direction runs the same formula every time: subtract 30, divide by 10, raise 10 to the result.

> **Worked example:** Transmit power 10 W = +40 dBm, transmit antenna gain 6 dBi, receive antenna gain 3 dBi, path loss 100 dB. Received level: 40 + 6 + 3 − 100 = −51 dBm (E4D13). The three wrong answers are −54, −57, and −60 dBm — the sums you get by dropping a gain from the chain.

> **Worked example:** Now the margin. Transmit 40 dBm, system antenna gain 10 dBi, cable loss 3 dB, path loss 136 dB: 40 + 10 − 3 − 136 = −89 dBm arrives at the receiver. The MDS is −103 dBm, so the signal lands 14 dB above the noise floor; hold back the required 6 dB signal-to-noise ratio and the link margin is 14 − 6 = +8 dB (E4D12). The distractors punish two slips: +14 dB forgets the SNR reserve, and the negative answers flip a sign somewhere.

One numbering note before you go hunting: E4D05 was withdrawn by the 4th errata (2026-02-04), and the group was not renumbered — E4D runs D01 through D04, then D06 through D14. The gap is intentional, not a missing page.

{{fig:ch04-link-budget}}

**FACT:** Link budgets are pure dB bookkeeping — received power = transmit power + antenna gains − losses − path loss: 40 dBm + 6 dB + 3 dB − 100 dB = −51 dBm received (each distractor omits exactly one gain).

**FACT:** Link margin = received power − MDS − required SNR: with 40 dBm + 10 dB − 3 dB − 136 dB = −89 dBm received, −89 − (−103) = 14 dB over MDS, and 14 − 6 = +8 dB of margin after the SNR reserve.

**FACT:** −100 dBm is 10⁻¹³ W = 0.1 picowatt, from P(W) = 10^((dBm−30)/10).

That's E4D's thirteen: two dynamic ranges, the intermod mechanics, and three flavors of dB bookkeeping.

### Noise Tools, RFI Hunting, and Grounding

The last group is the craft section: matching the tool to the noise, recognizing interference by its signature, and grounding so the protector you installed actually protects. Start with the noise-tool map, because the exam tests the boundaries. A noise blanker attacks impulse noise (E4E03) — ignition pops, power-line crackle, anything arriving as short, violent spikes the blanker can gate out between their arrivals. Digital noise reduction attacks a broader list: broadband white noise, ignition noise, and power-line noise — all three (E4E02). The blanker mutes the spikes; the DSP subtracts the hiss. Both carry side effects the pool knows about. A noise blanker can distort strong signals so they appear to cause spurious emissions (E4E09) — the blanker's gating chops the strong signal and sprays energy, so the "dirty transmitter across town" may be your own blanker. And an automatic notch filter hunting a steady carrier can notch out your own CW signal right along with the interfering one (E4E01) — to the ANF, your CW is exactly the steady tone it was built to kill.

RFI hunting is pattern recognition, and the exam gives each source a signature. Computer network equipment radiates unstable modulated or unmodulated signals at specific frequencies (E4E06) — wobbly, bursty carriers that shift as the traffic changes. Switch-mode power supplies radiate a series of carriers at regular intervals across a wide frequency range (E4E12) — evenly spaced fence posts marching across the panadapter, spaced by the supply's switching frequency, courtesy of the phone charger behind the couch or a neighbor's LED grow light. Intermittent loud roaring or buzzing on the AC line points at arcing thermostats, doorbell transformers, and flickering displays — all three (E4E10): something is arcing, and an arc is a broadband noise factory. And when local AM broadcast signals combine into spurious signals, suspect the rusty-bolt effect: nearby corroded metal connections mixing and reradiating the broadcast signals (E4E11). A corroded gutter joint is a diode, and a diode is a mixer.

The cures are as specific as the signatures. Conducted noise from a vehicle's battery-charging system yields to ferrite chokes on the charging-system leads (E4E04). RFI from a line-driven AC motor yields to a brute-force AC-line filter in series with the motor's power leads (E4E05) — series, in the leads, not a capacitor across the line. And before any cure comes the hunt: when a new noise moves into your shack, the first instrument is your own breaker panel. Kill the house circuits one at a time while you watch the S-meter, and when the noise dies with a breaker, walk that circuit with a portable receiver and a small loop until the loudest spot confesses. Most RFI is found with feet and patience, then fixed with ferrite.

Underneath half of these stories runs one mechanism: common-mode current, the current that flows equally on all conductors of a cable (E4E08), same direction, same instant, the whole cable acting as one fat wire. It is what makes shielded cables radiate or receive interference (E4E07) — the shield that was supposed to contain the signal becomes the antenna. The differential signal you care about flows out on one conductor and back on the other; the common-mode rides both at once. Ferrite chokes kill the common-mode current while leaving the differential signal untouched, which is why the answer to so much RFI is "wind it through a ferrite core."

Grounding closes the chapter, and the exam's two questions are one system. The station AC surge protector is installed on the single point ground panel (E4E13) — the one panel where every ground in the station meets. The panel's purpose is to ensure all lightning protectors activate at the same time (E4E14): when a surge arrives, every protector must fire together, because if one chassis sits at a different potential from the next during the event, thousands of volts appear between your radio and your computer — and the difference is what kills equipment. That is why the coax arrestors, the AC protector, the rotator control, and the station ground bus all bond to the same panel with short, wide strap: the geometry is the protection. Single point, simultaneous firing, no chassis-to-chassis potential.

That's E4E's fourteen: the noise-tool boundaries, the RFI signatures and cures, common-mode current, and the ground panel.

### Exam Focus

Subelement E4 — Amateur Practices — is 63 pool questions in five groups (E4A through E4E), and your 50-question exam draws 5 of them, one per group. The nine below are quoted verbatim from the pool: the four math anchors (E4B06, E4C06, E4D12, E4D13), the high-value confusables (the swapped axes, the S-parameter numbering, reciprocal mixing versus IMD), and the noise-signature and grounding boundaries.

> **E4A02** Which of the following parameters does a spectrum analyzer display on the vertical and horizontal axes?
> A. Signal amplitude and time
> B. Signal amplitude and frequency
> C. SWR and frequency
> D. SWR and time
> **Answer: B** — Amplitude versus frequency is the analyzer; amplitude versus time is the oscilloscope, and that swap is the classic miss.

> **E4B04** Which S parameter represents input port return loss or reflection coefficient (equivalent to VSWR)?
> A. S11
> B. S12
> C. S21
> D. S22
> **Answer: A** — S11 is the signal port 1 sends back — return loss or reflection coefficient, which is VSWR in network language.

> **E4B06** How much power is being absorbed by the load when a directional power meter connected between a transmitter and a terminating load reads 100 watts forward power and 25 watts reflected power?
> A. 100 watts
> B. 125 watts
> C. 112.5 watts
> D. 75 watts
> **Answer: D** — The load absorbs forward minus reflected: 100 − 25 = 75 W, and the 125 W choice adds what you should subtract.

> **E4C06** How much does increasing a receiver’s bandwidth from 50 Hz to 1,000 Hz increase the receiver’s noise floor?
> A. 3 dB
> B. 5 dB
> C. 10 dB
> D. 13 dB
> **Answer: D** — Noise power scales with bandwidth: 10·log₁₀(1000/50) = 13 dB.

> **E4C13** What is reciprocal mixing?
> A. Two out-of-band signals mixing to generate an in-band spurious signal
> B. In-phase signals cancelling in a mixer resulting in loss of receiver sensitivity
> C. Two digital signals combining from alternate time slots
> D. Local oscillator phase noise mixing with adjacent strong signals to create interference to desired signals
> **Answer: D** — Reciprocal mixing is one strong adjacent signal plus LO phase noise; the two-signal choice defines intermodulation instead.

> **E4D12** What is the link margin in a system with a transmit power level of 10 W (+40 dBm), a system antenna gain of 10 dBi, a cable loss of 3 dB, a path loss of 136 dB, a receiver minimum discernable signal of -103 dBm, and a required signal-to-noise ratio of 6 dB?
> A. -8dB
> B. -14dB
> C. +8dB
> D. +14dB
> **Answer: C** — 40 + 10 − 3 − 136 = −89 dBm received, 14 dB over the −103 dBm MDS, and 14 − 6 = +8 dB after the SNR reserve.

> **E4D13** What is the received signal level with a transmit power of 10 W (+40 dBm), a transmit antenna gain of 6 dBi, a receive antenna gain of 3 dBi, and a path loss of 100 dB?
> A. -51 dBm
> B. -54 dBm
> C. -57 dBm
> D. -60 dBm
> **Answer: A** — 40 + 6 + 3 − 100 = −51 dBm; each wrong answer drops a gain from the chain.

> **E4E12** What causes interference received as a series of carriers at regular intervals across a wide frequency range?
> A. Switch-mode power supplies
> B. Radar transmitters
> C. Wireless security camera transmitters
> D. Electric fences
> **Answer: A** — A switch-mode supply's switching frequency stamps evenly spaced carriers across a wide range — the panadapter fence posts.

> **E4E14** What is the purpose of a single point ground panel?
> A. Remove AC power in case of a short-circuit
> B. Prevent common-mode transients in multi-wire systems
> C. Eliminate air gaps between protected and non-protected circuits
> D. Ensure all lightning protectors activate at the same time
> **Answer: D** — The single point ground panel makes every lightning protector fire at once, so no lethal chassis-to-chassis potential can develop.

### Key Takeaways

- An oscilloscope shows amplitude versus time and a spectrum analyzer amplitude versus frequency — the scope for the waveform, the analyzer for transmitter spurs and intermod products.

- S21 is forward gain ("into port 2 from port 1") and S11 is input return loss or reflection coefficient, equivalent to VSWR; a two-port VNA calibrates with short, open, and 50 Ω loads.

- Absorbed power is forward minus reflected — 100 W forward and 25 W reflected delivers 75 W, and the same pair gives |Γ| = 0.5, a 3:1 SWR, and about 6 dB of return loss.

- The reference noise floor is −174 dBm in a 1 Hz bandwidth at room temperature, and receiver noise scales with bandwidth — widening from 50 Hz to 1,000 Hz costs 13 dB.

- Reciprocal mixing is one strong signal plus LO phase noise, intermodulation is two signals plus nonlinearity, blocking dynamic range is compression — and odd-order products like 2f1 − f2 land back in-band.

- Link budgets are pure dB bookkeeping: received = transmit + gains − losses − path loss, and margin = received − MDS − required SNR (40 + 10 − 3 − 136 = −89 dBm, 14 dB over MDS, +8 dB after the reserve); −100 dBm is 0.1 pW.

- Match the noise tool to the noise: blanker for impulses, DSP noise reduction for broadband, automatic notch for steady tones — your own CW included; switch-mode supplies radiate evenly spaced carriers across a wide range.

- Common-mode current flows equally on all conductors and makes shielded cables radiate; surge protectors mount on the single point ground panel so every lightning protector fires at the same time.
