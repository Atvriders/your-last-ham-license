## 9. Antennas and Transmission Lines at Extra Depth

Eight of the fifty questions on your exam come from this chapter — subelement E9, ninety-three questions in eight groups, tied with practical circuits as the biggest block on the test. This is the last frontier of the exam, and it is the stretch between the transmitter and the sky: the feed line that transforms whatever impedance it is given, the chart that maps every impedance a load can present, and the antenna that aims whatever power you have left. In this chapter you'll learn the gain bookkeeping behind ERP and EIRP, how to read the pool's two antenna patterns and its Smith chart, the phased-array and name-that-antenna drills, what loading does to a short antenna, the transmission-line transformation table, and the receiving antennas that hear what your beam cannot. Book 3 gave you the fundamentals — 468/f for the dipole, SWR as the match meter, gain quoted in dBd and dBi — and everything here builds directly on that base.

### Gain, ERP, EIRP, and the Isotropic Reference

Every gain number needs a reference, and the pool's primary one is the isotropic radiator: a hypothetical, lossless antenna that radiates equally in all directions (E9A01). It cannot exist — that is the point. It is the perfect sphere against which real antennas are measured, and gain quoted against it carries the unit dBi. Book 3's reference, the half-wave dipole, sits 2.15 dB above the sphere, so dBd = dBi − 2.15: an antenna with 6 dB of gain over isotropic has 3.85 dB of gain over a dipole (E9A12). dBi is always the bigger number, because the isotropic sphere is always the easier reference to beat.

The pairing law rides on that conversion: ERP pairs with dBd, EIRP with dBi. ERP — effective radiated power — is the term describing total radiated power that takes into account all gains and losses (E9A03): the transmitter power output, minus the feed line loss, minus every device sitting in the line, plus the antenna gain. The arithmetic is a single chain of signed decibels, and the sign discipline is the whole game — losses subtract, gains add, and only then does the net dB convert back to a power ratio. The anchors from Book 3 still steer: +3 dB is about double, +10 dB is exactly ten times, so a net of +2.8 dB is a multiplier near 1.9.

> **Worked example:** The pool's repeater station puts 150 W into a feed line costing 2 dB and a duplexer costing 2.2 dB, then an antenna with 7 dBd gain. The net is −2 − 2.2 + 7 = +2.8 dB, so $ERP = TPO \times 10^{(gains - losses)/10}$ gives 150 × 10^0.28 = 150 × 1.905 ≈ 286 W (E9A02). The bigger rig runs 200 W through losses of 4, 3.2, and 0.8 dB with a 10 dBd antenna: the net is +2 dB, so 200 × 1.585 ≈ 317 W (E9A06). Asked as EIRP, the gain arrives in dBi and the name changes but never the method: 200 W with losses of 2, 2.8, and 1.2 dB plus 7 dBi nets +1 dB, so 200 × 1.259 ≈ 252 W (E9A07). The traps are sign errors — 469 W is what you get for adding the losses in E9A02, 78.7 W is a deeper sign tangle, and 2,000 W in E9A06 is the price of treating 10 dB as a straight times-ten.

Three quieter questions round out the group's picture of what surrounds the antenna. Height above ground affects an antenna's feed-point impedance — line length, tuner settings, and power level do not (E9A04). Ground gain is an increase in signal strength from ground reflections in the environment of the antenna (E9A05), free decibels courtesy of the earth under a horizontal wire. And the first Fresnel zone — the ellipsoid around the path that should stay clear — shrinks as frequency rises, so of the bands the pool lists, 5.8 GHz has the smallest (E9A08).

Efficiency closes the group. Antenna efficiency is radiation resistance divided by total resistance (E9A09) — the share of the feed-point resistance that actually radiates, over everything that dissipates, including the loss resistance of the ground and the conductors. That is why installing a ground radial system improves the efficiency of a ground-mounted quarter-wave vertical (E9A10): the radials shrink the ground's share of the total resistance. Note that E9A10 dangles "all these choices are correct" as a decoy — the keyed answer is the radial system specifically, so read the choices rather than the pattern. And it is soil conductivity that determines ground losses for a ground-mounted vertical on HF (E9A11), which is why the same vertical sings over salt marsh and sulks over rock.

That is E9A — twelve questions, E9A01 through E9A12.

**FACT:** ERP is the total radiated-power figure that accounts for all gains and losses, computed ERP = TPO × 10^((gains − losses)/10): 150 W with −2 dB feed line, −2.2 dB duplexer, +7 dBd antenna → net +2.8 dB → 150 × 1.905 ≈ 286 W; 200 W with −4 −3.2 −0.8 dB, +10 dBd → net +2 dB → ≈317 W.

### Reading the Antenna Patterns: Figures E9-1 and E9-2

Before the patterns, one conservation law: directivity redirects power, it does not create it. A lossless antenna with gain and an isotropic radiator driven by the same power radiate exactly the same total power (E9B07) — the gain antenna simply puts more of it where you want it and less everywhere else. Gain is a reshaping, never a multiplier. And the pattern you read is the far field's: the region where the shape of the radiation pattern no longer varies with distance (E9B08). Close in, the fields are still settling into their final form; far out, the shape is fixed and only the amplitude falls with range.

A polar pattern reads like a compass with a ruler: the angle around the plot is the bearing, and the distance from the center is the relative response in decibels. Every plot is normalized to its own strongest bearing, so the main lobe always touches the outer 0 dB ring and every other bearing reads straight off the rings as a number below the peak — which is exactly why ratios like front-to-back come out as simple subtractions on the drawing.

Now read the pool's azimuth pattern. The redrawn Figure E9-1 is a free-space polar plot: 0° at the right, spokes every 15°, and concentric rings labeled −3, −6, −12, and −24 dB stacked inside the outer circle, which is the 0 dB reference.

Three questions read three numbers off this one trace, and each number has its own misread planted beside it. Beamwidth first: the main lobe points at 0° and touches the outer ring, and it crosses the −3 dB ring at about +25° and −25° — so the 3 dB beamwidth is twice 25°, which is 50° (E9B01). The −3 dB points are the half-power points, the standard width of any lobe; the distractors are the other widths you could have read instead, 75° running roughly null-to-null and 25° being only the one-sided half-width. Front-to-back next: the rear lobe at 180° sits at about −18 dB against the 0 dB main lobe, so the front-to-back ratio is 18 dB (E9B02). Front-to-side last: the trace at ±90° sits at about −14 dB, giving 14 dB (E9B03) — and the trap is −12 dB, which is the level of the sidelobes near ±55°, not the level at 90°. Read the bearing the question names, never the nearest bump. Between the lobes, notice the nulls: the trace dives below the −24 dB ring near ±70–80° and again flanking the rear — the deep folds that separate one lobe from the next, and the reason a plot like this is read lobe by lobe rather than as a single blob.

The second pattern changes the viewing angle. The redrawn Figure E9-2 is a semicircle above a flat baseline: 0° at the right end of the baseline, 90° at the zenith, 180° at the left, with arcs labeled −10, −20, −30, and −40 dB inside the 0 dB outer semicircle. The baseline is the ground itself, and the semicircle-above-ground format is the first answer — this is an elevation pattern, not an azimuth one (E9B05).

This trace hugs the horizon. The largest lobe peaks at 7.5° elevation — halfway to the first subdivision spoke — and reaches the outer arc, while successively smaller lobes stack above it near 22°, 38°, and 52°; the 45, 75, and 25 degree distractors read the wrong lobes (E9B06). The small rear lobes toward 150–180° are down about 28 dB, so the front-to-back ratio here is 28 dB (E9B04). Patterns of this shape come from modeling, and the pool tests the method by name. Antenna modeling commonly uses the Method of Moments: the wire is modeled as a series of segments, each carrying a uniform current (E9B09), and the segment currents are solved so the boundary conditions hold along the whole conductor. Resolution matters — drop below about ten segments per half-wavelength and the computed feed-point impedance can come out wrong (E9B10, E9B11), the classic garbage-in of the modeling world.

That is E9B — eleven questions, E9B01 through E9B11, six of them on the two patterns.

### Wire Antennas and Phased Arrays

Two quarter-wave verticals, a chosen spacing, and a chosen drive phase: the pool's whole phased-array table is three combinations. Spaced a half wavelength apart and fed 180 degrees out of phase, the pair makes a figure-eight oriented along the axis of the array — an end-fire pattern, firing through the line of the elements (E9C01). Keep the half-wave spacing but feed the two in phase, and the pattern flips ninety degrees: a figure-eight broadside to the axis of the array (E9C03). Move the elements to quarter-wave spacing and feed them 90 degrees out of phase, and the lobes merge asymmetrically into a cardioid (E9C02). The mnemonic is the pairing: in phase broadside, out of phase end-fire — at half-wave spacing, the phase and the pattern orientation move together.

{{fig:ch09-phased-array}}

The figure draws the three combinations from above, one panel each: the two elements as dots on the array axis, the pattern they make around the pair. In phase at half-wave spacing, the lobes stand broadside; 180 degrees out of phase at the same spacing, the lobes lie along the axis; at quarter-wave spacing with 90 degrees of phase, the pattern is the one-sided cardioid. When a stem gives you spacing and phase, picture the panel before you picture the name.

Long wires behave by their own rule. As an unterminated long wire is made longer, additional lobes form, and the strongest lobes align increasingly with the wire's axis (E9C04) — the pattern stops being broadside and starts chasing the wire. Add a terminating resistor at the far end and the traveling wave no longer reflects back toward the feed: a rhombic or long-wire antenna's pattern becomes unidirectional (E9C06), because the resistor absorbs the power that would otherwise light up the reverse lobe.

Then the name-that-antenna drill, where three questions raid each other's answer text and only precision survives. An OCFD — an off-center-fed dipole — is fed off-center to create a similar feed-point impedance on multiple bands (E9C05). A folded dipole is a half-wave dipole with an additional parallel wire connecting its ends (E9C07), and the folded construction steps the feed impedance up to about 300 Ω (E9C08) — the old twinlead number. A G5RV is a center-fed wire fed through a specific length of open-wire line to a balun and coax (E9C09). A Zepp is an end-fed half-wavelength antenna (E9C10), and an extended double Zepp is a center-fed dipole 1.25 wavelengths long (E9C12). Zepp end-fed a half wave, extended double Zepp center-fed 1.25λ, G5RV open-wire plus balun — pin each description to its name, because the choices quote one another nearly verbatim.

Terrain writes the last three answers. Mounting an antenna over seawater increases its low-angle radiation compared with mounting over soil (E9C11) — salt water's conductivity is nearly a mirror at HF, and the DX angles love it. Raising a horizontally polarized antenna lowers the takeoff angle of its lowest lobe (E9C13): height is the DX lever, and it is measured in wavelengths, not feet. And a long downhill slope lowers the main-lobe takeoff angle in the downhill direction (E9C14), which is why a hilltop station favors the side of the hill that faces its target.

That is E9C — fourteen questions, E9C01 through E9C14, the largest group in the subelement.

### Yagis, Dishes, and Loading

The parabolic dish owns the cleanest scaling law in the group: double the operating frequency and the ideal reflector's gain increases by 6 dB (E9D01). The dish is the same physical size, but at the doubled frequency it is twice as many wavelengths across — twice the diameter in wavelengths is four times the aperture area, and four times the power is 6 dB. The same law runs in reverse down the band, which is why a dish that is a giant on 10 GHz is a toy on 70 centimeters.

Circular polarization from ordinary linear Yagis is a recipe question, and the recipe has four clauses: arrange two Yagis on the same axis, perpendicular to each other, with the driven elements at the same point on the boom, and feed them 90 degrees out of phase (E9D02). The distractors offer parallel-plane mounting and a 180-degree feed — same axis, perpendicular, same boom point, 90 degrees is the keyed set, all four clauses together. The payoff is a wave whose polarization rotates as it travels, the standard answer for satellite work where the far end's orientation is never still.

Yagi anatomy earns three more. The driven element is approximately a half wavelength long (E9D05) — the distractors, 234 divided by the frequency and 1005 divided by the frequency, are wire-length formulas in feet, printed to catch anyone matching numbers instead of answering the electrical fraction the stem asks for. With only two elements, higher gain comes from a reflector rather than a director (E9D11). And the parasitic elements steer by detuning: they are made longer or shorter than resonance to control their phase shift (E9D12), each one re-radiating with a phase that pulls the pattern its way.

Loading a short antenna is a set of trades the pool tests from four directions. A loading coil resonates a short antenna by canceling its capacitive reactance (E9D09) — a short whip is a capacitor in disguise, and the coil supplies the equal and opposite inductance. For the best efficiency, place that coil near the center of the radiator (E9D03): base loading works, but it leaves only the stub below the coil carrying high current, while moving the coil up lets more of the radiator share the current that does the radiating. Wind the coil for a high reactance-to-resistance ratio (E9D04), because every ohm of coil resistance is power that never radiates. Top loading improves radiation efficiency (E9D07), raising the current along the whole whip instead of canceling reactance in one lump. The price is bandwidth: loading raises the antenna's Q, and the SWR bandwidth decreases (E9D06, E9D08 — every "increases" choice in those two stems is the trap). And below resonance, a base-fed whip's radiation resistance decreases (E9D10), which is the efficiency loss the loading was fighting in the first place.

That is E9D — twelve questions, E9D01 through E9D12.

### Matching Systems: Gamma, Beta, Stubs, and the Q-Section

Three matching systems carry near-identical descriptions across five questions, so learn each by its one unmistakable clause. The gamma match connects the coax shield to the center of the element and the center conductor to a point a fraction of a wavelength to one side (E9E02) — a series tap, off-center. Its series capacitor exists to cancel the inductive reactance of the gamma section itself (E9E04). And because the element's center stays at ground through the shield connection, the gamma is also the match used to shunt-feed a grounded tower at its base (E9E09): the tower itself is the radiator, the gamma rod runs alongside it like one half of a folded dipole, and the base keeps its solid ground connection the whole time. The beta or hairpin match works the opposite side of resonance: it needs a driven element electrically shorter than a half wavelength, so the feed point is capacitive and the hairpin's shunt inductance cancels it (E9E05), and it requires the driven element to be insulated from the boom (E9E01). The stub match carries the simplest description of the three: a short length of transmission line connected in parallel with the feed line at or near the feed point (E9E03).

The Q-section is the quarter-wave transformer, and its one formula is a geometric mean.

> **Worked example:** To match a 100 Ω feed point to a 50 Ω line, the quarter-wave section's impedance must be $Z_0 = \sqrt{Z_{line} \times Z_{load}}$ = √(50 × 100) = 70.7 Ω — and among the printed choices, the suitable line is 75 Ω (E9E06). The 62 Ω choice is the arithmetic-mean bait: the transformer's ratio runs through the square of the impedance, so the mean is geometric, never arithmetic.

> **The math, if you want it:** Why the geometric mean? A quarter-wave line transforms its load through the square of its own impedance: Z_in = Z₀² ÷ Z_load. Setting Z_in to the 50 Ω the transmitter wants gives Z₀² = 50 × 100, and the square root is the geometric mean. The same inversion formula explains the λ/4 row of the transformation table you will meet next section: a shorted load (Z = 0) makes the input look infinite, and an open load (Z = ∞) makes it look like a short.

The rest of the cast in one pass. The reflection coefficient — chapter 5's Γ — describes the interaction of a load and a transmission line (E9E07). A Wilkinson divider is used to divide power equally between two 50 Ω loads while maintaining a 50 Ω input impedance (E9E08), the clean way to feed a stacked pair of Yagis. And multiple driven elements connected through phasing lines are used to control an antenna's radiation pattern (E9E11) — the feed hardware behind the phased table two sections back.

One numbering note: this group skips E9E10. That question was withdrawn by the pool's first errata on 2024-01-31, and the group was never renumbered, so the gap in the sequence is intentional — there is simply no E9E10 in this pool.

That is E9E — ten questions, E9E01 through E9E09 plus E9E11.

**FACT:** A quarter-wave Q-section's impedance is the geometric mean Z₀ = √(Z_line × Z_load) — √(50 × 100) = 70.7 Ω, so 75 Ω line is the suitable choice.

### Transmission Lines: Velocity Factor and the Transformation Table

A wave in a transmission line travels slower than light in a vacuum, and the ratio is the velocity factor: the wave velocity in the line divided by the velocity of light in a vacuum (E9F01). VF divides the speed of light — the distractor multiplies, which would make the wave faster than light. The dielectric insulating material between the conductors has the biggest effect on it (E9F02), and the consequence is that a line's electrical length exceeds its physical length (E9F03): a quarter wavelength of foam coax is shorter on the spool than a quarter wavelength in air, because the wave needs less physical distance to accumulate the same electrical phase.

> **Worked example:** A half-wavelength of air-insulated parallel line at 14.10 MHz: λ = 300/14.10 = 21.28 m, and with air's velocity factor of essentially 1.0 the half-wave section is $L \approx 150 \times VF / f$ = 150 × 1.0 / 14.10 ≈ 10.6 m (E9F06). The 7.0, 8.5, and 13.3 meter choices are wrong-velocity-factor and third-wave slips. Keep the general form — 150 × VF / f with f in MHz and the answer in meters — and the whole line-length family falls at once.

Line types sort by loss and dielectric. Open-wire parallel line has lower loss than plastic-dielectric coax (E9F07) — air is still the best dielectric you can buy. Foam dielectric coax, measured against solid with all other parameters the same, has a lower safe operating voltage and lower loss per unit length and a higher velocity factor — all three clauses independently true, so "all these choices are correct" is honestly keyed there (E9F08). And microstrip is precision printed circuit conductors above a ground plane, providing constant-impedance interconnects at microwave frequencies (E9F05).

Now the group's core — the transformation table, with six questions riding one idea. A transmission line transforms whatever terminates it, and the transform depends on the line's electrical length. A half-wave line repeats its termination: short the far end and the input shows a very low impedance (E9F04) — whatever hangs on the far end arrives at the near end unchanged, which is why an exact electrical half-wave of line lets your analyzer at the shack read the antenna's true feed impedance. A quarter-wave line inverts its termination: shorted, the input shows a very high impedance (E9F09); open, a very low impedance (E9F12). An eighth-wave line converts the termination to reactance: shorted, the input is inductive (E9F10); open, capacitive (E9F11). The stub-tuner intuition pins the eighth-wave row — the shorted stub is the inductor that tuners have always hung across a feed line, so short goes to L, and open must be C.

{{fig:ch09-stub-matching}}

> **Worked example:** Run the table the way the exam does. Short the far end of a λ/2 line and the input repeats it — very low impedance (E9F04). The same short on a λ/4 line inverts — very high (E9F09). Open a λ/4 line and the inversion goes the other way — very low (E9F12). And at λ/8 the line becomes a reactance machine: shorted makes inductive (E9F10), open makes capacitive (E9F11). Repeats at λ/2, inverts at λ/4, reactance at λ/8 — six answers from three rules.

That is E9F — twelve questions, E9F01 through E9F12.

**FACT:** Velocity factor is the wave velocity in the line divided by the velocity of light in a vacuum, the dielectric insulating material has the biggest effect on it, and electrical length exceeds physical length because waves move slower in the line — a λ/2 air-insulated parallel line at 14.10 MHz is about 10.6 m long (λ/2 ≈ 150 × VF / f(MHz) with air VF ≈ 1.0).

**FACT:** The transmission-line transformation table: a λ/2 line repeats its termination (shorted far end → very low impedance at the input); a λ/4 line inverts its termination (shorted → very high impedance, open → very low); a λ/8 line converts to reactance (shorted → inductive, open → capacitive).

### The Smith Chart in Full: Reading Figure E9-3

Book 3 mentioned the Smith chart in a sidebar; the Extra exam makes you read one. What it is: a plot of normalized impedance on a coordinate system built from two families of curves — resistance circles and reactance arcs (E9G02, E9G04, E9G10, whose distractors invert the families). What it is for: calculating impedance along transmission lines and reading impedance and SWR values (E9G01, E9G03) — and its classic use is determining the length and position of an impedance-matching stub (E9G05). Normalizing means reassigning the prime center's impedance value to your system impedance, so the 1.0 point stands for 50 Ω (E9G08). During the design of a matching network, a third family of circles is often added — constant-SWR circles, every one centered on the prime center (E9G09). And on a full chart, the rim's wavelength scales are calibrated in fractions of transmission-line electrical wavelength (E9G11) — the line's electrical wavelength, not the antenna's. One caution about the pool's own figure: the published Figure E9-3 is the simplified chart and prints no wavelength scales at all, so the redraw below has none either — E9G11's rim scales are a fact about full Smith charts, not something to hunt for on this drawing.

Here is the redrawn Figure E9-3.

{{fig:ch09-pool-fig-e93}}

Orient yourself first. Infinity — the open-circuit point — sits at the right end of the horizontal diameter, and zero, the short circuit, at the left; that is the orientation the pool's first errata rotated the chart into, the conventional horizontal form. The horizontal diameter is the chart's only straight line, and it carries the resistance-circle labels 0, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, and 20, ascending left to right, with the 1.0 point at mid-chart the prime center. The constant-resistance circles are all tangent at the infinity point and bulge to the left from it. The constant-reactance arcs sweep from the rim into the infinity point, labeled 0.2 through 5.0 above the axis — inductive, +jX — with the same magnitudes mirrored below for capacitive, −jX. Every reactance arc terminates on the large outer circle.

The two figure questions are a naming pair, and they share an option pool, so "reactance axis" is the keyed answer in one and a distractor in the other. The large outer circle on which the reactance arcs terminate is the reactance axis (E9G06). The only straight line on the chart — the horizontal diameter — is the resistance axis (E9G07). Circles for resistance, arcs for reactance, the rim circle named reactance, the straight line named resistance: say the four aloud once and both questions turn mechanical.

What a plotted point means: a perfect 50 Ω load normalizes to 1.0 and lands exactly on the prime center, dead middle of the chart. Any mismatch lands off-center — the resistive part picks the circle, the reactive part picks the arc, and the point sits where the two curves cross. How far off-center it lands is the mismatch itself: every point the same distance from the prime center shares the same SWR, which is why the constant-SWR family is circles around that center, and why walking a load along a lossless line traces a circle — the mismatch magnitude stays put while the phase turns with the line's electrical length.

The stub procedure is what the chart exists for in the first place, and E9G05's classic use is exactly this walk.

> **Worked example:** Matching with a stub, the chart's classic job (E9G05). Normalize the load — divide its impedance by 50 Ω — and plot the point on the redrawn Figure E9-3. Set the compass on the prime center and swing the constant-SWR circle through the point. Walk the circle toward the generator until it crosses the 1.0 resistance circle — on a full chart the rim's wavelength scale counts this walk in fractions of a wavelength, but the redrawn E9-3 carries no rim scales, so count the electrical distance, not a printed scale. There the line presents a resistive 50 Ω plus some leftover reactance, which a pure-reactance stub can finish. The electrical distance you walked sets where the stub goes on the line, and the reactance you read at the crossing is what the stub must cancel — a shorted stub cut to supply the equal and opposite reactance, per the λ/8 row of the transformation table.

That is E9G — eleven questions, E9G01 through E9G11.

**FACT:** On Figure E9-3, the large outer circle on which the reactance arcs terminate is the reactance axis, and the only straight line (the horizontal diameter) is the resistance axis.

### Receiving and Direction-Finding Antennas

The Beverage is the low-band receiving antenna: a long wire a few feet off the ground, pointed at what you want to hear. Build it at least one wavelength long for good performance at the desired frequency (E9H01) — the directivity comes from wave travel along the wire, and a shorter wire gives up the effect. A signal arriving from the direction the wire points sweeps along it and builds up a traveling wave that marches toward your receiver, adding up over the wire's whole length; a signal arriving from the opposite end launches its wave the other way, toward the far end — and there the terminating resistor absorbs it (E9H07), which is where the front-to-back comes from. The correct termination value shows itself as the minimum variation in SWR over the desired frequency range (E9H06) — flat SWR across the band, not maximum DC current, not a front-to-back measurement.

Why bother with a dedicated receiving antenna at all: on 160 and 80 meters, atmospheric noise is so high that directivity matters far more than losses (E9H02). Your receiver hears the band's noise floor, not its own, so an inefficient antenna costs you almost nothing while a directive one drops the noise arriving from every direction you are not listening to. The metric is the receiving directivity factor — peak antenna gain compared to average gain over the hemisphere around and above the antenna (E9H03). Peak against the hemisphere's average: not front-to-back ratio and not dBi or dBd, which are the distractors.

Direction finding adds its own craft. A small DF loop's deep null is spoiled by unbalanced capacitive coupling to the surroundings, and an electrostatic shield around the loop eliminates that coupling, improving the null depth (E9H04). The loop's standing challenge is its bidirectional null — the figure-eight pattern hands you two bearings 180° apart and no way to tell which is the station (E9H05). Add a sense antenna and the combined pattern becomes a cardioid with a null in only one direction (E9H08), resolving the ambiguity in a single measurement. A terminated single-turn loop such as a pennant is cardioid for the same reason (E9H09). More turns or more enclosed area increases a multiturn loop's output voltage (E9H10). And the cardioid's DF value is precisely its single null (E9H11) — you steer for the null, never for a peak, because a null is far sharper than any maximum.

That is E9H — eleven questions, E9H01 through E9H11, and the end of the subelement.

### Key Takeaways

- dBd = dBi − 2.15, so 6 dBi is 3.85 dBd; ERP pairs with dBd and EIRP with dBi, and the chain is TPO × 10^((gains − losses)/10) with the losses subtracted — 286 W, 317 W, and 252 W EIRP on the pool's three stations.

- Directivity redirects power, it does not create it; the redrawn Figure E9-1 reads a 50° beamwidth, 18 dB front-to-back, and 14 dB front-to-side, while the redrawn Figure E9-2 reads an elevation pattern peaking at 7.5° with 28 dB front-to-back.

- Two phased verticals: in phase at λ/2 spacing gives a broadside figure-eight, 180° out of phase gives end-fire along the axis, and λ/4 spacing with a 90° feed gives a cardioid — the phase and the orientation move together.

- Zepp is end-fed a half wave, the extended double Zepp is center-fed 1.25λ, the G5RV runs open-wire line to a balun and coax, the folded dipole presents about 300 Ω, and the OCFD is fed off-center for a similar feed impedance on multiple bands.

- Loading cancels a short whip's capacitive reactance, works most efficiently near the center or the top, and raises Q so the SWR bandwidth decreases; doubling the operating frequency adds 6 dB to an ideal dish.

- The transformation table: λ/2 repeats, λ/4 inverts, λ/8 makes reactance — shorted goes inductive, open goes capacitive — and the Q-section is the geometric mean, 75 Ω to match 50 to 100.

- On the redrawn Figure E9-3, infinity sits at the right end of the diameter, the only straight line is the resistance axis, the large outer circle is the reactance axis, and normalizing reassigns the prime center to your system impedance.

- On 160 and 80 meters, receiving directivity beats efficiency; a Beverage wants at least one wavelength of wire and a termination that flattens SWR across the band, and a cardioid's DF value is its single null.
