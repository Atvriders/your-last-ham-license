## 3. Propagation beyond the Ordinary

One evening you swing your 6-meter beam north at nothing in particular — no repeater, no beacon, just cold sky — and the speaker fills with a buzzing, fluttering CW signal from a station a thousand miles away, on a path that shouldn't exist at all. Book 3 taught you the everyday ionosphere: the D region absorbing by day, the E and F regions bending your signal home, the MUF rising and falling with the sun. This chapter is about the paths that aren't everyday at all — moonbounce, tropospheric ducts, meteor trails, auroral curtains, transequatorial chords, and the summer's sporadic E. In this chapter you'll learn what triggers each exotic path, what frequencies it favors, which way to point, and when to listen: the cause, range, geometry, and time pattern of every mode in pool subelement E3.

### The Wave Itself

Before the exotic paths, the wave that travels them. An electromagnetic wave is two fields at once — an electric field and a magnetic field — and the two stand at right angles to each other in space. The wave itself travels at a right angle to both (E3A04): point the electric field east and the magnetic field up, and the wave moves north. The exam's classic trap here is a phase distractor. The two fields rise and fall together, in time-phase; they are spatially perpendicular, not "90 degrees out of phase" (E3A05). Right angles is a statement about geometry, not timing, and that one distinction is the difference between the keyed answer and the most-chosen wrong one.

The geometry has a practical edge you already use. A vertical whip couples to the vertical component of the passing wave's electric field, which is why polarization match matters on line-of-sight paths and why cross-polarized stations on 2 m simplex can be 20 dB down while both swear the band is dead. Hold the picture: two fields, mutually perpendicular, the wave marching off at right angles to the pair.

What sets the wave's speed is the medium it moves through — specifically, the medium's index of refraction (E3A10). That single fact explains most of what follows in this chapter. A wave crossing into a region of different refractive index changes speed, and the change bends the path. The ionosphere bends HF back to Earth that way; a temperature inversion bends VHF around the curve of the Earth that way.

One more property matters for later: polarization can rotate. A circularly polarized wave is one whose electric and magnetic fields rotate as the wave advances (E3A14) — the corkscrew you met in chapter 2's satellite work, where a tumbling bird and Faraday rotation keep changing the polarization arriving at your antenna.

And the ordinary daily rhythm, because it frames everything else: when darkness drops the MUF below your band, you move to a lower-frequency band to keep the path (E3A06). HF operators plan the evening migration 20 to 40 to 80 m the same way VHF operators watch the evening tropo build — you follow the propagation; it never follows you.

That's E3A's physics five: E3A04, E3A05, E3A10, E3A14, and E3A06.

### EME Geometry: The Moon as a Reflector

The longest regular path in amateur radio is also the simplest to describe: bounce your signal off the moon. Any two stations with the moon mutually visible can attempt Earth-Moon-Earth, and since about half the globe sees the moon at any given moment, the maximum separation between EME stations is about 12,000 miles measured along the surface of the Earth (E3A01). The pool puts "visible" in quotes for a reason — the moon clears your local horizon, and you have a path. Mutual-visibility windows are why EME schedules are negotiated around moonrise and moonset: a station in Florida and a station in Hawaii share the moon for hours, but a station in Japan and one in Germany catch each other only in narrow windows when the moon hangs between them.

Scheduling matters for a second reason. Path loss is least when the moon is at perigee, its closest approach to Earth (E3A03), and the perigee-versus-apogee swing is worth about 2 dB on the round trip — the number WSJT-X reports as DGRD when you plan a schedule. The distractors are mirror traps: a full moon is bright, not close, and apogee is the far end of the orbit. Keep the scale of the undertaking in mind, too — EME path loss runs about 252 dB at 144 MHz and about 271 dB at 1296 MHz, which is why moonbounce contacts live at the edge of decodability even with big antennas and serious power, and why a free 2 dB is worth scheduling around.

Then there's the sound of the path. The moon is a rough, curved, wobbling reflector, and echoes off its face travel slightly different distances and arrive out of step, combining and canceling — multipath, in other words, off a mirror that won't hold still. The result is libration fading: a fluttery, irregular fading on the returned signal (E3A02), with slow, deep polarization fading layered on top as the echo's polarization rotates on the way home. Nothing at your station fixes it; you wait out the deep fades and take the peaks.

Chapter 2 owns the operating discipline — Q65, the time-synchronous sequences, the strict receive/transmit alternation. This chapter owns the geometry: E3A01, E3A02, and E3A03.

### Tropospheric Ducts

The most common VHF enhancement isn't in the ionosphere at all — it's weather. When a temperature inversion forms, warm air stacked over cool surface air, the refractive index changes fast enough with height to bend VHF and UHF energy back toward the surface. The bent ray returns to Earth, reflects, and bends over again: trapped between the ground and the layer, the signal pipes itself far past the normal horizon. That's tropospheric ducting, and the steeper ray that would have escaped to space on a normal day is the one that suddenly works three states away.

The exam pins two facts about it. Atmospheric ducts capable of propagating microwave signals often form over large bodies of water (E3A07), and the typical range for tropospheric duct propagation of microwave signals is 100 to 300 miles (E3A11). Not 10 to 50, not 1,200 — a few hundred miles, over water.

{{fig:ch03-ducting}}

Operators have a name for the condition: a duct opening is a "lift," and the habit that predicts one is reading the surface weather map rather than the space-weather page. A strong high-pressure system settling in brings subsiding warm air aloft and the inversion that builds the duct, and paths over water — the Great Lakes, the Gulf Coast, offshore California — are the reliable producers. You'll recognize a lift on the air before you ever check a map: 2 m simplex starts sounding like a local repeater net, with full-quieting stations two and three hundred miles out who have no business being there on any normal evening. When the forecast parks a big high over your favorite water path, point the beam along it and call CQ. That's E3A07 and E3A11.

### Meteor Scatter

Every day, around the clock, meteors streak into the upper atmosphere and burn, and each one leaves a brief, pencil-thin ionized trail hanging where it died. The exam wants the altitude pinned by layer: the trail forms at E-region height (E3A08) — not the F2 region your HF reflex reaches for. And the frequencies that scatter best off those trails run 28 to 148 MHz (E3A09), which is why 6 m and 2 m dominate the mode while 160 m and 70 cm sit out the dance.

The mode is bursts. A typical usable trail gives a ping shorter than a tenth of a second, and modern digital meteor scatter works exactly those pings — any day of the year, no shower required — out to about 1,300 miles. Once in a while a big trail, the kind old-timers call a blue whizzer, holds a path open for several seconds of solid copy. Ping duration scales as 1/f², so pings at 144 MHz run about an eighth as long as pings at 50 MHz. The named showers raise the ping rate — more meteors per hour means more trails overhead — and the morning hours beat the evening ones, because the Earth's leading face sweeps up particles the way a windshield collects bugs. But the background rain of sporadic meteors never stops, which is why the mode works in March as well as in Perseid season.

{{fig:ch03-meteor-scatter}}

> **Worked example:** Ping duration scales as 1/f² — halve the frequency and pings last four times longer. A trail that gives a 40 ms ping on 144 MHz gives roughly $40 \times (144/50)^2 \approx 330$ ms on 50 MHz — the practical reason 6 m is the forgiving band for learning meteor scatter, and the reason MSK144 carries a 20 ms short format for the briefest 2 m pings.

This is exactly the physics MSK144 was built to ride, and chapter 2 covered the protocol itself: a complete 144-bit message every 72 ms, repeated through each 15-second transmit slot, so a single fraction-of-a-second ping can carry a whole exchange. The lineage runs from European operators who worked high-speed CW off slowed cassette tapes, through K1JT's FSK441 in 2001, to MSK144 today. The operator reality is patience — a 6 m contact can mean a quarter hour of mostly silence, a 2 m one an hour — and skeds get arranged on ping-jockey sites rather than met by luck; 50.260 MHz serves as the 6 m MSK144 calling frequency by informal convention. Meteor scatter: E3A08 and E3A09.

### Aurora

When a severe geomagnetic storm arrives, the same disturbance that ruins HF hands VHF operators one of the strangest paths in the hobby: auroral propagation (E3A12). Charged particles stream into the polar upper atmosphere and light up the auroral curtain — a vast, rippling, field-aligned structure that scatters VHF signals. This is the same storm-watching you'll meet again in the space-weather section below: climbing A- and K-indices, an arriving CME, and then the curtain comes alive.

The geometry breaks every habit you have. Both stations beam north — at the curtain, never at each other. Regardless of where the station you want to work actually sits, you point your antenna at the aurora, they point theirs at the aurora, and the curtain scatters your signals between you. A direct great-circle path between the two stations is irrelevant; the curtain is the reflector. Once signals appear, swing the beam either side of north to maximize them.

{{fig:ch03-aurora-geometry}}

Then there's the sound, which is the exam's point. The curtain never holds still, and its motion imposes fast, erratic phase distortion on everything it scatters: a pure carrier comes back spread into a buzz a few kilohertz wide, and SSB comes back as mushy, unintelligible garble. The mode that survives is the one that's nothing but a keyed carrier — CW is the most usable mode for auroral propagation (E3A13). The distortion grows with frequency, so 6 m is easier than 2 m, and 2 m is easier than 70 cm. If you only remember one operating habit from this chapter, make it this one: storm warning, beam north, send CW.

One grace note: during some events an auroral-E mode appears — E-layer propagation tied to the aurora but without the buzz, behaving for all the world like ordinary sporadic E. If the "aurora" sounds clean, it probably isn't the curtain anymore. Aurora: E3A12 and E3A13.

### The F-Layer Exotics

Now the paths that bend the F region itself into shapes Book 3 never covered. First, transequatorial propagation — TEP. It works between stations about 2,000 to 3,000 miles apart on a path roughly perpendicular to the geomagnetic equator (E3B01): across the equator, not along it. In practice that means stations sitting roughly 1,500 to 2,500 miles north and south of the geomagnetic equator — which tilts through South America, well south of the geographic one — with the path crossing it within about 20 degrees of a right angle. The maximum range is about 5,000 miles (E3B02): two different numbers living in two different questions, so keep station separation and maximum range unmerged in your head. TEP is most likely in the afternoon or early evening (E3B03), and the mechanism is beautiful — the signal ducts between the two equatorial-anomaly electron peaks that build on either side of the geomagnetic equator, making two F-region hops with no ground reflection in between. The first amateur TEP contact ran Mexico to Argentina on 50 MHz in August 1947, and 6 m operators still chase the mode near the equinoxes and at high sunspot numbers. Old hands distinguish two flavors: afternoon TEP, strong and steady with the MUF reaching about 60 MHz, and a later evening TEP with Doppler spread and rapid fading that has carried contacts on 2 m and sometimes 70 cm.

{{fig:ch03-transequatorial}}

While you're picturing the ionosphere as a magnetized medium, add one more mechanism to the inventory: the ionosphere splits an incoming wave into two independently propagating, elliptically polarized components — the ordinary and extraordinary waves (E3B04). You will rarely hear operators discuss them on the air, but they are why polarization twists and fades on HF paths.

Darkness next. Long-haul 160 m propagation needs a path entirely in darkness, because D-region absorption kills any sunlit stretch of a low-frequency path (E3B05). Long path — beaming the "wrong" way around the world to meet the other station's dawn — is most frequent on 40 and 20 m (E3B06). And whatever band you're on, a lower takeoff angle means longer hops (E3B07): the shallower the launch, the farther each refraction carries the signal before it comes down. Ground wave is the other low-angle mode, with two rules of its own: it supports vertical polarization only (E3B13), and its maximum range decreases as frequency increases (E3B08) — which is why 160 m ground wave outreaches 10 m ground wave.

Sporadic E gets the wording you have to get exactly right. It peaks around the solstices, especially the summer solstice (E3B09) — which is why the June VHF contest lands in prime Es season and 6 m earns its "magic band" reputation. And the pool keys it as a daytime mode: most likely between sunrise and sunset (E3B11), because the mechanism is solar ionization. Plenty of 6 m operators will tell you about the fine evening Es they've worked, and that operating lore is real — but it is lore, not the exam answer. The sunset-to-midnight choice is the trap laid for experienced operators; the keyed answer is the daytime one, and it comes with its mechanism attached.

Finally, the chordal hop: successive ionospheric refractions without an intermediate reflection from the ground (E3B12). The signal refracts off the layer, skims along beneath it, and refracts again — never touching down between hops. Skipping the lossy ground bounce is precisely why the mode works: the signal experiences less loss than in ordinary multi-hop propagation, which uses the Earth as a reflector (E3B10).

That's the whole E3B group — thirteen questions, E3B01 through E3B13.

### Space Weather and the Tools

Everything above has a trigger, and the triggers are readable. Start with the two indices. The A-index and K-index measure geomagnetic disturbance, and a rising value of either one means increasing disturbance of the geomagnetic field (E3C02). The K-index runs 0 to 9; watch it climb through an afternoon and you know what the evening holds. When the indices run high, the paths that suffer most are the ones through the auroral oval, where absorption hammers the signal (E3C03). Notice that this is one event seen from two bands: the same storm that blacks out the HF operator's polar path is the aurora section's VHF bonanza.

The coupling mechanism has a name worth learning: Bz, the north–south component of the interplanetary magnetic field (E3C04). Orientation is everything. A southward Bz couples solar-wind energy into the magnetosphere and produces disturbed conditions (E3C05); a northward Bz is the calm orientation. The exam tests the inversion directly, so pin it before you move on: southward is the troublemaker.

Then the event scales — three of them, and the distractors interleave them on purpose. Solar flare classes ascend A, B, C, M, X, with class X indicating the greatest intensity (E3C07). Geomagnetic storms use the G scale, with G5 an extreme storm (E3C08). And the K-index runs 0 to 9, as above. Flare letters and storm G-numbers measure different events; X5 is a flare, G5 is a storm, and never the twain shall trade places.

Two on-air signatures are worth recognizing live. A sudden short-term HF blackout — the bands simply dying on the daylight side of the planet — comes from a solar flare, whose X-rays ionize the sunlit D region until it absorbs everything trying to pass (E3C01). And a sudden rise in background noise across a large portion of the HF spectrum likewise announces that a coronal mass ejection impact or a solar flare has occurred (E3C12). When the noise floor jumps and then the band goes quiet, you've heard the event arrive in real time.

One everyday number, and it's the exam's piece of arithmetic: the VHF/UHF radio horizon is approximately 15 percent farther than the geographic horizon, because atmospheric refraction bends the wave slightly past the visual line (E3C06).

> **Worked example:** How much farther is the radio horizon? A hilltop repeater with a 20-mile geographic line of sight gains the atmospheric-refraction bonus the pool pins at 15 percent: $20 \times 1.15 \approx 23$ miles (E3C06). The same 15 percent applies to every VHF/UHF path — it's why the 2 m simplex record books run longer than the map says they should.

The tools finish the group. Propagation reporting networks — PSK Reporter, WSPRNet, the Reverse Beacon Network — report digital-mode and CW sightings (E3C09), which makes them the practical answer to the oldest question in the hobby: where is my signal actually being heard right now? Call once, then read the map. The 304A index measures solar ultraviolet emissions at 304 angstroms, correlated to the solar flux index (E3C10) — angstroms of UV, not gigahertz of radio. And VOACAP is the software that models HF propagation (E3C11), the descendant of the IONCAP predictions DXers once ran from printed tables; feed it two locations, a band, and a solar number, and it sketches the hours and bands most likely to connect you. Space weather and its instruments: E3C01 through E3C12, twelve questions.

**FACT:** Auroral propagation follows severe geomagnetic storms; the signals arrive phase-distorted (the moving curtain spreads a carrier into a buzz a few kHz wide), so CW is the most usable mode — beam north first, both stations pointing at the curtain rather than at each other.

**FACT:** Transequatorial propagation works between stations about 2,000–3,000 miles apart on a path roughly perpendicular to the geomagnetic equator (practice: about 1,500–2,500 miles north and south of it, crossing within ±20° of right angles), with a maximum range of about 5,000 miles, most likely in the afternoon or early evening, ducting between the two equatorial-anomaly electron peaks with no intermediate ground reflection.

**FACT:** Sporadic E peaks around the solstices, especially the summer solstice, and the pool keys it as a daytime mode — most likely between sunrise and sunset (see §7.6 for the evening-Es operating lore).

**FACT:** Chordal hop is successive ionospheric refraction without an intermediate ground reflection — skipping the lossy ground bounce means less loss than ordinary multi-hop.

**FACT:** Bz is the north–south component of the interplanetary magnetic field, and a southward Bz couples solar-wind energy into the magnetosphere, producing disturbed conditions.

### Exam Focus

Subelement E3 — Radio Wave Propagation, groups E3A through E3C — puts 39 questions in the pool, and your 50-question exam draws 3 of them. The eight below are the high-value confusables: the perigee/apogee mirror, the E-region meteor trail, the TEP geometry, the sporadic-E timing question whose pool answer is the daytime one, and the space-weather vocabulary.

> **E3A03** When scheduling EME contacts, which of these conditions will generally result in the least path loss?
> A. When the Moon is at perigee
> B. When the Moon is full
> C. When the Moon is at apogee
> D. When the MUF is above 30 MHz
> **Answer: A** — The moon's closest approach gives the least path loss, and a full moon is bright, not close — taught in chapter 2.

> **E3A08** When a meteor strikes the Earth’s atmosphere, a linear ionized region is formed at what region of the ionosphere?
> A. The E region
> B. The F1 region
> C. The F2 region
> D. The D region
> **Answer: A** — Meteor trails ionize at E-region height; the F2 choice is the reflexive HF trap.

> **E3A13** Which of these emission modes is best for auroral propagation?
> A. CW
> B. SSB
> C. FM
> D. RTTY
> **Answer: A** — The moving curtain phase-distorts every signal into a buzz, and a keyed carrier is what survives it.

> **E3B01** Where is transequatorial propagation (TEP) most likely to occur?
> A. Between points separated by 2,000 miles to 3,000 miles over a path perpendicular to the geomagnetic equator
> B. Between points located 1,500 miles to 2,000 miles apart on the geomagnetic equator
> C. Between points located at each other’s antipode
> D. Through the region where the terminator crosses the geographic equator
> **Answer: A** — TEP runs across the geomagnetic equator, not along it, between stations 2,000 to 3,000 miles apart.

> **E3B11** At what time of day is sporadic-E propagation most likely to occur?
> A. Between midnight and sunrise
> B. Between sunset and midnight
> C. Between sunset and sunrise
> D. Between sunrise and sunset
> **Answer: D** — The pool keys sporadic E as a daytime, solar-ionization mode; sunset-to-midnight is the operating-lore trap.

> **E3B12** What is chordal-hop propagation?
> A. Propagation away from the great circle bearing between stations
> B. Successive ionospheric refractions without an intermediate reflection from the ground
> C. Propagation across the geomagnetic equator
> D. Signals reflected back toward the transmitting station
> **Answer: B** — A chordal hop keeps refracting off the ionosphere and skips the lossy mid-path ground bounce.

> **E3C04** What does the value of Bz (B sub z) represent?
> A. Geomagnetic field stability
> B. Critical frequency for vertical transmissions
> C. North-south strength of the interplanetary magnetic field
> D. Duration of long-delayed echoes
> **Answer: C** — Bz is simply the north–south axis of the interplanetary magnetic field.

> **E3C06** How does the VHF/UHF radio horizon compare to the geographic horizon?
> A. It is approximately 15 percent farther
> B. It is approximately 20 percent nearer
> C. It is approximately 50 percent farther
> D. They are approximately the same
> **Answer: A** — Atmospheric refraction bends VHF/UHF about 15 percent beyond the visible horizon.

### Key Takeaways

- The E and H fields of a radio wave are at right angles to each other, the wave travels at right angles to both, and the fields are spatially perpendicular but in time-phase — "at right angles," never "90 degrees out of phase."

- EME works between any two stations with the moon mutually visible, up to about 12,000 miles apart along the Earth's surface; path loss is least at perigee, and libration gives the echo its flutter.

- Tropospheric ducts form over large bodies of water and pipe VHF/UHF 100 to 300 miles; meteor trails ionize at E-region height and scatter best from 28 to 148 MHz.

- Aurora follows severe geomagnetic storms: both stations beam north at the curtain, and the phase distortion it adds makes CW the usable mode.

- TEP crosses the geomagnetic equator between stations 2,000 to 3,000 miles apart, with a 5,000-mile maximum range and an afternoon-or-early-evening peak; sporadic E peaks at the solstices and the pool keys it as a sunrise-to-sunset daytime mode.

- A rising A- or K-index means growing geomagnetic disturbance, a southward Bz invites it, flare classes top out at X while storms top out at G5, and the radio horizon runs about 15 percent past the visible one.
