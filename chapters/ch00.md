## 0. The Last Upgrade: Why Extra, and How This Book Works

You called into the pileup for the better part of an hour. The rare one was running simplex CW down at 14.005 MHz — the bottom sliver of 20 meters, where the crowd is thin and a sharp operator can actually pick out calls — and your General license stops at 14.025 MHz, so you listened while a parade of stations worked him, one after another, and you hoped he would tune up the band to you. He never did. That bottom 25 kHz is the Amateur Extra license in miniature, and this book is the course that gets you the whole band. In this chapter you'll learn exactly what Extra opens beyond your General privileges, what the one exam between you and it looks like — 50 questions, 37 of 50 to pass, drawn from a public pool that expires 2028-06-30 — what happens at the session and in the days after you pass, and how this book is organized to take you from experienced General to passed Extra.

### What the Extra License Opens

The one-sentence version is ARRL's own: "The Amateur Extra class license conveys all available U.S. Amateur Radio operating privileges on all bands and all modes."

Hold that against where you stand. The FCC currently issues new licenses in only three license classes — Technician, General, and Amateur Extra — so Extra is the top of the US license structure: there is no higher class and no element beyond Element 4. (Novice and Advanced live on only as grandfathered renewals.) As a General you already hold some privileges on every band; Extra completes the map. Your new privileges are exact, and they live in exactly five places:

- 80 m: 3.500–3.525 MHz

- 75 m: 3.600–3.800 MHz

- 40 m: 7.000–7.025 MHz and, in Region 2, 7.125–7.175 MHz

- 20 m: 14.000–14.025 MHz and 14.150–14.225 MHz

- 15 m: 21.000–21.025 MHz and 21.200–21.275 MHz

That is the entire exclusive territory — Extra-only spectrum exists only on 80, 75, 40, 20, and 15 meters. Everything else you may have heard about is already yours: 160, 60, 30, 17, 12, and 10 meters, and every VHF and UHF band, General shares in full. One nuance for precision: holders of the legacy Advanced class — no longer issued, still renewed — share parts of those slices, so a few of them are Extra-only among currently issued licenses rather than Extra-only in absolute terms. These slices are the surviving architecture of incentive licensing, the late-1960s restructuring that layered HF privileges by class to reward upgrading, and they were placed where they would be coveted.

They still are. The bottom of the band is where the DX lives: a rare station working CW at 14.005 MHz can only be answered by US Extras, and 14.150–14.225 MHz — the bottom of the 20 m phone band — is prime real estate for the same reason. The pileups are thinner down there, because every General on the band is, by rule, somewhere above you, and the calling grounds are quieter whether you chase DX, contest points, or unhurried CW. And 60 meters, for the record, is not an Extra prize: it is shared by General and above, so do not upgrade expecting it.

The upgrade reaches beyond spectrum. Travel: under FCC Public Notice DA 16-1048, a US Amateur Extra or Advanced grant earns full CEPT privileges in participating European countries under Recommendation T/R 61-01 — CEPT being the European postal-and-telecommunications conference whose arrangement lets US amateurs operate abroad — while a General grant earns only CEPT Novice privileges. Operating in a CEPT country means carrying three documents: a copy of the Public Notice, proof of US citizenship, and evidence of your FCC license grant. Call signs: the Group A vanity call sign formats — 1×2, 2×1, and 2×2 with an A-first prefix — are reserved to Extra, which is why the shortest calls in the pileup sound the way they do. And service: an Amateur Extra licensee may serve as a Volunteer Examiner at all three exam levels, including administering Element 4 itself — after one session, you could be sitting on the other side of that table.

### The Capstone Exam: Element 4

First, the best news in this chapter: you take one exam, not three.

**FACT:** A General upgrading to Extra needs Element 4 only: an unexpired (or in-grace-period) General license earns Elements 2 and 3 examination credit, so Elements 2 and 3 are not re-tested.

Your General grant already credits Elements 2 and 3; the only new hurdle is Element 4, the written exam on Amateur Extra privileges. The credit carries one practical condition — your General license must be unexpired or inside its renewal grace period — so if your ten-year term is anywhere near its end, renew first and upgrade second.

**FACT:** "Element 4: 50 questions concerning the privileges of an Amateur Extra Class operator license. The minimum passing score is 37 questions answered correctly."

The shape is the one you know, scaled up. Fifty questions, drawn one from each of the question pool's 50 groups, out of a public 599-question pool organized into ten subelements, E1 through E9 plus E0 — rules, operating procedures, propagation, amateur practices, electrical principles, circuit components, practical circuits, signals and emissions, antennas and transmission lines, and safety. (E7 and E9 run deep enough that their group letters reach H.) The rule pins a count, not a percentage: **37 of 50** to pass — you can miss thirteen and still walk out an upgrader. Wherever you see "74%" — including in this book — treat it as derived arithmetic, never the authority.

Now the date that matters, stated as plainly as we can put it.

**FACT:** "EXTRA Class (Element 4) Pool is effective July 1, 2024 and is valid until June 30, 2028," and question pools rotate on a four-year cycle (Technician 2026–2030; General 2023–2027; Extra 2024–2028; "No question pools will be updated or released in 2025 or 2029") — the next Extra pool takes effect 2028-07-01.

**This book tracks the 2024–2028 pool, and that pool expires 2028-06-30.** Until then there are no surprise questions: every question on every Element 4 exam is printed verbatim in Appendix A of this book, with the keyed answer and a one-line why. The pool is written by the NCVEC Question Pool Committee and released into the public domain; this book carries the current document, with all four errata incorporated (2024-01-31, 2024-11-08, 2025-09-24/25, and 2026-02-04) and no fifth errata as of this writing. Four questions were withdrawn along the way — E9E10, E2A13, E6D07, and E4D05 — withdrawn without renumbering, never quoted and never tested; three of them survive as numbering gaps in their groups, while E2A13 was its group's last question and leaves no gap. If you are reading this late in the window, the honest advice is the same as it was for General: schedule your exam before the window closes.

One structural fact should drive how you study: because every exam draws exactly one question from every group, each of the 50 groups is guaranteed exactly one seat on your exam — no topic can be skipped, and none will be. The back half of the pool carries the weight: subelements E6 through E0 supply 27 of the 50 seats, and E7 (practical circuits) plus E9 (antennas and transmission lines) alone supply 16. Element 4 is the deepest of the three exams, and it is built so that half-learning shows. That is not a threat; it is the reason this book teaches the physics instead of the flashcards.

### Exam Day, for Upgraders

The logistics are lighter than your first two trips through the system, because the identity work is long done. You have an FRN — the FCC Registration Number you registered in CORES before your Technician exam — and it stays with you for life, so there is no new registration step. (If you somehow never obtained one, registering in CORES carries no fee and no exam requirement.)

Finding a session works the way you remember: the ARRL's online session finder and hamstudy.org/sessions list sessions from many VE teams. Exams are administered by teams of at least three Volunteer Examiners working under a Volunteer Examiner Coordinator, and at an Element 4 session every VE at the table must hold Amateur Extra. Some teams run remote video-supervised exams online alongside the in-person kind, but availability depends entirely on the individual VE team — Laurel VEC runs in-person only — so treat remote testing as a question to ask a team, never a plan to count on.

The session fee, for calendar 2026: the ARRL VEC charges $15.00.

Candidates younger than 18 pay a reduced five dollars, and Laurel VEC charges nothing at all. Then comes the line item upgraders get to skip.

**FACT:** The FCC's $35 application fee (effective April 19, 2022) applies per application to new-license, renewal, rule-waiver, and vanity-call-sign applications — and "modification applications to upgrade an amateur radio licensee's operator class … are exempt from fees," so a General→Extra upgrader has no FCC payment step and no 10-day payment window.

If you remember the Technician-course drill — wait for the FCC email, pay online within ten days — you can let it go. The session fee you hand the VE team is the only money in the entire transaction.

What to bring is unchanged: one legal photo ID — or two forms of non-photo ID if you have none — your FRN, and the session fee. The application, the NCVEC Quick-Form 605, is completed at the session; the 2022 edition is current, and on it your email address and FRN are mandatory, and every applicant answers the Basic Qualification Question. When you hand in your answer sheet the VEs grade it on the spot. Pass, and the three Extra-class examiners certify your qualification and issue your CSCE — the Certificate of Successful Completion of Examination, your proof of the pass, valid for 365 days.

### After You Pass: On the Air Immediately, Signing /AE

Here is the upgrader's perk — and it arrives as a package, never in pieces. Under FCC rule §97.9(b), once two things have happened at the session — your Form 605 requesting the higher operator class has been properly submitted to the administering VEs, and the CSCE showing your pass is in your hand — you may exercise your new Extra privileges immediately: before the VEC files anything, before ULS changes, before any email arrives. And from the first transmission under those privileges, a second requirement rides along: you must identify with the AE indicator after your call sign. The interim authority runs until final disposition of the application or 365 days after you pass, whichever comes first.

**FACT:** While operating under §97.9(b), "an indicator must be included after the call sign as follows: … (3) For a control operator who has requested a license modification from Novice, Technician, General, or Advanced Class to Amateur Extra Class: AE" — the AE indicator is paragraph (f)(3); paragraph (f)(2) is AG for General.

Keep the paragraphs straight, because the exam does: (f)(3) is AE, for upgrades to Extra; (f)(2) is AG, the indicator you may have used upgrading to General. The rule itself — §97.119(c) — requires only that the indicator be separated from your call sign by the slant mark (/) or any suitable word that denotes it. One VEC's practical reading of that, printed here as practice rather than rule text: on phone, say your call sign followed by "temporary AE," as in "This is KX9ABC temporary AE"; on CW or digital modes, sign KX9ABC/AE. The suffix is the honest signal that you are exercising privileges your database entry does not yet show — use the new privileges only with the indicator attached, and only while both conditions hold. The day your ULS record shows Amateur Extra, drop the suffix and never say it again.

The rest of the wait is calm, and shorter than the one you remember. The VE team sends your application to the coordinating VEC, which screens it and forwards everything to the FCC electronically; your ULS record typically updates within days — typically, not a promise. Your call sign does not change: the station is reassigned its same call sign when the license is modified, unless you yourself apply for a new one. And keep the contrast with the Technician course in your pocket, because you will meet new hams who get it wrong: a first-time licensee has no operating authority at all until the grant appears in ULS, while an existing licensee upgrading operates immediately under §97.9(b). You are the second kind.

### How This Book Works

The architecture is the one that carried you through Books 2 and 3, retargeted at the Extra pool. The map is one pool subelement per chapter: E1, the commission's rules, is chapter 1; E2, operating procedures, is chapter 2; and so on in order through E0, safety, which is chapter 10. Every teaching chapter closes with an Exam Focus — verbatim pool questions from that chapter's subelement, each with its keyed answer and a one-line why — so you always know when you have a subelement cold. This chapter is the one exception: it owns no pool questions, so it closes with a checklist instead.

Behind the chapters sit the reference tools. Appendix A carries all 599 pool questions verbatim, in pool order, each with its answer and a one-line explanation naming the chapter that teaches it — your drill ground once the reading is done. Appendix B is the glossary and the Extra formula set with micro-examples. And the book's companion practice page builds realistic practice tests from the pool: 50 questions, one per group, exactly like the real session. Take them until 37 of 50 or better is routine, then go sit for the real thing.

The depth steps up, and you should know that going in. This is the deepest book in the series — real math this time, not as decoration but as the working material: complex impedance in rectangular and polar form, Q and bandwidth, link budgets, SWR and the reflection coefficient, ERP and EIRP, stub matching, the Smith chart properly. Every formula is derived just enough to be believed, then drilled with the pool's own numbers. What the book assumes is you — an experienced General. Reactance and resonance, decibels, the HF bands, how a VEC session runs: those are yours already, and General material gets at most a one-line refresher here. (If any of it has gone fuzzy, *Your Next Ham License: The General Course* is the refresher.)

Notation is the series convention you already know, with the Extra additions stated openly. Prose writes V for volts and the × sign for multiplication, while the pool states quantities in words, so the verbatim quotes and the prose never clash. Complex impedance appears as the exam writes it: 50 − j25 Ω in rectangular form, 55.9 ∠−26.6° Ω in polar. Unit case remains load-bearing: kHz with a lowercase k, MHz with a capital M, mA, pF — exactly as the pool prints them.

And what remains after Extra? Choice, not rank. The Group A vanity formats open to you. VE service at all three levels, including the exam you just passed. Your license keeps its ten-year term and renews like any other. But the honest answer is the craft itself: satellites and EME, weak-signal work, propagation physics, the Smith chart — the material in this book is the part of amateur radio that rewards an experienced operator for decades, and the license is almost a side effect. The series arc completes here: *Your First Ham License* for Technician (Element 2, 35 questions, 26 to pass), *Your Next Ham License* for General (Element 3, 35 questions, 26 to pass), and now *Your Last Ham License* for Extra — Element 4, 50 questions, 37 of 50, pool valid through 2028-06-30. Your last license. Not your last thing to learn.

### Your Capstone Checklist

Everything in this chapter, compressed into what you can actually do, starting today:

1. Confirm your FRN and the email address on your FCC registration are current — and renew first if your General term is nearly up.

2. Find an Element 4 session through the ARRL session finder or hamstudy.org/sessions — and ask the VE team directly if you need a remote option.

3. Read chapters 1 through 10 in order; each one owns one pool subelement.

4. Drill each subelement in Appendix A, then take practice tests on the book's practice page until 37 of 50 or better is routine.

5. The night before, gather your photo ID, your FRN, and the session fee.

6. Pass Element 4 and walk out with your CSCE.

7. Operate on your new Extra privileges immediately — Form 605 properly submitted to the VEs, CSCE in hand, signing /AE on every transmission that uses them.

8. Watch ULS for the Amateur Extra grant, and drop the /AE suffix the day it appears.

9. Call back into that pileup — the bottom of the band is yours now.

### Key Takeaways

- Extra conveys all available US Amateur Radio operating privileges on all bands and all modes — the Extra-only slices of 80, 75, 40, 20, and 15 meters on top of everything General already holds, plus full CEPT travel privileges, Group A vanity call signs, and VE service at every level.

- You pass Element 4 only — your General license credits Elements 2 and 3 — 50 questions drawn one per group from the public 599-question pool, with 37 of 50 to pass.

- The 2024–2028 pool is fixed and public, every exam question is in Appendix A verbatim — and the pool expires 2028-06-30, so plan to test inside the window.

- Upgrades are exempt from the FCC's $35 application fee: no payment step, no ten-day payment window — the VE team's session fee is the only money in the transaction.

- With your Form 605 properly submitted to the administering VEs and your CSCE in hand, you may use your new Extra privileges immediately — signing /AE on every transmission until your ULS record shows Amateur Extra.

- You keep your call sign, your ULS record typically updates within days, and what remains after Extra is choice, not rank — the craft is the prize.
