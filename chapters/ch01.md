## 1. The Rules at Extra Depth

You know your way around Part 97 — you passed General, and everything in that rule set still holds. What changes at Extra is the depth of the cut. On the General exam, knowing where the band edges sit was enough; on this one, the exam asks whether your signal stays inside them. Your dial reads 14.348 MHz on 20 meters, comfortably inside the band, and the *sideband* you are transmitting is not. That gap between where the dial sits and where your energy lands is the most-tested idea in this subelement. In this chapter you'll learn the Extra-only segments down to the kilohertz and the band-edge arithmetic the pool drills four ways; the special rules of 60, 2200, and 630 meters as they exist today; the space-station, telemetry, and telecommand vocabulary; protected places and stations in motion; the emission standards with their pinned numbers; the international and reciprocal map; the volunteer examiner system you may soon join; and the prohibited-communications, amplifier, Line A, and STA rules — all 68 questions of subelement E1, worth 6 of the 50 questions on your exam.

### The Extra-only slices and the band-edge math

Start with what the upgrade buys. The Extra ticket adds no new bands — it opens the reserved corners of five bands you already know. The Extra-only HF segments are 3.500–3.525 and 3.600–3.800 MHz on 80/75 meters, 7.000–7.025 and 7.125–7.175 MHz on 40 meters, 14.000–14.025 and 14.150–14.225 MHz on 20 meters, and 21.000–21.025 and 21.200–21.275 MHz on 15 meters — nothing anywhere else in the spectrum is Extra-exclusive. Holders of the legacy Advanced class share parts of those slices (their 75 meter phone segment starts at 3.700 MHz, not 3.600 MHz), so the bottom slice of 20 meter phone is one of the few places you work Extras only.

{{fig:ch01-extra-band-chart}}

Inside those bands, §97.305(c) sorts the emission types. On 40 meters, RTTY and data run 7.000–7.125 MHz with phone and image above; on 20 meters, data ends and phone begins at 14.150 MHz; on 15 meters the line sits at 21.200 MHz; on 10 meters at 28.3 MHz. The 30 meter band is RTTY and data only, and on 75 meters phone and image get the entire band. CW, as ever, may be transmitted on any frequency authorized to the control operator — one wonderfully simple sentence of §97.305(a).

Now the skill this group actually tests. A modern transceiver in SSB displays the suppressed-carrier frequency — where the carrier would sit if it were not suppressed. Your signal is not a line on the dial; it is a slab of spectrum about 3 kHz wide, and the dial marks one edge of that slab. On USB the signal occupies the displayed frequency up to displayed plus bandwidth. On LSB it occupies displayed minus bandwidth up to displayed. Section 97.307(b) then requires your emissions to be "confined to the band or segment available to the control operator" — so legality is judged on where the sideband energy falls, never on where the dial sits.

**FACT:** The Extra-only HF segments (in §97.301(b) but absent from the General table §97.301(d)) are exactly 3.500–3.525 MHz, 3.600–3.800 MHz, 7.000–7.025 MHz, 7.125–7.175 MHz (Region 2), 14.000–14.025 MHz, 14.150–14.225 MHz, 21.000–21.025 MHz, and 21.200–21.275 MHz — Extra-exclusive spectrum exists only on 80, 75, 40, 20, and 15 meters.

> **Worked example:** Your transceiver displays the suppressed-carrier frequency. Set USB at 14.348 MHz with a 3 kHz bandwidth and the signal occupies 14.348–14.351 MHz — the 20 meter band ends at 14.350 MHz, so the upper 1 kHz of your sideband is outside the band even though the dial reads legal (E1A01). LSB runs the geometry the other way: the signal occupies displayed − 3 kHz up to displayed, so the lowest legal LSB carrier is 3 kHz above the lower band edge — not the edge itself, and not 300 Hz or 1 kHz above it (E1A02).

> **Worked example:** The 20 meter RTTY/data segment ends at 14.150 MHz, so the highest legal carrier for a 2.8 kHz wide USB data signal is 14.150 − 0.0028 = 14.1472 MHz (E1A03). On 75 meters the Extra phone segment starts at 3.600 MHz, and an LSB carrier displayed at 3.601 MHz puts sideband energy down to about 3.598 MHz — beyond the segment edge, so you may not answer that CQ. The distractor claiming US stations may not use phone below 3.610 MHz is simply false, because Extra phone begins at 3.600 MHz (E1A04).

**FACT:** A USB signal occupies roughly carrier to carrier + 3 kHz and an LSB signal roughly carrier − 3 kHz to carrier, and §97.307(b) requires emissions to be "confined to the band or segment available to the control operator" — so a USB carrier at 14.348 MHz with 3 kHz bandwidth puts energy to 14.351 MHz and the upper 1 kHz is outside the 20 m band.

Two definitions make all of this precise. The **necessary bandwidth** is the width of a frequency band outside of which the signal's mean power falls at least 26 dB below its mean power within the band. The **carrier frequency** of your SSB emission is the suppressed carrier's frequency — the number on the dial. New words: necessary bandwidth, carrier frequency, suppressed carrier, emission segment. This section covers the first four questions of pool group E1A, E1A01 through E1A04.

### The special bands: 60, 2200, and 630 meters

The 60 meter band gets its own briefing because it changed after the pool was written, and this book teaches it as it exists today. Under the FCC's WRC-15 order (91 FR 1430, effective January 14, 2026), the band has a two-part structure. Part one is a contiguous segment, 5351.5–5366.5 kHz, which you may use anywhere inside at up to 9.15 W ERP. Part two is four discrete channels — 5332, 5348, 5373, and 5405 kHz — each good for up to 100 W ERP. The old 5358.5 kHz channel is gone as a discrete assignment; it lies inside the new segment. Phone is upper sideband, a CW carrier is set to the channel center, and no emission anywhere on the band may exceed 2.8 kHz of bandwidth. If an older study guide describes 60 meters as five channels at a flat 100 W ERP, that rule is dead.

Both of the pool's surviving 60 meter questions remain literally correct under the current text, so drill the keyed answers exactly as published: E1A06 keys the CW transmit frequency at the center frequency of the channel, and E1C01 keys the maximum data bandwidth at 2.8 kHz. What moved is the citation. The channel and CW rules now live at §97.303(h)(3) and the two power limits at §97.313(i), while E1A06's printed reference line still shows the superseded [97.303(h)(1)] — preserved as published, never repaired.

The two lowest amateur bands, 2200 meters (135.7–137.8 kHz) and 630 meters (472–479 kHz), are fixed-location bands with radiated-power limits rather than transmitter-power limits. On 2200 meters the cap is 1 W EIRP (E1A07). On 630 meters it is 5 W EIRP — except that in Alaska, stations within 800 kilometers of the Russian Federation are held to 1 W EIRP (E1A09). The choices arrive as four-rung ladders — 50 W PEP, 100 W PEP, 1 W EIRP, 5 W EIRP — and the two EIRP rungs are cross-paired between the questions, so pin the pairing: 2200 is 1, 630 is 5. Antennas on both bands must not exceed 60 meters in height above ground (§97.15(c)).

> **Worked example:** EIRP is effective isotropic radiated power — your transmitter's output multiplied by your antenna's gain over an isotropic radiator, so antenna gain counts against you. On 630 meters, suppose your antenna has 3 dB of gain, a factor of 2: the 5 W EIRP limit then means a transmitter output of at most 5 ÷ 2 = 2.5 W. A gain antenna buys you less transmitter power, not more free reach. (The transmitter faces its own ceiling on these bands — 1.5 kW PEP on 2200 meters and 500 W PEP on 630 meters — but the EIRP limit almost always bites first.)

Access to these two bands runs on notification, not licensing. Before your first transmission you notify the Utilities Telecom Council — the rule text's name for the utilities' coordination body — of your call sign, your intended band or bands, and the coordinates of your antenna's fixed location. Then you wait: after 30 days you may operate unless the UTC tells you your location is within 1 kilometer of power-line carrier systems on the same or overlapping frequencies (E1C03, E1C07). The electric utilities were on these frequencies first, running control signals over their own transmission lines, and the notification dance keeps your signal out of their receivers. One naming quirk to file away: the pool prints the body's name as the Utilities Technology Council while the current rule text reads Utilities Telecom Council — the same organization, two spellings, each preserved exactly where it is published.

One more fact on these bands defies HF intuition, and the pool knows it: phone and image emissions are authorized on the entire 630 meter band (E1C12) — no carved-out phone segment, the whole 472–479 kHz. New words: EIRP, channelized operation, UTC, power-line carrier (PLC), fixed location. This section covers E1A06, E1A07, and E1A09 from group E1A, and E1C01, E1C03, E1C07, and E1C12 from group E1C.

### Stations in motion and protected places

Part 97 has no special ship or aircraft endorsement. Any holder of an FCC amateur license — or a person authorized for alien reciprocal operation under §97.107 — may be the control operator of an amateur station aboard a US-documented vessel or craft, in domestic or international waters or airspace (E1A05, E1A11). The conditions sit elsewhere: the installation and operation must be approved by the master of the ship or the pilot in command of the aircraft (E1A10), and the amateur station must be separate from and independent of all other radio apparatus aboard, except that a common antenna may be shared with a voluntary ship radio installation. The license is yours; the vessel is not — hence the captain's approval.

Some places get special protection. A station within 1 mile — 1600 meters in the rule's phrasing — of an FCC monitoring facility must protect that facility from harmful interference (E1B03). The National Radio Quiet Zone is the area surrounding the National Radio Astronomy Observatory at Green Bank, West Virginia (E1B05), where radio discipline protects the telescope's receivers; the distractors — Laurel, White Sands, Cape Canaveral — are real radio sites attached to the wrong facility. And on 70 centimeters the amateur allocation is secondary to US government radiolocation, so if your 70 centimeter repeater interferes with a radiolocation system, the control operator must cease operation or change the repeater to mitigate the interference (E1B04). Secondary means you yield: cause no interference, accept what you receive.

Two rules govern the tower in your yard. Structures more than 200 feet (60.96 meters) above ground, and structures near or at a public-use airport at almost any height, may require you to notify the FAA and register the structure with the FCC under Part 17 (E1B06) — the FAA thinks in terms of height above average terrain and airport approach paths, not your antenna's performance. The second rule is PRB-1, the 1985 FCC declaratory ruling written into §97.15(b): state and local regulation of a station antenna structure must not preclude amateur service communications — it must reasonably accommodate them, with the minimum practicable regulation for the locality's legitimate purpose (E1B07, E1B11). Pin the boundary the pool loves to test: PRB-1 binds state and local zoning only. Homeowners' associations are private contracts, and PRB-1 does not reach them — the classic trap answer.

One last interference rule carries surprising mercy. If your station causes general interference to domestic broadcast reception when receivers of good engineering design are used, the FCC's remedy is not a shutdown: the station shall not be operated from 8 p.m. to 10:30 p.m. local time, plus Sunday from 10:30 a.m. until 1 p.m., on the frequency or frequencies used when the interference is created (E1B08). Quiet hours on the offending frequencies — the rest of your operating continues. New words: control operator, harmful interference, National Radio Quiet Zone, PRB-1, reasonable accommodation, HAAT. This section covers E1A05, E1A10, and E1A11 from group E1A, and E1B03 through E1B08 plus E1B11 from group E1B.

### RACES and emergency operation

RACES — the Radio Amateur Civil Emergency Service — is a wartime-and-disaster structure with a two-key lock. Any FCC-licensed amateur station may operate under RACES rules provided the station is certified by the responsible civil defense organization for the area served, and the control operator is certified as enrolled in that organization (E1B09); the "club stations only" and "all but Technician" distractors misstate the certification requirement. In normal times a RACES station shares all the frequencies and emissions authorized to its control operator (E1B10) — only if the President's War Emergency Powers are invoked do RACES stations get confined to segments authorized under Part 214. And one line of emergency doctrine you already own from the General course, refreshed: when normal communication systems are not available, no provision of the rules prevents an amateur station from using any means of radiocommunication at its disposal in connection with the immediate safety of human life and the immediate protection of property (§97.403). New words: RACES, civil defense organization, War Emergency Powers. That finishes pool group E1B, with E1B09 and E1B10.

### Emission standards

Memorize this one cold: a spurious emission is an emission outside the necessary bandwidth of a transmission, the level of which may be reduced without affecting the information being transmitted (E1B01). Notice what the definition does not contain — no decibel figure. The distractor offering "40 dB below the fundamental" borrows a number from the numeric limits and pastes it into the definition; the definition is about reducibility without information loss.

**FACT:** A spurious emission is "an emission, or frequencies outside the necessary bandwidth of a transmission, the level of which may be reduced without affecting the information being transmitted" — the definition contains no dB figure.

The numeric limits live one section over. For transmitters installed after January 1, 2003 and operating below 30 MHz, the mean power of any spurious emission must be at least 43 dB below the mean power of the fundamental emission (E1C10 — the pool's choice A prints "- 43 dB" with a space after the minus, a published quirk preserved byte-exact wherever the choice is quoted). Between 30 and 225 MHz the limit tightens to at least 60 dB below the fundamental. Below 29 MHz, angle modulation — FM and PM — is narrowband-only in practice: no angle-modulated emission may have a modulation index greater than 1.0 at the highest modulating frequency (E1C09). And no non-phone emission may exceed the bandwidth of a communications-quality phone emission of the same modulation type, which is why about 3 kHz is an acceptable bandwidth for HF digital voice or slow-scan TV (E1B02).

**FACT:** For transmitters installed after January 1, 2003 and operating below 30 MHz, "the mean power of any spurious emission from a station transmitter or external RF power amplifier transmitting on a frequency below 30 MHz must be at least 43 dB below the mean power of the fundamental emission."

For RTTY and data, the rules speak in authorized bandwidth. On HF the authorized bandwidth is 2.8 kHz, using a specified digital code listed in §97.309(a). If you studied from older material you may remember a 300-baud symbol-rate cap — that rule was replaced by the bandwidth standard in December 2023, before this pool was written, and it survives today only on 2200 and 630 meters. Treat it as history, never as an exam fact. On VHF the limits are a 19.6 kilobaud symbol rate and a 20 kHz bandwidth; on UHF and above, 56 kilobauds and 100 kHz.

Spread spectrum gets its own miniature rulebook. It first appears in the emission table at the 1.25 meter row, so spread spectrum is authorized only on amateur frequencies above 222 MHz (E1F01 — the ladder runs 50/144/222/420 MHz, and 222 is the rung). It may never be used to obscure the meaning of a communication, and the transmitter output cap is 10 W PEP. New words: spurious emission, necessary bandwidth, mean power, modulation index, angle modulation, spread spectrum. This section covers E1B01 and E1B02 from group E1B, E1C09 and E1C10 from group E1C, and E1F01 from group E1F.

### International, reciprocal, and third-party

International contacts carry a content limit: transmissions to a different country, where permitted, are limited to communications incidental to the purposes of the amateur service and to remarks of a personal character (E1C02). Third-party traffic — passing messages on behalf of someone who is not a licensed operator — crosses a border only where the foreign administration has made arrangements with the United States to allow it, or for emergency and disaster relief (§97.115(a)(2)).

The reciprocal map has exactly two entries to memorize. CEPT — the European Conference of Postal and Telecommunications Administrations, under Recommendation T/R 61-01 — is the arrangement that lets a US amateur operate in many European countries, and vice versa (E1C11). The IARP, the International Amateur Radio Permit, does the same job for certain countries of the Americas (E1C04). CEPT points to Europe; IARP points to the Americas. Two of this cluster's questions — E1C04 and E1C06, the only two in the whole subelement — carry no citation tag at all, because the operating mechanics live outside Part 97.

For a US operator, the CEPT mechanics live in FCC Public Notice DA 16-1048, not in the rules. Your Extra grant — or a legacy Advanced grant — earns full CEPT radio amateur license privileges; a General grant earns CEPT Novice privileges only. Operating under it, you carry three documents: a copy of the public notice itself, proof of US citizenship, and evidence of your FCC license grant (E1C06). No "/CEPT" suffix gets appended to your call sign, and no local-language identification requirement exists — both are invented distractors.

{{fig:ch01-cept-carry}}

**FACT:** A US Amateur Extra or Advanced operator operating in a CEPT country must carry three documents: "a copy of this Public Notice, proof of U.S. citizenship, and evidence of the FCC license grant" — no "/CEPT" suffix and no local-language ID requirement exists.

One reciprocal relationship needs no paperwork at all. A Canadian licensee operating in the US works under the terms and conditions of their home license, capped at the control operator privileges of a US Amateur Extra (E1F02) — and the same ceiling applies to other reciprocal countries. New words: CEPT, IARP, reciprocal operating authority, third-party communications, DA 16-1048. This section covers E1C02, E1C04, E1C06, and E1C11 from group E1C, and E1F02 from group E1F.

### Special operations: control, space, telemetry, and telecommand

The pool plays a deliberate definition swap in this cluster, so learn the pair as a pair. Telemetry is a one-way transmission of measurements at a distance from the measuring instrument (E1D01) — telemetry measures. A space telecommand station transmits communications to initiate, modify, or terminate functions of a space station (E1D03) — telecommand commands. Each question's distractors contain the other's definition. And encryption — flatly prohibited almost everywhere in the amateur service — has exactly one lawful home: a telecommand station may transmit special codes intended to obscure the meaning of telecommand messages to a station in space operation (E1D02). Not mesh backbones, not repeater links: space telecommand only.

A space station is an amateur station located more than 50 km above the Earth's surface, and it carries obligations: it must be capable of ceasing transmissions by telecommand whenever the FCC orders it. Its authorized frequencies form a list the pool drills three ways. On HF, space stations are authorized on the 40, 20, 15, and 10 meter bands (E1D07) — the WARC bands are excluded, which kills the "30, 17, and 10 meters" distractor. The only VHF band on the list is 2 meters (E1D08 — "6 meters and 2 meters" feels right to a VHF operator and is wrong). On UHF, the list is 70 centimeters and 13 centimeters (E1D09 — 33 centimeters is not on it). Eligibility is pinned just as tightly: any amateur station may be an Earth station, within the control operator's privileges (E1D11), and any amateur station the space-station licensee designates may serve as that space station's telecommand station (E1D10) — no AMSAT course, no ITU designation; those approving bodies are invented. Controlling a model craft by telecommand caps the transmitter at 1 W (E1D06). And the one-way club — the stations that may transmit one-way communications — has three members: space stations, beacon stations, and telecommand stations (E1D12).

A second set of rules covers telecommanded stations on or within 50 km of the Earth's surface — remote bases, high-altitude balloons, anything that is not in space. Such a station posts all three of the listed items: a photocopy of the station license, a label with the licensee's name, address, and telephone number, and a label naming at least one designated control operator (E1D05 — one of the pool's legitimate "all these choices are correct" answers). If the control link fails, provisions must limit transmissions to no more than 3 minutes (E1C08). If the control link is radio, it runs through an auxiliary station — and an auxiliary station's control operator may hold Technician or higher (E1F10; "General or higher" is the trap). A balloon-borne telemetry station flies within 50 km of Earth, so it is not a space station, and it must identify with its call sign (E1D04).

Two rules close out the cluster. A station under automatic control may transmit third-party communications only in RTTY or data emissions (E1C05). And in a message-forwarding system, accountability for a rule-violating message belongs to the control operator of the originating station — a station that forwards it inadvertently is not accountable (E1A08). One dated-rule caution, not tested but worth knowing: post-mission disposal for a low-Earth-orbit space station is now required as soon as practicable and no later than 5 years after the mission ends, so any older reference that says 25 years is pre-2024 text. New words: telemetry, telecommand, space telecommand station, Earth station, space station, beacon station, one-way communication, auxiliary station, automatic control, remote control, model craft. This section covers E1C05 and E1C08 from group E1C, all twelve questions of group E1D, E1A08 from group E1A, and E1F10 from group E1F.

### The volunteer examiner system

You have met this system from the candidate's chair; the Extra exam makes you recite its org chart. The flow is candidate → VE team → VEC → FCC. A Volunteer Examiner Coordinator is an organization that has entered into an agreement with the FCC to coordinate, prepare, and administer examinations (E1E03). The question pools — including the one this book is built on — are maintained by the VECs cooperating together, not by the FCC and not by the ARRL (E1E02); the ARRL runs a VEC, but it is a VEC, not the VEC. Accreditation is a VEC confirming that a VE applicant meets the FCC's requirements to serve as an examiner (E1E04). Element 4 itself is 50 questions drawn one per pool group, and the minimum passing score is 37 of 50.

In the session room, responsibility sits with the team. Each administering VE is responsible for the proper conduct and necessary supervision of the examination (E1E06), and every administering VE observes each examinee throughout. A candidate who fails to comply with the examiners' instructions has their examination terminated immediately (E1E07) — no warning shot required. And a VE may not administer an examination to the relatives listed in the rules — spouse, children, grandchildren, parents, grandparents, siblings, aunts, uncles, nieces, nephews, and the step- and in-law versions (E1E08). Employees and friends are fine.

The paperwork path is one-way. A failing candidate gets the application document back, along with the grade (E1E05). After a pass, three VEs certify that the examinee is qualified for the license grant and that they have complied with the administering requirements (E1E11), and the application document goes to the coordinating VEC according to that VEC's instructions (E1E10) — VEs never issue licenses and never file with the FCC directly. (Both questions' printed citation tags, [97.509(m)] and [97.509(i)], are the current published form after the pool's first errata.) Money is equally narrow: VEs and VECs may be reimbursed for out-of-pocket expenses of preparing, processing, administering, and coordinating an examination — teaching a course and writing study materials do not count (E1E01). Fraudulent administration or certification costs the VE their amateur station license grant, revoked, and their amateur operator license grant, suspended (E1E09); the fine and prison distractors are criminal penalties that do not apply here. The prize for knowing all of this: an Extra-class VE may administer all three elements, Technician through Extra. New words: VE, VEC, accreditation, administering VE, CSCE, examination element. That covers all eleven questions of pool group E1E.

### Prohibited communications, amplifiers, Line A, and the STA

The business-traffic rule has no dollar threshold. An amateur station may send a message to a business when neither the amateur nor their employer has a pecuniary interest in the communications (E1F07) — the distractors invent thresholds of 25 and 50 dollars that appear nowhere in the rules. Communications transmitted for hire or for material compensation, direct or indirect, paid or promised, are prohibited except as the rules specifically provide (E1F08). And messages encoded to obscure their meaning are barred even when they travel over an amateur mesh network (E1F09) — third-party traffic and ordinary email may cross a mesh; hidden meanings may not.

External RF power amplifiers carry FCC certification rules with one amateur-shaped hole: certification is not required for an amplifier constructed or modified by an amateur radio operator for use at an amateur station, so a dealer may sell you that amplifier without a certification grant (E1F03). When certification does apply, the amplifier must satisfy the spurious-emission standards while operated at the lesser of 1500 watts or its full output power (E1F11) — not at rated maximum, not at some rounder number.

Line A is a geography fact with an interference purpose. It runs roughly parallel to and south of the US–Canada border (E1F04), beginning at Aberdeen, Washington, and arcing east along the 48th parallel through the southernmost point of Duluth. North of Line A, no amateur station may transmit in the 420–430 MHz segment (E1F05) — the restriction protects Canadian systems across the border.

STA stands for Special Temporary Authority, and the pool keys it to a purpose: the FCC might issue one to an amateur station to provide for experimental amateur communications (E1F06). One honest footnote so you are not surprised later: the STA mechanism lives in §1.931 of the FCC's general rules, which never mentions the amateur service — it is the Commission's generic temporary-authority tool, and the pool's answer reflects FCC practice rather than any amateur-specific rule text. New words: pecuniary interest, external RF power amplifier, FCC certification, Line A, STA, mesh network. This section covers E1F03 through E1F09 and E1F11 from group E1F — and with it, all 68 questions of subelement E1.

### Exam Focus

This chapter covers pool subelement E1 (Commission's Rules) — groups E1A through E1F, 68 of the pool's 599 questions, worth 6 of the 50 questions on your exam: one from each group. Here are nine representative questions, quoted exactly as the pool prints them.

> **E1A01** Why is it not legal to transmit a 3 kHz bandwidth USB signal with a carrier frequency of 14.348 MHz?
> A. USB is not used on 20-meter phone
> B. The lower 1 kHz of the signal is outside the 20-meter band
> C. 14.348 MHz is outside the 20-meter band
> D. The upper 1 kHz of the signal is outside the 20-meter band
> **Answer: D** — a USB signal occupies the carrier up to carrier plus bandwidth, so the signal reaches 14.351 MHz and the 20 meter band ends at 14.350 MHz.

> **E1A06** What is the required transmit frequency of a CW signal for channelized 60 meter operation?
> A. At the lowest frequency of the channel
> B. At the center frequency of the channel
> C. At the highest frequency of the channel
> D. On any frequency where the signal’s sidebands are within the channel
> **Answer: B** — channelized 60 meter CW sits at the channel center — the rule now lives at §97.303(h)(3), and this question's printed [97.303(h)(1)] cite is the superseded one.

> **E1B07** To what type of regulations does PRB-1 apply?
> A. Homeowners associations
> B. FAA tower height limits
> C. State and local zoning
> D. Use of wireless devices in vehicles
> **Answer: C** — PRB-1 binds state and local zoning only; homeowners' associations are private contracts it never reaches.

> **E1C03** How long must an operator wait after filing a notification with the Utilities Technology Council (UTC) before operating on the 2200-meter or 630-meter band?
> A. Operators must not operate until approval is received
> B. Operators may operate after 30 days, providing they have not been told that their station is within 1 kilometer of PLC systems using those frequencies
> C. Operators may not operate until a test signal has been transmitted in coordination with the local power company
> D. Operations may commence immediately, and may continue unless interference is reported by the UTC
> **Answer: B** — notify, then wait 30 days — you may operate unless the UTC reports your station within 1 kilometer of PLC systems on those frequencies.

> **E1C06** Which of the following is required in order to operate in accordance with CEPT rules in foreign countries where permitted?
> A. You must identify in the official language of the country in which you are operating
> B. The US embassy must approve of your operation
> C. You must have a copy of FCC Public Notice DA 16-1048
> D. You must append "/CEPT" to your call sign
> **Answer: C** — the public notice is one of three documents you carry, with proof of US citizenship and evidence of your license grant — no suffix, no embassy, no language rule.

> **E1D02** Which of the following may transmit encrypted messages?
> A. Telecommand signals to terrestrial repeaters
> B. Telecommand signals from a space telecommand station
> C. Auxiliary relay links carrying repeater audio
> D. Mesh network backbone nodes
> **Answer: B** — space telecommand is the amateur service's one lawful home for encryption; repeaters, auxiliary links, and mesh nodes may not obscure meaning.

> **E1D07** Which of the following HF amateur bands include allocations for space stations?
> A. 40 meters, 20 meters, 15 meters, and 10 meters
> B. 30 meters, 17 meters, and 10 meters
> C. Only 10 meters
> D. Satellite operation is permitted on all HF bands
> **Answer: A** — §97.207(c) authorizes 40, 20, 15, and 10 meters for space stations; the WARC bands are excluded, and 30 meters is not on the list.

> **E1E02** Who is tasked by Part 97 with maintaining the pools of questions for all US amateur license examinations?
> A. The VEs
> B. The FCC
> C. The VECs
> D. The ARRL
> **Answer: C** — the VECs maintain the question pools cooperatively — the FCC administers the program, and the ARRL is one VEC among several.

> **E1F01** On what frequencies are spread spectrum transmissions permitted?
> A. Only on amateur frequencies above 50 MHz
> B. Only on amateur frequencies above 222 MHz
> C. Only on amateur frequencies above 420 MHz
> D. Only on amateur frequencies above 144 MHz
> **Answer: B** — spread spectrum first appears in the emission table at 222–225 MHz, so the ladder's rung is 222 MHz — never 6 meters or 2 meters.

### Key Takeaways

- Legality lives where the sideband energy falls, not where the dial sits: USB occupies carrier up to carrier + bandwidth, LSB occupies carrier − bandwidth up to carrier, and §97.307(b) confines all of it to the band or segment available to the control operator.

- The Extra-only slices sit on five bands only: 3.500–3.525 and 3.600–3.800 MHz, 7.000–7.025 and 7.125–7.175 MHz, 14.000–14.025 and 14.150–14.225 MHz, 21.000–21.025 and 21.200–21.275 MHz.

- 60 meters today is two parts: the contiguous 5351.5–5366.5 kHz segment at 9.15 W ERP plus four channels (5332, 5348, 5373, 5405 kHz) at 100 W ERP — CW at the channel center, 2.8 kHz maximum bandwidth everywhere on the band.

- 2200 meters is 1 W EIRP and 630 meters is 5 W EIRP (1 W in parts of Alaska); before operating, notify the UTC with your call sign, bands, and antenna coordinates, then wait 30 days unless told you are within 1 kilometer of PLC systems.

- Telemetry measures, telecommand commands — and only space telecommand may be encrypted; space stations are authorized on 40, 20, 15, and 10 meters, on 2 meters, and on 70 and 13 centimeters.

- The exam pipeline is candidate → VE team → VEC → FCC: the VECs maintain the question pools, three VEs certify a pass, and the paperwork goes to the coordinating VEC, never straight to the FCC.

- PRB-1 binds state and local zoning, never homeowners' associations; north of Line A, 420–430 MHz is off limits; spread spectrum lives above 222 MHz at 10 W PEP.
