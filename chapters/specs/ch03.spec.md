### Beat 1 — The wave itself (E3A04, E3A05, E3A10, E3A14, E3A06)
- EM anatomy: the E and H fields are at right angles to each other, and the wave propagates at right angles to both (E3A04, E3A05 — in time-phase but spatially perpendicular; the exam wants the geometry, not "90° out of phase"); speed through a medium is set by its index of refraction (E3A10); circular polarization = the field rotating as the wave advances (E3A14 — the bridge to satellite work in ch02).
- The daily rhythm: the MUF falls after dark, so you move to a lower-frequency band (E3A06) — HF operators plan the evening migration 20→40→80 m the same way VHF operators watch the evening tropo build.
- Vocabulary: electromagnetic wave, index of refraction, circular polarization, MUF.

### Beat 2 — EME geometry (E3A01, E3A02, E3A03)
- Any two stations with the moon mutually visible can attempt EME, up to about 12,000 miles apart measured along the Earth's surface (E3A01); about half the globe sees the moon at once.
- Least path loss at **perigee** (E3A03) — perigee vs apogee is worth about 2 dB round trip (WSJT-X reports it as DGRD); "full moon" and "apogee" are the mirror traps (bright ≠ close).
- Libration fading = the fluttery, irregular fading from multipath off the moon's rough, curved, wobbling face (E3A02); polarization rotation adds slow deep QSB on top. ch02 owns the operating discipline (Q65, time-synchronous sequences); this beat owns the geometry.
- Vocabulary: EME, perigee, apogee, libration fading, path loss.

### Beat 3 — Tropospheric ducts (E3A07, E3A11)
- A temperature inversion traps VHF/UHF energy and pipes it past the horizon: microwave ducts form over large bodies of water, typical range 100–300 miles (E3A07, E3A11). The operator's name for an opening is a "lift"; the weather-reading habit is watching high-pressure inversions settle over water paths.
- Figure: `{{fig:ch03-ducting}}`.
- Vocabulary: tropospheric ducting, temperature inversion.

### Beat 4 — Meteor scatter (E3A08, E3A09)
- Meteors ionize trails at **E-region** height (E3A08 — not the reflexive F2); the best scatter range is 28–148 MHz (E3A09), which is why 6 m and 2 m dominate the mode.
- The burst reality: modern digital meteor scatter works pings shorter than 0.1 s any day of the year out to ~1,300 miles; the rare long "blue whizzer" trails last several seconds; ping duration scales as 1/f², so 2 m pings run about an eighth as long as 6 m pings. This physics is exactly what MSK144 (ch02) was built to ride — brief pings on a timeline, the fast handshake fitting inside them.
- Figure: `{{fig:ch03-meteor-scatter}}`.
- Vocabulary: meteor scatter, E region, ping, ionized trail.

### Beat 5 — Aurora (E3A12, E3A13)
- Trigger: auroral propagation follows **severe geomagnetic storms** (E3A12) — the same A/K-index watching as Beat 7.
- Geometry: beam **north** first — both stations point at the auroral curtain, not at each other; swing either side of north to maximize.
- Why CW wins (E3A13): the moving curtain imposes fast erratic phase distortion — a pure carrier spreads into a buzz a few kHz wide and SSB turns to mush — so signals arrive phase-distorted and CW is the most usable mode. Distortion grows with frequency: 6 m is easier than 2 m, 2 m easier than 70 cm.
- Figure: `{{fig:ch03-aurora-geometry}}`.
- Vocabulary: auroral propagation, geomagnetic storm, auroral curtain.

### Beat 6 — The F-layer exotics: TEP, long path, chordal hop, Es, ground wave (E3B)
- Transequatorial propagation: stations about 2,000–3,000 miles apart on a path roughly **perpendicular** to the geomagnetic equator (E3B01 — across it, not along it), maximum range ~5,000 miles (E3B02 — the two numbers live in two different questions), most likely afternoon/early evening (E3B03); the mechanism is field-aligned ducting between the two equatorial-anomaly electron peaks, no intermediate ground reflection. First amateur TEP: August 1947, Mexico–Argentina on 50 MHz. Figure: `{{fig:ch03-transequatorial}}`.
- The ionosphere splits waves into independently propagating, elliptically polarized ordinary and extraordinary waves (E3B04).
- Darkness and long path: long-haul 160 m needs a path entirely in darkness — D-region absorption kills sunlit paths (E3B05); long path is most frequent on 40 and 20 m (E3B06); lower takeoff angle → longer hops (E3B07).
- Ground wave: vertical polarization only (E3B13), and maximum range decreases as frequency rises (E3B08) — why 160 m ground wave outreaches 10 m.
- Sporadic E: peaks around the solstices, especially the summer solstice (E3B09), and the pool keys it as a **daytime** mode — most likely between sunrise and sunset (E3B11 — see the wording law in §8: teach the pool answer with its solar-ionization mechanism; **never print "Es ends at sunset"**; evening-Es operating lore stays exactly that — lore you can mention, not the exam answer).
- Chordal hop: successive ionospheric refractions without an intermediate ground reflection (E3B12); skipping the lossy ground bounce means less loss than ordinary multi-hop (E3B10).
- Vocabulary: transequatorial propagation (TEP), geomagnetic equator, ordinary/extraordinary waves, long path, takeoff angle, ground wave, sporadic E (Es), chordal hop, multi-hop.

### Beat 7 — Space weather and the tools (E3C)
- The indices: a rising A- or K-index means increasing geomagnetic disturbance (E3C02); elevated indices hammer paths through the auroral oval with absorption (E3C03). Bz = the north–south component of the interplanetary magnetic field (E3C04); a **southward** Bz couples solar-wind energy into the magnetosphere → disturbed conditions (E3C05 — northward is the calm condition; the question tests the inversion).
- The event scales: flare classes ascend A→B→C→M→X with X strongest (E3C07); geomagnetic storms use the G scale with G5 extreme (E3C08) — two scales, two top letters; K-index runs 0–9. Sudden short-term HF blackouts come from solar flares (X-rays ionize the sunlit D region — E3C01); a sudden broadband rise in HF background noise likewise means a flare or CME impact (E3C12).
- The radio horizon: approximately 15% farther than the geographic horizon thanks to atmospheric refraction (E3C06) — the §5 worked example.
- The tools: reporting networks (PSK Reporter, WSPRNet, the Reverse Beacon Network) report digital-mode and CW sightings (E3C09) — the practical way to see where you're being heard right now; the 304A index measures solar UV at 304 angstroms, correlated to the solar flux index (E3C10); VOACAP models HF propagation (E3C11).
- Vocabulary: A-index, K-index, Bz, interplanetary magnetic field, flare class, CME, G scale, sudden ionospheric disturbance, radio horizon, 304A, VOACAP, reporting network.
