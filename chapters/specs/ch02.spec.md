### Beat 1 — How a satellite hears and talks back (E2A's transponder and orbit questions)
- The linear transponder as a bent-pipe translator: the uplink is mixed with a local oscillator and the difference product retransmitted (E2A03); being linear it relays any mode — FM, CW, SSB, SSTV, PSK, packet (E2A07). Figure: `{{fig:ch02-linear-transponder}}`.
- The inverting transponder's three simultaneous truths (E2A02 — all-of-the-above): USB↔LSB swap, band positions reverse, and uplink/downlink Doppler move in opposite directions and partially cancel — the operator on an inverting bird has less net tuning to do.
- Power discipline: downlink power is shared; excessive uplink ERP captures the transponder's AGC and steals downlink power from everyone (E2A08) — run uplink power so your downlink is about as strong as the satellite's beacon. This is a **linear-bird** question; on FM birds the failure mode is capture-effect hogging, a different etiquette — never merge the two (r5 watch 2).
- Orbits: an ascending pass travels south→north (E2A01); Keplerian elements are the orbit-defining parameter set tracking software eats, distributed as TLEs (E2A06); geostationary = appears fixed (E2A10 — QO-100 as the example, 25.9°E, 2.4 GHz up / 10 GHz down, footprint Brazil-to-Thailand: **the geostationary example, not a US operating opportunity** — say so explicitly, r5 watch 6). AOS/LOS vocabulary; circular polarization at the ground station mitigates spin modulation and Faraday rotation (E2A11). Store-and-forward = the orbiting mailbox (E2A12 — FalconSAT-3 flew it literally until it re-entered January 2023; every named bird is a time-sensitive fact, canon §7.11).
- Vocabulary: ascending/descending pass, Keplerian elements, TLE, LEO/HEO/geostationary, linear transponder, inverting transponder, AOS/LOS, spin modulation, Faraday rotation, store-and-forward.

### Beat 2 — The mode letters (E2A04, E2A05, E2A09)
- **The satellite-mode letter convention (this chapter's pinned law):** a satellite's "mode" is its uplink/downlink **band pair** — not FM-vs-SSB and not an orbit class (E2A04) — and the mode letters specify the uplink and downlink frequency ranges, **uplink letter first** (E2A05). The letters: V = 2 m, U = 70 cm, L = 23 cm, S = 13 cm (E2A09 pins L/S) — microwave letters, not "long/short." So "mode U/V" means 70 cm up, 2 m down.
- Where those bands live in the US band plan: 145.80–146.00 (OSCAR subband), 435–438 (satellite only), 1260–1270 (satellite uplinks, L), 2400–2410 (amateur satellite, S) — QO-100's 2.4 GHz uplink is the same 13 cm allocation seen from the other side.
- Doppler craft: LEO Doppler runs a few kHz on VHF, tens of kHz on UHF — about ±3.5 kHz on 2 m, ±10 kHz on 70 cm as **typical-LEO magnitudes (order-of-magnitude practice, never constants)**; tune the link with the bigger Doppler (the higher-frequency one): on U/v birds adjust the uplink, on V/u birds adjust the downlink.
- Figure: `{{fig:ch02-satellite-modes}}`.
- Vocabulary: mode designator, uplink, downlink, L band, S band, Doppler shift, ERP.

### Beat 3 — EME: the moon as a passive reflector (E2D03, E2D06, with E3A01–A03 cross-pointed)
- The concept: any two stations with the moon mutually visible, up to ~12,000 miles apart (E3A01 — ch03 owns the propagation physics; here the operating discipline). Round-trip delay ≈ 2.5 s; the lunar surface reflects only ~6% of arriving power; path loss ≈252 dB at 144 MHz, ≈271 dB at 1296 MHz (**band-specific figures, never generalized** — r5 watch 7); perigee vs apogee ≈ 2 dB round trip (the practice behind E3A03's "least path loss at perigee"); libration fading = fluttery multipath off the rough wobbling face (E3A02).
- The modes: JT65 was designed for EME (E2D05, multitone AFSK, 65 tones — E2D09); Q65 — which averages multiple receive cycles where JT65 doesn't (E2E07) — is effective for EME and fast-fading paths (E2D03). Digital wins because it decodes 10–15 dB below CW.
- The discipline the pool tests: EME runs on time-synchronous transmit/receive alternation (E2D06) — JT4/JT9/JT65 use 1-minute sequences (a minimal QSO takes 4–6 minutes, one station on odd UTC minutes, the other even); Q65 offers 15/30/60/120/300 s. The preamp belongs at the antenna, not in the shack (one sentence of station craft).
- Figure: `{{fig:ch02-eme-path}}`.
- Vocabulary: EME, perigee/apogee, libration fading, path loss, grid square, time-synchronous.

### Beat 4 — Weak-signal VHF+ and the contest/DX craft (E2C, E2D02)
- The geography: weak-signal segments at the band bottoms; national calling frequencies 50.125 / 144.200 / 222.100 / 432.100 — custom says raise a station there, then move up the band, which is why activity clusters *near*, not *on*, the calling frequency (E2C06). Beacons are the band-open alarm: 50.060–50.080, 144.275–144.300, 222.05–222.06, 432.30–432.40.
- Contest craft: VHF contests exchange **grid squares, not signal reports** (E2D02 — FT8/FT4 in a VHF contest exchanges grid in place of the SNR report); 30 m is contest-free by long convention, **not an FCC rule** — the pool's own wording is "generally excluded" (E2C03, canon §7.6: say "convention, not regulation" in the same breath). Split operation's three simultaneous purposes (E2C10 — all-of-the-above); in a pileup send your full call sign once or twice (E2C11); a QSL manager handles a DX station's confirmations (E2C05).
- The paper trail: ADIF = log interchange, Cabrillo = contest-log submission (E2C02, E2C07 — the distractors swap them), LoTW confirms contacts of every flavor (E2C08).
- Remote operation: a US remote-control station needs **no additional ID indicator** (E2C01 — teach it flatly, canon §7.6; many Generals remember otherwise); latency = delay between control action and the change in the transmitted signal (E2C12). Mesh: channels shared with unlicensed Part 15 services, routers with custom firmware (e.g., AREDN) (E2C04, E2C09).
- Vocabulary: weak-signal segment, calling frequency, split operation, pileup, QSL manager, LoTW, ADIF, Cabrillo, grid square, mesh network, AREDN, latency, rover.

### Beat 5 — Amateur television (E2B)
- NTSC fast-scan = 525 lines per frame, interlaced — odd lines one field, even the next (E2B02, E2B03); vestigial sideband = one full sideband plus a vestige of the other, cutting bandwidth while preserving low-frequency video fidelity (E2B05, E2B06); 70 cm ATV is watchable on analog cable-ready TVs via the shared cable-channel frequencies (E2B08).
- Analog SSTV: brightness is encoded by tone frequency (E2B10), color as sequential color lines (E2B04), the VIS code announces the mode so software auto-selects (E2B11 — it identifies the mode, not the station), specific tones trigger each new line (E2B12); DRM-protocol SSTV is receivable on an ordinary SSB receiver (E2B09).
- Digital TV: coding rate 3/4 means 25% of the transmitted data is FEC overhead (E2B01); amateur DVB-T uses QAM and QPSK (E2B07).
- Vocabulary: NTSC, interlaced scanning, field/frame, vestigial sideband, chroma, SSTV, VIS code, DRM, DVB-T, QAM, QPSK, coding rate, FEC.

### Beat 6 — Digital modes in the field: MSK144, APRS, JT65 (E2D)
- The mode→job map (the group's anchor): MSK144 → meteor scatter (E2D01 — 144-bit frames at 2000 baud = a complete message every 72 ms, decodes to −8 dB SNR in 2500 Hz; built because meteor pings are milliseconds long); Q65 → EME (E2D03); APRS → real-time balloon tracking (E2D04); JT65 → very-low-SNR decodes via multitone AFSK (E2D05, E2D09).
- APRS internals: runs on AX.25 (E2D07); beacons use Unnumbered Information (UI) frames — connectionless, no ack (E2D08); relays are packet digipeaters (E2D11); WIDE3-1 = three hops requested, one remaining — the counter decrements per digipeater (E2D10 — "3-1" is "of 3, 1 left," not stations or subcarriers).
- Vocabulary: MSK144, Q65, JT65, WSPR, APRS, AX.25, UI frame, digipeater, WIDE path, multitone AFSK.

### Beat 7 — Digital mechanics: how the modes actually move bits (E2E)
- FSK vs AFSK: below 30 MHz data emissions use FSK (E2E01); direct FSK shifts the transmitter VFO itself, AFSK feeds audio tones into an SSB rig (E2E11).
- WSJT-X internals: synchronized computer clocks are the mode (E2E02 — sync to UTC within ±1 s; a waterfall full of signals with zero decodes = check the clock); FT8 cycle = 15 s (E2E06), FT4 = 7.5 s built for contesting; FT4's "4" names its four-tone continuous-phase FSK (E2E03); FST4 = four-tone Gaussian FSK with variable T/R periods and seven tone spacings (E2E04 — all three clauses true); Q65 averages multiple receive cycles where JT65 doesn't (E2E07); WSPR is beacon-only, no keyboard-to-keyboard (E2E05 — 2-minute sequences, −31 dB SNR in 2500 Hz per the current guide; attribute any −28 figure to "earlier WSPR documentation"); FT8 has the narrowest bandwidth of the listed modes, ~50 Hz (E2E10 — the swapped option set with E2E13).
- Keyboard modes and throughput: PSK31's variable-length varicode gives common letters short codes (E2E09); PACTOR transfers binary files on HF (E2E08); PACTOR IV has the highest throughput of the listed modes in clear conditions (E2E13); ALE constantly scans a frequency list and activates on the designated call sign (E2E12).
- Vocabulary: FSK, AFSK, direct FSK, continuous phase, GFSK, varicode, WSPR beacon, ALE, PACTOR, throughput, T/R cycle.

## 5. Worked examples (≥1 `> **Worked example:**` blockquote)

- "Your own voice comes back from the Moon: the round trip is 2 × 384,400 km at 300,000 km/s ≈ 2.56 s — the ~2.5-second delay that makes EME QSOs feel like correspondence (WSJT-X's Echo mode exists to let you hear it). At 144 MHz the path costs ≈252 dB and the lunar surface returns only about 6% of what arrives — which is why EME stations run big antennas, low-noise preamps at the antenna, and digital modes that decode 10–15 dB below CW."
- "Why MSK144 wins the meteor game: a meteor ping lasts milliseconds. MSK144 packs a complete message into 144 bits at 2000 baud — 144 ÷ 2000 = 72 ms, repeated through a 15-second sequence until one ping carries it through (E2D01). Ping duration scales as 1/f², so 2 m pings run about an eighth as long as 6 m pings — hence the 20 ms short format for reports."
- APRS path arithmetic (Beat 6): "A WIDE3-1 path reads as 'of 3 hops requested, 1 remains' — each digipeater decrements the counter, so the packet dies after its third relay (E2D10)."
