## 8. Signals and Emissions at Extra Depth

Watch a busy band on a waterfall and you are looking at arithmetic: every trace on that display was a waveform first, and it became sound only at the far end of a long pipeline of numbers. This chapter walks that pipeline — how a waveform is described, how it is digitized, how the digitized stream is shaped into a modulation with a measurable bandwidth, and how the pool turns each step into definitions and arithmetic. Four of the fifty questions on your exam come from subelement E8 — forty-eight pool questions in four groups, one exam seat per group. In this chapter you'll learn waveform analysis and the conversion craft (Fourier, true RMS, dither, flash converters, peak envelope power), the modulation-index and deviation-ratio arithmetic behind four calculation questions, the digital-mode vocabulary with its three bandwidth rules of thumb, and the keying and spread-spectrum material — every number drilled with the pool's own figures.

### Waveforms and Conversion: Fourier, True RMS, PEP, and the ADC Craft

Book 3 treated a waveform as a shape on a scope: amplitude up the vertical axis, time along the horizontal. That picture has a name — the time domain, a signal described as its amplitude at different times (E8A03). The alternative view, the frequency domain, lists which sines add up to the shape, and the bridge between the two views is Fourier analysis — the technique that shows a square wave is made up of a sine wave and its odd harmonics (E8A01). That is not abstraction for its own sake. It is why a 1 kHz square wave carries energy at 3 kHz, 5 kHz, and 7 kHz; why hard keying edges spray bandwidth (you will meet them again as key clicks in E8D); and why filtering a square wave down to the fundamental leaves you holding a pure sine. Learn the two views as one idea seen from two sides and most of this group is already yours.

The same waveform realism pays off at the bench. An average-responding meter calibrated in RMS tells the truth for exactly one shape — the sine wave — and lies by a shape-dependent factor for everything else. A true-RMS calculating meter measures RMS for both sinusoidal and non-sinusoidal signals (E8A05), which is what you want when the thing under test is speech, noise, or a switching supply's sawtooth rather than a clean sine.

Peak envelope power is where Book 3's SSB refresher earns its keep. One line: single sideband removes the carrier and one sideband from AM, so the transmitted envelope is the voice itself, with no steady carrier to average against. PEP is the power at the envelope's crest, and the long-term average sits far below it: an unprocessed single-sideband phone signal runs a PEP-to-average power ratio of about 2.5 to 1 (E8A06). The "25 to 1" choice beside the keyed answer is the decimal slip. And the ratio is determined by speech characteristics (E8A07) — the pauses and peaks of a human voice — not by the degree of carrier suppression and not by amplifier gain, the two transmitter-flavored traps. Keep this in mind when you reach for the processor: heavy compression raises the average toward the peak, which is exactly why over-processed SSB sounds louder at the same PEP.

> **Worked example:** Take the 100 W PEP rig. With a PEP-to-average ratio of about 2.5:1, unprocessed speech averages roughly 100 ÷ 2.5 = 40 W over the syllables (E8A06) — and nothing in the transmitter sets that ratio; speech characteristics do (E8A07).

Now the conversion craft. Making a waveform digital takes two operations — sampling in time, quantizing in amplitude — and chapter 7 already covered the sampling half: Nyquist's rate, and the aliasing that punishes undersampling. This group tests the quantizing half and the converters themselves. Successive approximation is a type of analog-to-digital conversion (E8A02): the converter binary-searches the input against an internal DAC, settling one bit per clock, so an N-bit answer takes N clocks. At the other extreme sits the flash or direct converter — a comparator at every level boundary, every bit decided in a single step — and raw speed is the whole point: direct or flash conversion serves software-defined radios because its very high speed allows digitizing high frequencies (E8A08). On that question "all these choices are correct" is a decoy, not a gift; the low-power and out-of-sequence claims are invented virtues.

You have met both converters in the wild already, even if the names are new. The successive-approximation part is the one inside your antenna tuner's microcontroller and your bench multimeter — cheap, accurate, and fast enough for anything short of RF. The flash part lives inside every direct-sampling SDR, burning silicon and power to buy its one-step conversion. Between the two architectures sits quantization itself: rounding every sample to the nearest level, with the rounding error becoming quantization noise. With a small, steady signal that error marches in step with the waveform and turns into audible distortion — so converters add dither: a small amount of noise added to the input signal to reduce quantization noise (E8A04). Randomizing the error spreads it into a harmless hiss. The quality metric the pool wants for an ADC is total harmonic distortion (E8A11) — how much harmonic trash the converter adds to a clean test tone. The other three choices in that question are borrowed RF terms: peak envelope power belongs to your transmitter, reciprocal mixing to your receiver's local oscillator, and power factor to the AC mains.

The level count itself is a plain power of two, and the pool checks that you can count it.

> **Worked example:** An 8-bit converter encodes $2^8 = 256$ different input levels (E8A09). The "8" choice confuses the bit count with the level count — and the other distractors multiply or divide 256 by the input amplifier's gain, which changes the size of each step, never their number.

The trip back out of the digital world is the mirror image of the trip in. A DAC's output is a staircase, and the staircase carries copies of the signal up at the sampling frequency and beyond — spurious sampling artifacts — so a low-pass filter at the DAC's output removes them (E8A10), leaving the smooth waveform. The figure below gathers the sampling half of this craft into one picture, drawn for a 48 kHz sample rate: keep the signal under 24 kHz and the copies sit harmlessly far away; let content reach 30 kHz and it folds back across the Nyquist line, and a 30 kHz tone is heard as an 18 kHz alias. Band-limit before you sample, because no filter can remove an alias afterward.

**FACT:** Unprocessed SSB phone has a PEP-to-average power ratio of about 2.5:1, determined by speech characteristics.

### Modulation Index, Deviation Ratio, and Multiplexing

Book 3 taught FM as deviation: the louder the audio, the farther the carrier swings, and mic gain is the knob that sets the swing. The Extra exam turns that idea into two ratios. The modulation index is the ratio of frequency deviation to modulating signal frequency (E8B01) — deviation divided by the modulating frequency, in that order. A phase-modulated emission's index does not depend on the RF carrier frequency (E8B02): the index lives entirely in the modulation, not in where on the dial the carrier sits, so the same audio applied at 29 MHz or 146 MHz produces the same index. The deviation ratio is the same shape built from the worst case: the ratio of the maximum carrier frequency deviation to the highest audio modulating frequency (E8B09). The index describes one tone at one level; the deviation ratio describes the transmitter's full design envelope. Same formula shape, different scope — E8B01 versus E8B09 is the pair to pin.

The arithmetic is one division: $i = \Delta f / f_{mod}$. Here is the exam's favorite trap — all four calculation questions in this group are that same division with new numbers, and every one offers the inverted ratio as a distractor. Divide deviation by modulating frequency, never the reverse, and read each stem once for the deviation and once for the modulating frequency before you touch the calculator.

> **Worked example:** Your FM transmitter deviates 3 kHz on a 1 kHz tone: the index is 3000 ÷ 1000 = 3 (E8B03 — the "0.3" choice is the flip). At 6 kHz deviation on a 2 kHz tone the index is 6000 ÷ 2000 = 3 again (E8B04 — both index questions key to 3, which is itself a memory hook). Now take the maxima: 5 kHz maximum deviation against a 3 kHz highest modulating frequency gives a deviation ratio of 5 ÷ 3 = 1.67 (E8B05), and 7.5 ÷ 3.5 = 2.14 (E8B06). The distractor columns — 0.3, 0.6, 0.47 — are the same numbers turned upside down, padded with decimal slips like 0.167 and 0.214.

The figure below shows what the index means physically. As it grows, the carrier and each sideband pair trade power — their amplitudes are the Bessel functions of the index — so the carrier shrinks while more sideband pairs become significant. Both of the pool's index calculations land at index 3, where the carrier is down to about a quarter of its unmodulated strength and the significant sidebands reach about four times the modulating frequency on each side.

{{fig:ch08-modulation-index}}

> **The math, if you want it:** The carrier's amplitude is J₀(index) and the n-th sideband pair's is Jₙ(index), the Bessel functions of the first kind. At an index of 3, J₀ ≈ −0.26 — the minus sign is a phase reversal of the carrier — and the significant sidebands reach about (index + 1) times the modulating frequency on each side, so a 3 kHz deviation on a 1 kHz tone occupies roughly 8 kHz of spectrum. The pool never asks for a Bessel value — only for the ratio — but the curves explain why raising deviation at a fixed modulating frequency widens the occupied band.

Multiplexing closes the group — three ways to share a channel. OFDM is a digital modulation technique using subcarriers at frequencies chosen to avoid intersymbol interference (E8B08), and its amateur use is digital modes (E8B07): dozens of narrow subcarriers run side by side, each keyed slowly enough that its symbols are long compared to the channel's echoes — which is exactly what kills intersymbol interference — and each subcarrier peaks where its neighbors cross zero, so they overlap without mutual interference. FreeDV's digital voice is the amateur example you are most likely to have on the air, and the same technique backs the Wi-Fi and DSL in your house. Frequency-division multiplexing is the older, roomier scheme: dividing the transmitted signal into separate frequency bands that each carry a different data stream (E8B10) — your receiver's filter passing one station while rejecting the next is FDM seen from the receiving end. Time-division multiplexing shares the other axis: two or more signals are arranged to share discrete time slots of a data transmission (E8B11), the trick DMR uses to put two conversations on one repeater. Watch the distractor cross-dressing: E8B10's "jumps from band to band" is frequency hopping from group E8D, and E8B11's "discrete sub-carriers" is OFDM wearing TDM's clothes.

That is E8B — eleven questions, E8B01 through E8B11.

**FACT:** Modulation index = frequency deviation ÷ modulating frequency (3000/1000 = 3 and 6000/2000 = 3), deviation ratio = maximum carrier deviation ÷ highest modulating frequency (5 kHz/3 kHz = 1.67 and 7.5 kHz/3.5 kHz = 2.14), and a phase-modulated emission's index does not depend on the RF carrier frequency — every calculation question offers the inverted ratio as a distractor.

### Digital Signals: Modes, Codes, and Bandwidths

Chapter 7's SDR split the world into I and Q, the two channels 90 degrees apart. QAM is that same machinery pointed the other way: data is transmitted by modulating the amplitude of two carriers of the same frequency but 90 degrees out of phase (E8C01). Every transmitted symbol is one point in the I-Q plane — a particular amplitude at a particular phase — and the constellation diagram is the map of the legal points: it shows the possible phase and amplitude states for each symbol (E8C13). QPSK is the four-point map; step up to 16 or 64 points and each symbol carries more bits, at the price of shrinking the distance between points, and with it the noise a symbol can survive.

Two rate questions sit in this group, and the second is a gift. The symbol rate is the rate at which the waveform changes to convey information (E8C02) — how many symbol-times per second, regardless of how many bits ride on each symbol. And the relationship between symbol rate and baud is that they are the same (E8C11): two words, one quantity. The distractors that halve or double it are counting bits per symbol instead of symbols per second — a different number that depends on the code, which is exactly the remaining distractor.

Why digital signals care about gentleness is the square wave again: a waveform that changes state instantly owns a wide spectrum. The craft of narrowband digital is the craft of soft transitions. Change a PSK signal's phase at the zero crossing of the RF — the instant the envelope has no amplitude to radiate the jump — and the reason is to minimize bandwidth (E8C03). There, too, "all these choices are correct" is a decoy. PSK31 applies the same lesson in the time domain, minimizing its bandwidth with sinusoidal data pulses (E8C04) — each phase flip shaped like a raised cosine instead of a step. That shaping is how a live keyboard QSO fits into a signal narrower than most of the CW on the band.

CW's own bandwidth obeys a rule of thumb worth memorizing as a number, not just a shape. What sets the bandwidth of a transmitted CW signal is keying speed and the keying waveform's shape factor — its rise and fall time (E8C12) — never output power and never a modulation index; a kilowatt of CW is no wider than a watt at the same speed, and this group's "all these choices are correct" is a decoy as well. The pool's arithmetic is $BW \approx 4 \times WPM$.

> **Worked example:** At 13 WPM the rule gives 4 × 13 ≈ 52 Hz of occupied bandwidth (E8C05). The distractors are 13 Hz for anyone who skips the multiplication, and the halving-and-doubling pair 26 Hz and 104 Hz.

Frequency-shift keying widens with both of its ingredients — how far apart the mark and space sit, and how fast the symbols come. The pool's formula is $BW \approx (1.2 \times shift) + baud$: a little more than the shift, plus the symbol rate.

> **Worked example:** A 9600-baud ASCII FM transmission with a 4800 Hz shift: 1.2 × 4800 = 5760, plus 9600, gives 15.36 kHz (E8C07). Every distractor is a fragment of the recipe — the raw 4.8 kHz shift, the raw 9.6 kHz baud, and the 5.76 kHz product before the baud is added. At the opposite extreme, an FT8 signal occupies just 50 Hz (E8C06) — narrow enough that roughly fifty FT8 signals fit inside one SSB channel. That narrowness is chapter 4's noise-versus-bandwidth rule working for you: the narrower the bandwidth you must listen to, the less noise competes with the signal, which is a large part of how FT8 decodes stations you cannot hear.

Error handling sorts into two philosophies. Forward error correction spends extra bits so the receiver can repair damage alone; ARQ — automatic repeat request — spends a return path instead: if errors are detected, a retransmission is requested (E8C08). The distractors offer self-correcting polynomial codes and redundant substitution, which are FEC's job description, not ARQ's. Gray code is the quiet hero of encoders and dials: it allows only one bit to change between sequential code values (E8C09), so a value read mid-transition is off by at most one count, where plain binary flipping several bits at once can read wildly wrong. And the efficiency principle behind every modern mode: you can raise the data rate without more bandwidth by using a more efficient digital code (E8C10) — more bits per symbol, per the constellation lesson above.

Mesh networking closes the group with two questions that punish packet-radio habits. Mesh nodes are routers: each node has an Internet Protocol (IP) address (E8C14), and the network forms itself through discovery and link establishment protocols (E8C15) — nodes find their neighbors, negotiate links, and hand traffic along whatever path is up. The trap is "acting as store-and-forward digipeaters": digipeating is packet radio's fixed, dumb relay, while a mesh recomputes its routes continuously. If the answer sounds like 1995 packet, it is the wrong answer here.

That is E8C — fifteen questions, E8C01 through E8C15, the largest group in the subelement.

**FACT:** CW bandwidth is set by keying speed and rise/fall shape (not power or modulation index) — approximately 4 × WPM, so 13 WPM ≈ 52 Hz.

**FACT:** An FT8 signal's bandwidth is 50 Hz, and FSK/data bandwidth ≈ (1.2 × frequency shift) + baud — 1.2 × 4800 + 9600 = 15.36 kHz.

### Keying Defects, Spread Spectrum, and the Character Codes

E8C's lesson — soft transitions, narrow signal — has teeth in CW. Key a carrier with near-instant on and off edges and every edge is a little square wave, spraying odd-harmonic sidebands across kilohertz of band: the primary effect of extremely short rise or fall time on a CW signal is the generation of key clicks (E8D04). The clicks land on your neighbors, not on you, which is why the fix is shaping rather than filtering: the most common method of reducing key clicks is to increase the keying waveform's rise and fall times (E8D05). "Reduce" the times is the inverted trap, and the output-filter choices cannot help — by the time the clicks exist, they sit inside the passband any output filter must leave open. Modern radios expose the shaping as a rise-time menu item in milliseconds; this is the knob it turns.

Spread spectrum runs the opposite direction on purpose: instead of squeezing a signal narrow, you smear it wide and let the receiver gather it back. The interference resistance falls straight out of that gathering: signals not using the spread spectrum algorithm are suppressed in the receiver (E8D01). Correlating against the spreading code collapses the wanted signal back to its narrow original — that recovery is the process gain — while everything without the code stays spread and gets treated as noise. The two techniques must never blur. Direct sequence uses a high-speed binary bit stream to shift the phase of an RF carrier (E8D02) — many code chips per data bit, each chip flipping the carrier's phase — while frequency hopping rapidly varies the frequency of a transmitted signal according to a pseudorandom sequence (E8D03), transmitter and receiver stepping through the same prearranged pattern of channels. The sequence is pseudorandom rather than random because both ends must generate the identical pattern on cue; to anyone without it, the signal is indistinguishable from noise. The pool quotes each definition as the other's distractor, so pin the hook once: phase means direct sequence, frequency means hopping.

{{fig:ch08-spread-spectrum}}

The signal-quality trio after that is practical operating. Parity buys detection, not repair: including parity bits in ASCII characters lets some types of errors be detected (E8D06) — a single flipped bit shows up as a wrong count of ones, but nothing in the parity bit says where the error lives. AFSK overmodulation is the sound-card disease: the common cause is excessive transmit audio levels (E8D07) — the audio drive high enough that the transmitter clips the tones — and the distortion it makes is evaluated as intermodulation distortion, spurious products between the tones and their harmonics (E8D08). PSK operators read each other's idle IMD the way CW operators read a fist: the acceptable maximum IMD level for an idling PSK signal is about −30 dB (E8D09). The answer is negative — the +5, +10, and +15 dB choices are decoys for anyone who has never watched an IMD reading.

The character codes close the subelement, and the question is a two-column comparison. Baudot, the RTTY code, carries 5 data bits per character — thirty-two code points, not enough for letters and figures together — so it reserves 2 characters as letters/figures shift codes, while ASCII uses 7 or 8 bits with no shift codes at all (E8D10). The 4-, 6-, and 7-bit distractors surround the keyed 5. A Baudot receiver latches into whichever shift case it last saw, which is why a lost shift character turns a string of RYRY into 4545 on the screen. And ASCII's named advantage is one you use every day: it is possible to transmit both uppercase and lowercase text (E8D11) — the full alphabet in both cases, no shift gymnastics.

That is E8D — eleven questions, E8D01 through E8D11, and the end of the subelement.

**FACT:** Spread-spectrum receivers suppress signals not using the spreading algorithm (hence the interference resistance): direct sequence uses a high-speed binary bit stream to shift the phase of an RF carrier, and frequency hopping rapidly varies the carrier frequency per a pseudorandom sequence — "phase" = direct sequence, "frequency" = hopping.

### Exam Focus

Subelement E8 — Signals and Emissions, groups E8A through E8D — puts 48 questions in the pool, and your 50-question exam draws 4 of them, one per group. The ten below span all four groups: the PEP ratio and the ADC level count, every calculation pattern in the subelement (note that E8B03 and E8B04 both key to 3, and E8B04's stem carries the pool's published interior double space, quoted exactly as printed), the CW and FSK bandwidth numbers, the symbol-rate identity, the swapped spread-spectrum definitions, and the IMD limit whose answer is the negative one.

> **E8A06** What is the approximate ratio of PEP-to-average power in an unprocessed single-sideband phone signal?
> A. 2.5 to 1
> B. 25 to 1
> C. 1 to 1
> D. 13 to 1
> **Answer: A** — About 2.5:1, and speech characteristics set the ratio; 25 to 1 is the decimal slip.

> **E8A09** How many different input levels can be encoded by an analog-to-digital converter with 8-bit resolution?
> A. 8
> B. 8 multiplied by the gain of the input amplifier
> C. 256 divided by the gain of the input amplifier
> D. 256
> **Answer: D** — 2⁸ = 256 levels; the "8" choice confuses the bit count with the level count.

> **E8B03** What is the modulation index of an FM phone signal having a maximum frequency deviation of 3000 Hz either side of the carrier frequency if the highest modulating frequency is 1000 Hz?
> A. 3
> B. 0.3
> C. 6
> D. 0.6
> **Answer: A** — Index = deviation ÷ modulating frequency = 3000 ÷ 1000 = 3; 0.3 is the inverted ratio.

> **E8B04** What is the modulation index of an FM phone signal having a maximum carrier deviation of plus or minus 6 kHz if the highest modulating frequency  is 2 kHz?
> A. 0.3
> B. 3
> C. 0.6
> D. 6
> **Answer: B** — The same division, 6000 ÷ 2000 = 3 — both index questions key to 3, and this stem's interior double space is printed exactly as the pool publishes it.

> **E8B05** What is the deviation ratio of an FM phone signal having a maximum frequency swing of plus or minus 5 kHz if the highest modulation frequency is 3 kHz?
> A. 6
> B. 0.167
> C. 0.6
> D. 1.67
> **Answer: D** — Deviation ratio uses the maxima: 5 kHz ÷ 3 kHz = 1.67; 0.6 is the flip.

> **E8C05** What is the approximate bandwidth of a 13-WPM International Morse Code transmission?
> A. 13 Hz
> B. 26 Hz
> C. 52 Hz
> D. 104 Hz
> **Answer: C** — Bandwidth ≈ 4 × WPM: 4 × 13 ≈ 52 Hz; 26 and 104 are the halving-and-doubling traps.

> **E8C07** What is the bandwidth of a 4,800-Hz frequency shift, 9,600-baud ASCII FM transmission?
> A. 15.36 kHz
> B. 9.6 kHz
> C. 4.8 kHz
> D. 5.76 kHz
> **Answer: A** — (1.2 × 4800) + 9600 = 5760 + 9600 = 15.36 kHz; the raw shift and baud values are the distractors.

> **E8C11** What is the relationship between symbol rate and baud?
> A. They are the same
> B. Baud is twice the symbol rate
> C. Baud rate is half the symbol rate
> D. The relationship depends on the specific code used
> **Answer: A** — They are the same — the twice-and-half choices are counting bits per symbol, not symbols.

> **E8D02** What spread spectrum communications technique uses a high-speed binary bit stream to shift the phase of an RF carrier?
> A. Frequency hopping
> B. Direct sequence
> C. Binary phase-shift keying
> D. Phase compandored spread spectrum
> **Answer: B** — "Phase" means direct sequence; "frequency" is hopping's keyed definition, the classic swap.

> **E8D09** What is considered an acceptable maximum IMD level for an idling PSK signal?
> A. +5 dB
> B. +10 dB
> C. +15 dB
> D. -30 dB
> **Answer: D** — About −30 dB, and the answer is negative, against the +5/+10/+15 dB decoys.

### Key Takeaways

- Fourier analysis shows a square wave is a sine plus its odd harmonics; the time domain describes a signal as amplitude at different times, and a true-RMS meter measures sinusoidal and non-sinusoidal signals alike.

- The conversion craft: successive approximation is an A/D method, flash converters serve SDRs on raw speed, dither tames quantization noise, total harmonic distortion measures ADC quality, an 8-bit ADC encodes 2⁸ = 256 levels, and the DAC's output low-pass removes spurious sampling artifacts.

- Unprocessed SSB phone runs a PEP-to-average ratio of about 2.5:1 — 100 W PEP averaging about 40 W — and speech characteristics set the ratio, not carrier suppression or amplifier gain.

- Modulation index = deviation ÷ modulating frequency, and both pool index questions key to 3 (3000/1000 and 6000/2000); deviation ratio is the same division built from the maxima — 5/3 = 1.67 and 7.5/3.5 = 2.14 — and every one of the four offers the inverted ratio as a distractor.

- Symbol rate and baud are the same thing; CW occupies about 4 × WPM (13 WPM → 52 Hz), FT8 just 50 Hz, and FSK data about (1.2 × shift) + baud — 15.36 kHz for a 4800 Hz shift at 9600 baud.

- Key clicks come from extremely short rise and fall times, and the cure is longer rise and fall times; in spread spectrum, "phase" means direct sequence and "frequency" means hopping, with the receiver suppressing every signal not using the spreading algorithm.

- An idling PSK signal's acceptable maximum IMD is about −30 dB; Baudot carries 5 data bits per character with 2 letters/figures shift codes, while ASCII uses 7 or 8 bits with no shifts and carries both upper- and lowercase.
