## 10. RF Exposure and Safety at Extra Depth

The smallest subelement on your exam — a single group, twelve questions, one guaranteed seat out of fifty — guards the only two things in this hobby that can actually hurt you: the RF your antenna pours into the neighborhood and the tower you climb to hang it. Book 3 gave you the safety basics — grounding, bonding, and the idea that RF exposure limits exist. The Extra exam asks you to run those limits the way the FCC writes them. In this chapter you'll learn what the MPE limits protect and where they bite hardest, who must perform an RF exposure evaluation and who is exempt, the sharing rule at multi-transmitter sites, and the three tower-climbing rules that close the group. Every one of the twelve questions is conceptual — the pool requires no exposure calculation at all — so the one piece of math in this chapter is flagged plainly as enrichment: it exists to make the word "evaluation" concrete, not to be memorized.

### What the MPE Limits Protect

Start at the dirt. Book 3's grounding chapter boils down to one line — every conductor that might carry a dangerous voltage needs a solid, low-impedance path to earth — and the pool asks what that path is primarily for. The primary function of an external earth connection or ground rod is lightning charge dissipation (E0A01). The distractors all name other devices' jobs: preventing static build-up on power lines, reducing RF current flow between pieces of equipment (that is bonding's job — the short, wide strap between chassis), and protecting the breaker panel from surges (the surge protector's). The rod exists to give a lightning strike somewhere to go that is not your shack, your feedline, or you.

The exposure rules themselves sort the world into two populations. The controlled limits protect people who are aware of the exposure and can manage it — you, and your own household once you have briefed them on the station. The uncontrolled limits protect the general population: people who neither know about your transmissions nor can do anything about them. A neighbor's home is general-population territory, so when you evaluate the signals from your station there, you must ensure they are less than the uncontrolled maximum permissible exposure (MPE) limits (E0A02). The four choices attack on two axes at once — they swap "uncontrolled" for "controlled," and they swap "exposure" for "emission." MPE limits what a body may absorb, not what a transmitter may emit; read both words before you mark the answer.

**FACT:** A neighbor's home is general-population territory, so the station's signals there must meet the uncontrolled MPE limits.

How much absorption a given field produces depends on frequency, because the human body is a lossy conductor of a particular size, and somewhere in the low-VHF range it picks up energy most efficiently — a body-resonance effect, the same physics that makes a quarter-wave whip work. The limits tighten to match: FCC human-body RF exposure limits are most restrictive over 30–300 MHz (E0A03). The distractors are the neighboring decades — 300 kHz to 3 MHz, 3 to 30 MHz, 300 to 3000 MHz — so pin the one decade where the neighbor's body is, electrically speaking, nearly a resonant antenna.

Above the resonance region the limits relax with frequency, but a different hazard grows to take its place: gain. The hazard created by operating at microwave frequencies is that the high-gain antennas commonly used can result in high exposure levels (E0A05). A dish or a long Yagi concentrates everything the transmitter makes into a narrow beam, and power that would be harmless spread over a sphere is not harmless in the beam's core — the working rule at microwave frequencies is to stay out of the main lobe, especially at eye level on a tower. Microwaves are not ionizing radiation; that first distractor is false at every amateur frequency, and the choices about wave velocity and antenna-structure joints exist to catch guesses.

Below 300 MHz the rules also set separate limits for the electric (E) and magnetic (H) fields, and the pool asks why. Every clause in its answer list is true: the body reacts to electromagnetic radiation from both fields, the two fields' intensity peaks can occur at different locations, and ground reflections and scattering make the field strength vary with location — so "all these choices are correct" is honestly keyed (E0A06). The mechanism is near-field physics. In the far field, E and H travel locked in a fixed ratio, and measuring one tells you the other. Close to an antenna — the near field, which at HF can extend farther than your lot line — one field can dominate the other, and their maxima need not coincide. This is one of the sixteen places in the pool where "all these choices are correct" is the keyed answer rather than a decoy; learn the content, not the pattern.

The measuring stick under all of it is SAR, the specific absorption rate: the rate at which RF energy is absorbed by the body (E0A08), expressed in practice in watts per kilogram of tissue. Every MPE limit in the tables exists to keep SAR below the thresholds where heating becomes harm — the limits are written as field strength and power density because those are what you can measure at a lot line, but SAR is what they protect.

One honesty note about citations. These exposure answers rest on Part 1 and Part 2 text — §§1.1307, 1.1310, 2.1091, and 2.1093 — and on OET Bulletin 65, all of it pointed to by §97.13(c)(1)'s requirement that amateur stations meet the MPE limits. None of it lives in Part 97's own text, and this book will not invent a Part 97 citation for it. New words: maximum permissible exposure (MPE), controlled and uncontrolled exposure limits, E-field and H-field limits, specific absorption rate (SAR).

**FACT:** FCC human-body RF exposure (MPE) limits are most restrictive over 30–300 MHz — the body-resonance region.

### Who Must Evaluate, Who Is Exempt

Every amateur station must meet the MPE limits; the regulatory question is whether you must sit down and perform an RF exposure evaluation to show it. The current rules exempt many lower-power setups by frequency and power, and the pool pins the two ends of that map. At one end, the exemption that exists is a date, not a power level: hand-held transceivers sold before May 3, 2021 are exempt from RF exposure evaluations (E0A09). The other choices — transceivers with less than 7 watts, antennas that radiate only in the near field, dishes under one meter — invent thresholds the rule does not contain. At the other end sits the band with no carve-out at all: on 80 meters an evaluation must always be performed (E0A10). Its distractors invent conditions the rule does not have — ERP under 10 watts, CW operation, transmitter output under 100 watts — and the keyed "always" beats every one of them.

Where several transmitters share a site — a Field Day operation, a contest multi-multi, a commercial repeater hill that also hosts your club's machine — the exposures add, and the rules assign the cleanup by formula. The operators and licensees of each transmitter that produces 5 percent or more of its MPE limit in an area where the total MPE limit is exceeded share responsibility for mitigating the over-exposure (E0A04). Twenty percent is the decoy, and the duty-cycle choices — 25 and 50 percent — belong to a different idea entirely. Five percent is a deliberately low bar: it pulls every meaningful contributor to the table, and below it your share of the problem is small enough that the rule leaves you out of the remediation. Mitigation in practice means the same few moves everywhere — less power, a higher or re-aimed antenna, a fence or a sign that keeps people out of the hot spot, or a schedule that keeps the transmitters from running together.

**FACT:** At a multi-transmitter site, every transmitter producing 5 percent or more of its MPE limit in an area where the total limit is exceeded shares responsibility for mitigation.

Now, what does an evaluation actually look like? No E0A question asks you to compute a thing — the exam wants the thresholds above, not arithmetic — but "perform an evaluation" stays abstract until you have watched one happen, so here is the shape of the exercise, flagged plainly: enrichment — not exam math. In the far field, your antenna's power spreads over the surface of an expanding sphere, and the power density at distance R is the radiated power divided by that sphere's area: $S = ERP / (4\pi R^2)$. ERP out the top, distance squared underneath — that is the whole engine. Two honest levers then lower what a body actually absorbs: duty cycle, because your transmitter is not on all the time, and time averaging, because the limits are judged against an average over a window rather than against a peak — 6 minutes for controlled exposure, 30 minutes for the uncontrolled limits that apply at your neighbor's lot line.

> **Worked example:** The MPE check, enrichment — not exam math. Your antenna system radiates 100 W ERP, and the neighbor's lot line is 10 m away. The far-field power density is S = ERP/(4πR²) = 100/(4π × 10²) ≈ 0.08 W/m² (0.008 mW/cm²) — already comfortably under the MPE limits, whose uncontrolled values bottom out at 2 W/m² (0.2 mW/cm²) across the 30–300 MHz body-resonance region (E0A03), twenty-five times above even this peak. Then the levers: conversational SSB at about 50% duty, with speech's own pauses on top, averaged over the 30-minute uncontrolled window, cuts the effective exposure further still. ERP out, distance squared in the denominator, duty and averaging applied after — that shape is what the FCC means by "evaluation" (E0A10).

> **Worked example:** The duty-cycle arithmetic, same flag: enrichment — not exam math. A 100 W PEP SSB transmission at 50% transmit duty within the averaging window presents the thermal load of a 50 W continuous source over that window, and ordinary speech adds its own roughly 20% duty inside the key-down time, so the true average falls further yet. Key-down modes get no such discount: RTTY at full power, or FT8 running continuous cycles, is a carrier for the window's purposes — one more reason an honest evaluation is mode-aware.

Two notes before we leave the math where it lies. First, "180/f²" and "f/1500" are just the limit curve's own description of how the body's coupling changes with frequency — you will never be asked to apply them, but knowing the curve dips hardest at VHF tells you why the 6-meter and 2-meter stations face the strictest limits. Second, the levers only work when the duty is real: a mode that runs key-down through the whole window averages no better than a carrier, which is why the evaluation is mode-aware in practice. New words: RF exposure evaluation, duty cycle, time averaging, power density, aggregate contribution.

**FACT:** Hand-held transceivers sold before May 3, 2021 are exempt from RF exposure evaluations, and on 80 meters an RF exposure evaluation must always be performed.

### Tower Work: The Climbing Rules That Save Lives

The other way this hobby hurts people is simpler and older than RF: gravity. Tower work kills by falls, and the pool's three climbing questions are the three rules that prevent them — worth one exam question among them, and worth memorizing even if you never leave the ground yourself, because the day you help on a tower crew you are part of the system.

The first rule has a name worth learning as a phrase: 100% tie-off means at least one lanyard is attached to the tower at all times (E0A07). The mechanics follow from the name. A proper climbing harness carries two lanyards; you climb clipped in on one, and before you move the other to a higher anchor, the first is still holding you. At no instant from ground to work position and back is the climber unhooked — not "attached except while passing this bracket," not ever. The distractors describe other disciplines entirely — securing loose ropes and guys, securing tools to the harness, and one joke answer about tying circuit breakers closed — and recognizing them as such is the whole question.

The second rule says what the lanyards attach to: tower legs (E0A11). The legs are the structural members, engineered for the loads of the tower itself. Rungs and climbing pegs are steps, rated for a boot rather than for the shock load of a falling body; the guy brackets belong to the guy system and can rotate; the antenna mast is not part of the tower's structure at all. Leg.

The third rule says where: a shock-absorbing lanyard anchors above the climber's head level (E0A12). The reason is fall arithmetic. A lanyard anchored at your waist lets you fall its full length below the anchor before it loads, and the shock absorber then has to arrest all of that; anchored overhead, the fall is short to begin with, the absorber deploys early, and you stay above the brackets and dishes you were climbing past. Attach high, fall far less.

One sentence of craft to close: the harness, the lanyards, and the discipline are a single system — the exam tests these three attachment facts, and they are also the three things an experienced climber checks before leaving the ground. And if the work ever exceeds your gear or your training, hiring the climb is not defeat; it is the most experienced decision on the site. New words: 100% tie-off, shock-absorbing lanyard, fall arrest.

**FACT:** 100% tie-off means at least one lanyard is attached to the tower at all times, lanyards attach to tower legs, and a shock-absorbing lanyard anchors above the climber's head level.

### Exam Focus

Subelement E0 — Safety — is one group, E0A, twelve questions in the pool, and your 50-question exam draws exactly 1 of them. The seven quoted below carry the group's high-value confusables: controlled versus uncontrolled, the most restrictive range, the 5-percent sharing rule, the all-true E- and H-field question, the exemption date, the 80-meter "always," and the lanyard attachment. The remaining five — the ground rod, the microwave hazard, 100% tie-off, SAR, and the shock-absorbing lanyard — are taught above and quoted in Appendix A.

> **E0A02** When evaluating RF exposure levels from your station at a neighbor’s home, what must you do?
> A. Ensure signals from your station are less than the controlled maximum permissible exposure (MPE) limits
> B. Ensure signals from your station are less than the uncontrolled maximum permissible exposure (MPE) limits
> C. Ensure signals from your station are less than the controlled maximum permissible emission (MPE) limits
> D. Ensure signals from your station are less than the uncontrolled maximum permissible emission (MPE) limits
> **Answer: B** — The neighbor's home is general-population territory, so the uncontrolled limits apply — and the word is "exposure," not "emission."

> **E0A03** Over what range of frequencies are the FCC human body RF exposure limits most restrictive?
> A. 300 kHz - 3 MHz
> B. 3 - 30 MHz
> C. 30 - 300 MHz
> D. 300 - 3000 MHz
> **Answer: C** — 30–300 MHz is the body-resonance region, where the limits sit at their tightest.

> **E0A04** When evaluating a site with multiple transmitters operating at the same time, the operators and licensees of which transmitters are responsible for mitigating over-exposure situations?
> A. Each transmitter that produces 20 percent or more of its MPE limit in areas where the total MPE limit is exceeded
> B. Each transmitter operating with a duty cycle greater than 25 percent
> C. Each transmitter that produces 5 percent or more of its MPE limit in areas where the total MPE limit is exceeded
> D. Each transmitter operating with a duty cycle greater than 50 percent
> **Answer: C** — The sharing threshold is 5 percent of the MPE limit in an over-limit area; 20 percent is the decoy.

> **E0A06** Why are there separate electric (E) and magnetic (H) MPE limits at frequencies below 300 MHz?
> A. The body reacts to electromagnetic radiation from both the E and H fields
> B. Ground reflections and scattering cause the field strength to vary with location
> C. E field and H field radiation intensity peaks can occur at different locations
> D. All these choices are correct
> **Answer: D** — The body reacts to both fields, their peaks can occur at different locations, and reflections vary the field with location — all true, so "all these choices are correct" is honestly keyed.

> **E0A09** Which of the following types of equipment are exempt from RF exposure evaluations?
> A. Transceivers with less than 7 watts of RF output
> B. Antennas that radiate only in the near field
> C. Hand-held transceivers sold before May 3, 2021
> D. Dish antennas less than one meter in diameter
> **Answer: C** — The exemption is a date, not a power threshold: hand-held transceivers sold before May 3, 2021.

> **E0A10** When must an RF exposure evaluation be performed on an amateur station operating on 80 meters?
> A. An evaluation must always be performed
> B. When the ERP of the station is less than 10 watts
> C. When the station’s operating mode is CW
> D. When the output power from the transmitter is less than 100 watts
> **Answer: A** — On 80 meters the evaluation must always be performed; the power and mode conditions in the other choices are invented.

> **E0A11** To what should lanyards be attached while climbing?
> A. Antenna mast
> B. Guy brackets
> C. Tower rungs
> D. Tower legs
> **Answer: D** — Lanyards attach to tower legs — the structural members — not rungs, guy brackets, or the mast.

### Key Takeaways

- The ground rod's primary job is lightning charge dissipation; bonding between chassis and surge protection at the panel are other devices' jobs.

- A neighbor's home is uncontrolled territory: your station's signals there must meet the uncontrolled MPE limits, and MPE is about exposure, never emission.

- MPE limits are most restrictive over 30–300 MHz — the body-resonance region — and at microwave frequencies the hazard is the high-gain antenna's concentrated beam.

- Below 300 MHz, separate E- and H-field limits exist because the body reacts to both fields and their peaks can occur in different places; SAR — the specific absorption rate — is the absorption measure underneath all the limits.

- At a multi-transmitter site, producing 5 percent or more of your MPE limit in an over-limit area makes you share the mitigation duty; 20 percent is a decoy.

- Hand-helds sold before May 3, 2021 are exempt from RF exposure evaluations, but on 80 meters an evaluation must always be performed — no power or mode carve-outs.

- Duty cycle and time averaging are the honest levers on exposure — the average, not the peak, is judged — and no E0A question requires the arithmetic, which appears in this chapter only as flagged enrichment.

- 100% tie-off means at least one lanyard attached at all times; lanyards attach to tower legs; a shock-absorbing lanyard anchors above the climber's head level.
