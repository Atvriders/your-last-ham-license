## 5. Electrical Principles: Complex Impedance and Resonance

For most of your radio life, "fifty ohms" has been a complete description — fifty-ohm coax, a fifty-ohm dummy load, fifty ohms at the antenna port, and Ohm's law to arbitrate the rest. The moment a capacitor or a coil joins the circuit, one number stops being enough. Opposition to alternating current splits into two parts that behave nothing alike: resistance, which burns power, and reactance, which borrows energy each cycle and hands it back. Describing the pair takes a second dimension, and this chapter teaches that dimension's arithmetic the way the exam tests it — as a plotting skill with real numbers, not a math course. In this chapter you'll learn resonance and Q, RC time constants, admittance and susceptance, phase arithmetic, rectangular and polar impedance notation, the Figure E5-1 impedance chart, real versus reactive power, and the reflection-coefficient math that ties a wattmeter to SWR — the forty-nine questions of subelement E5, the most mathematical subelement in the pool.

### Resonance: Where the Reactances Cancel

Book 3 gave you reactance and a first taste of resonance; here is the whole picture. An inductor's reactance climbs with frequency, $X_L = 2\pi fL$, while a capacitor's falls as X_C = 1/(2πfC). Somewhere, the two curves cross. At that one frequency — the resonant frequency — the inductive and capacitive reactances are equal in magnitude, and in a series circuit they cancel each other exactly. What is left is the resistance, standing alone.

That cancellation plays out as mirror images, and the pool tests both directions. In a series RLC circuit at resonance the impedance drops to approximately the circuit resistance (E5A03) — the minimum the circuit ever presents — so the line current peaks, and voltage and current fall into phase with each other (E5A08). A parallel RLC circuit inverts every one of those behaviors: the impedance rises to a maximum, the current drawn at the input falls to a minimum (E5A07), and yet inside the L–C loop the circulating current sloshing between coil and capacitor is at its maximum (E5A06). Keep those two currents straight — input minimum, circulating maximum, at the same resonance — because swapping them is the trap E5A06 and E5A07 are built around. One model note: E5A04 keys the parallel circuit's resonant impedance as "approximately equal to circuit resistance," which assumes the parallel-R model of the tank, the resistance drawn across the L and C rather than in series with them. Flag the assumption and answer as keyed; the exam is testing the model, not your preferred equivalent circuit.

The resonant frequency itself comes from one formula: f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)). Look at what is missing — R. Resistance decides how sharp a resonance is, never where it sits, and both of the pool's resonant-frequency questions print a resistor as bait.

> **Worked example:** Fifty microhenries and 40 picofarads: LC = 50×10⁻⁶ × 40×10⁻¹² = 2×10⁻¹⁵, so √(LC) = 4.472×10⁻⁸ and $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ = 3.56 MHz (E5A02). Swap in a 10 pF capacitor and the same arithmetic gives √(LC) = 2.236×10⁻⁸ and 7.12 MHz (E5A10). The resistor printed in each question — 22 Ω in one, 33 Ω in the other — does nothing. And if 22.36 MHz tempted you on the first one, that choice is 2π × f₀: the forgot-to-divide-by-2π slip, waiting there because the distractor writers know it happens.

Q — the quality factor — measures how much reactance a circuit stores relative to the resistance that damps it. The definitions split by topology: series Q is reactance over resistance, parallel Q is resistance over reactance (E5A09). Two consequences follow. First, loading: any Q you measure with the circuit connected to the outside world — the loaded Q — runs lower than the unloaded Q of the bare components, because the load is one more resistance doing the damping. Second, bandwidth: raising the Q of an impedance-matching circuit decreases its matching bandwidth (E5A05). High Q means a sharp, narrow resonance; low Q means a broad, forgiving one.

That trade-off has a formula, and it is one of the most useful in this book: the half-power bandwidth of a resonant circuit is BW = f₀/Q. Half power means the two frequencies where the response has fallen to the −3 dB points, and the formula says Q alone decides how wide the window is at a given center frequency.

{{fig:ch05-q-bandwidth}}

> **Worked example:** A circuit resonant at 7.1 MHz with a Q of 150 passes a window BW = f₀/Q = 7.1 MHz/150 = 47.3 kHz wide (E5A11). At 3.7 MHz with a Q of 118, the window is 3.7 MHz/118 = 31.4 kHz (E5A12). The formula runs backwards just as easily — measure a filter's half-power bandwidth, divide its center frequency by that width, and you have measured its Q. That is the antenna-analyzer trick from chapter 4, and the exam tests both directions.

Series resonance has one more property, and it is the one that arcs capacitors. At resonance the voltages across the inductor and the capacitor can each exceed the voltage applied to the whole circuit — resonance itself is the cause (E5A01) — and the magnification factor is approximately Q. Raising the Q of a series resonant circuit raises those internal voltages (E5A13).

> **Worked example:** Use E5A02's own tank. At 3.56 MHz the 50 µH coil has a reactance of about 1.12 kΩ, and against the printed 22 Ω resistor the series Q is 1118/22 ≈ 51. Apply 10 volts to the series circuit and each reactance sees roughly $V_L \approx Q \times V_{applied}$ — about 510 V across the coil, and 510 V across the capacitor. Fifty-one times the applied voltage from a 10 V source is why tank capacitors in high-Q amplifiers arc, why coil insulation matters, and why "internal voltages increase" is the keyed effect of raising series Q.

That is the resonance group: thirteen questions, E5A01 through E5A13.

### Time Constants, Admittance, and Phase

Some of E5's math is about how fast circuits move. Charge a capacitor through a resistor and the voltage climbs along an exponential curve; the yardstick is the time constant, τ = RC (for an inductor, τ = L/R). One time constant after you close the switch, the capacitor has reached 63.2% of the applied voltage; one time constant into a discharge, it holds 36.8% of its initial voltage (E5B01). Those two percentages are e in disguise — 1 − 1/e and 1/e — and they are worth knowing cold. By five time constants the capacitor sits at 99.3%, which every bench tradition calls fully charged.

> **The math, if you want it:** The charge curve is $V(t) = V_S(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$ and the discharge curve is V = V₀·e^(−t/τ). Set t = τ and the parentheses read 1 − 1/e = 0.632 and 1/e = 0.368 — the pool's two percentages are just the exponential sampled at one time constant. Five τ leaves less than 0.7% to go, which is where the "five time constants and you're done" rule comes from.

The exam's one calculation here punishes a specific slip, so build the habit now: simplify the circuit before you multiply.

> **Worked example:** E5B04 hands you two 220-microfarad capacitors and two 1-megohm resistors, all in parallel. Combine like components first: the capacitors add to 440 µF, the resistors parallel down to 500 kΩ, and then $\tau = RC$ = 440×10⁻⁶ × 500×10³ = 220 s. In one of those time constants the capacitor charges to 63.2% of the applied voltage. The wrong choices — 55, 110, and 440 seconds — are exactly the three wrong ways to combine four parts, so the question is really testing whether you simplify before you reach for the calculator.

{{fig:ch05-time-constants-e}}

Now the mirror world. Everything impedance does, admittance does upside down: admittance is the inverse of impedance (E5B12), written Y = 1/Z and measured in siemens. In rectangular form Y = G + jB, where the real part is conductance G and the imaginary part is susceptance — the letter is B (E5B02, E5B06). The polar conversion is the tidiest rule in the group: take the reciprocal of the magnitude and change the sign of the angle (E5B03), so a capacitive impedance's negative angle emerges as a positive admittance angle. And a pure reactance converts to a susceptance of reciprocal magnitude (E5B05) — 100 Ω of pure capacitive reactance becomes a 10 millisiemens susceptance. Why bother? Because parallel components add admittances the way series components add impedances, and RF work is full of parallel parts.

Phase is the other everyday skill, and two memory hooks carry it: ELI the ICE man. In an inductor (L), voltage (E) comes before current (I) — ELI says voltage leads current by 90° (E5B10). In a capacitor (C), current comes before voltage — ICE says current leads voltage by 90° (E5B09). Say the name, read the order of the letters, and the lead/lag direction is decided before you compute anything.

When a series RLC circuit carries both reactances, the phase angle between the voltage across it and the current through it is θ = atan((X_L − X_C)/R). The recipe is mechanical: subtract the reactances first, divide by the resistance, take the arctangent, then read the sign. Positive means net inductive, so the voltage leads; negative means net capacitive, so the voltage lags.

> **Worked example:** E5B07 gives X_C = 500 Ω, X_L = 250 Ω, and R = 1 kΩ, so $\theta = \arctan((X_L - X_C)/R)$ = atan((250 − 500)/1000) = atan(−0.25) = −14.0°. Negative angle, capacitive circuit, voltage lagging the current — 14.0 degrees lagging is the keyed answer. The identical recipe runs the rest of the group: (100 − 300)/100 gives −63°, lagging (E5B08), and (75 − 25)/100 gives +27°, leading (E5B11). Every one of these questions offers both angles in both directions, and the solvers who compute perfectly and still miss are the ones who flipped lead and lag at the last step.

Twelve questions: E5B01 through E5B12.

### Complex Impedance: Rectangular, Polar, and Phasors

Here is the reason resistance and reactance cannot simply be added: they act at right angles to each other. The bookkeeping device is the j-operator — the book uses j, the engineering convention, never the mathematician's i — which marks a 90° rotation on the impedance plane. Rectangular notation writes impedance as Z = R ± jX: the resistance is the real part, the reactance is the imaginary part, and the sign carries the physics. The law is +j inductive, −j capacitive. So a pure capacitive reactance of 100 Ω is written 0 − j100 (E5C01), and an impedance of 50 − j25 Ω means 50 Ω of resistance in series with 25 Ω of capacitive reactance (E5C06).

Polar notation states the same impedance as a magnitude and a phase angle (E5C02). A pure inductive reactance sits at a positive 90 degree phase angle, a pure capacitance at −90° (E5C03). Converting rectangular to polar takes the Pythagorean theorem for the magnitude and an arctangent for the angle: |Z| = √(R² + X²) and θ = atan(X/R). Going back the other way, R = |Z|cos θ and X = |Z|sin θ.

> **Worked example:** Convert the pool's 50 − j25 Ω to polar. The magnitude is $|Z| = \sqrt{50^2 + 25^2}$ = √3125 = 55.9 Ω, and the angle is θ = atan(−25/50) = −26.6°, so the same impedance is 55.9 ∠−26.6° Ω — the negative angle confirming what the −j already told you. Run the admittance mirror on it too, since the rule travels with you: |Y| = 1/55.9 = 17.9 mS with the angle negated to +26.6°, per E5B03. One impedance, two spellings, and the exam reads both dialects.

{{fig:ch05-complex-plane}}

The group finishes with five questions of pure diagram vocabulary. On rectangular impedance coordinates, the horizontal axis represents the resistive component and the vertical axis the reactive component (E5C09). A pure resistance therefore plots right on the horizontal axis (E5C07) — no reactance, no height. Polar coordinates are the system that displays the phase angle of a circuit containing resistance and reactance directly (E5C08). A phasor diagram is the picture that shows the phase relationships among impedances at a given frequency (E5C05) — arrows frozen at one instant, their angles frozen with them. And graphs of circuit frequency response almost always use a logarithmic vertical axis (E5C04), because decibels are how RF people look at gain.

This notation is not exam-only trivia. Your antenna analyzer talks to you in exactly this dialect: sweep a dipole and it reports something like 73 + j12 at one frequency and 68 − j35 at another, and now you can hear what it is saying — 73 Ω of resistance with 12 Ω of inductive reactance at the first spot, 68 Ω of resistance with 35 Ω of capacitive reactance at the second. The sign of the j-term tells you which side of resonance you are standing on before you touch a single turn of a matching coil.

Nine questions, E5C01 through E5C09. The remaining three E5C questions get their own section, because they come with a chart.

### Reading the E5-1 Chart

Three of the hardest questions in the pool are plotting exercises, and the first thing to know about their figure is what it is not. Figure E5-1 is a rectangular-coordinate impedance chart — a graph, not a circuit schematic. There are no components drawn in it at all. The resistor and the capacitor or inductor live in each question's stem; the figure is simply the plane on which your computed impedance lands. The frame is a square, both axes run −600 to +600 Ω with numeric labels every 100 Ω, the horizontal axis is resistance, the vertical axis is reactance, and eight dots are labeled Point 1 through Point 8.

{{fig:ch05-pool-fig-e51}}

The skill is a three-step procedure, and it never varies. Compute the reactance from the component value and frequency, using X_L = 2πfL or X_C = 1/(2πfC). Attach the sign: inductive reactance is +j and plots in the upper half, capacitive is −j and plots in the lower half. Then pair the reactance with the resistor — (R, ±X) — walk out along the horizontal axis to R, walk up or down to X, and name the nearest point.

Walk through all three against the redrawn chart above. E5C10: a 400 Ω resistor in series with a 38 pF capacitor at 14 MHz. The capacitive reactance works out to 1/(2π × 14 MHz × 38 pF) ≈ 299 Ω — call it 300 on this grid — so the impedance is 400 − j300 Ω: right 400, down 300, Point 4. E5C11: 300 Ω in series with an 18 µH inductor at 3.505 MHz. The inductive reactance is 2π × 3.505 MHz × 18 µH ≈ 396 Ω — call it 400 — which gives 300 + j400 Ω: right 300, up 400, Point 3. E5C12: 300 Ω with a 19 pF capacitor at 21.2 MHz. The capacitive reactance is ≈ 395 Ω — call it 400 — which gives 300 − j400 Ω: right 300, down 400, Point 1.

> **Worked example:** The same recipe, condensed. E5C10: 400 Ω and 38 pF at 14 MHz gives $X_C = 1/(2\pi fC)$ ≈ 300 Ω, so Z = 400 − j300 Ω, fourth quadrant, Point 4. E5C11: 300 Ω and 18 µH at 3.505 MHz gives X_L = 2πfL ≈ 400 Ω, so Z = 300 + j400 Ω, Point 3. E5C12: 300 Ω and 19 pF at 21.2 MHz gives X_C ≈ 400 Ω, so Z = 300 − j400 Ω, Point 1. Convert the prefixes before anything else — pF is 10⁻¹² and µH is 10⁻⁶ — and let the chart's 100 Ω grid absorb your rounding; every labeled point sits a full grid square away from its neighbors.

On exam day, take the sign before the magnitude. Read the stem and name the component: a capacitor means the answer lives in the lower half of the chart, an inductor the upper half, and that one observation already kills half the choices. Only then reach for the calculator. Notice, too, that the pool never asks you to be precise — 299, 396, and 395 Ω all round to the nearest labeled point with room to spare, so estimate boldly and plot.

The distractors on this chart are an anatomy lesson. Points 5 and 7 sit at negative resistance, and negative resistance is impossible for any passive series circuit — a negative resistor would deliver power instead of dissipating it — so any answer naming Point 5 or Point 7 is auto-wrong before you compute anything. Points 6 and 8 sit on the horizontal axis, which makes them pure resistances: bait for the solver who forgot the capacitor or the coil entirely. And watch the notation collision the figure sets for you. Its axis tips are labeled +X, −X, +Y, and −Y, naming coordinate axes, while everywhere else in this chapter X names reactance. E5C09 holds the resolution — on rectangular impedance coordinates the X axis is the resistive component and the Y axis is the reactive component — and the practical reading is that capacitive impedances always plot in the chart's lower half.

Three questions, E5C10 through E5C12 — the group's arithmetic finale, reduced to compute, pair, plot.

### RF Reality: Skin Effect, Parasitics, and Real Power

The last group is about what components actually do at radio frequencies, as opposed to what the schematic says they do. Start with wire itself. Direct current uses a conductor's whole cross-section; radio-frequency current does not. As frequency climbs, current crowds toward the conductor's surface — the skin effect — so the effective cross-section shrinks and resistance increases with frequency (E5D01). The same mechanism is the primary cause of loss in film capacitors at RF (E5D08), where the current has to fight through ever-thinner effective metal in the foils and leads. The bench consequence is that your ohmmeter is an optimist: it measures the DC resistance of the whole copper cross-section, while at 14 MHz the current rides in a skin only a few ten-thousandths of an inch deep. That is why high-power tank coils are wound from wide strap or tubing, why RF grounding is done with broad braid rather than round wire — surface area, not cross-section, is what carries RF.

Every length of wire is also an inductor, which is why component leads are parasitic inductance: for circuits at VHF and above, keep lead lengths short to minimize inductive reactance (E5D02). At microwave frequencies the argument gains a second clause — short connections also reduce phase shift along the connection (E5D04), because at 10 GHz a single centimeter is a respectable fraction of a wavelength. And then the counterintuitive one, pinned exactly as the pool keys it: as a conductor's diameter increases, its electrical length increases (E5D10). As an aside, the mechanism is honest physics — a larger diameter changes how the wave propagates along the conductor, so the wave experiences more length than the tape measure shows — and it is why a fat dipole resonates a hair lower than a thin wire cut to the same length. On the exam, don't argue with it: it increases.

Parasitics explain two more exam facts and a whole category of bench surprises. Electrolytic capacitors carry too much parasitic inductance to work at RF (E5D05) — the wound-foil construction that packs microfarads into a can is a coil as well as a capacitor. An inductor's own winding has capacitance between its turns, and that inter-turn capacitance creates the inductor's self-resonance (E5D06). Self-resonance in general is just the component's nominal and parasitic reactances combining (E5D07) — and above its self-resonant frequency an inductor's parasitic capacitance wins the tug-of-war, so the part stops being inductive and turns capacitive. That is why a choke chosen for one band can do nothing, or worse, on a higher one.

The group closes with the distinction that settles every argument about wattless power: real versus reactive. Reactive power is what flows when voltage and current stand 90° out of phase (E5D03). Ideal inductors and capacitors store energy in magnetic and electric fields and hand it back each half-cycle — the energy is stored, but power is not dissipated (E5D09) — which is why reactive power earns the name wattless, nonproductive power (E5D12). Only resistance consumes real power. The volt-ampere product you would compute from the meter readings alone is the apparent power, and the ratio of real to apparent is the power factor — but the pool's arithmetic stays on the real side.

> **Worked example:** E5D11 puts a 100 Ω resistor in series with a 100 Ω inductive reactance and draws 1 ampere. Real power touches the resistance alone: $P = I^2 R$ = 1² × 100 = 100 W, and the j100 reactance adds zero real watts. The distractors are the two classic slips: 141.4 W comes from treating |Z| = 141.4 Ω as if it were resistance, and 200 W from adding 100 + 100 arithmetically. Reactance borrows and returns; resistance burns. Only the burning is billed.

One quantity ties this chapter to everything the antenna chapters do next. When a transmission line meets a load that isn't its characteristic impedance, part of the forward power reflects back, and the reflection coefficient describes how much, and in what phase: Γ = (Z_L − Z₀)/(Z_L + Z₀). You rarely measure Γ directly — you measure power — so the wattmeter form matters more: the magnitude is the square root of the reflected-to-forward power ratio. From there, SWR and return loss are the same mismatch read two different ways: SWR = (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|), and return loss = −20·log₁₀|Γ|, with the 20 rather than 10 because Γ is an amplitude ratio, not a power ratio.

Three landmarks calibrate the scale. A perfect match has Γ = 0, nothing reflects, and SWR reads 1:1. An open or a short reflects everything, |Γ| approaches 1, and SWR climbs without limit — the curve stays gentle through about 0.5 and then goes nearly vertical. In between sits the threshold every tuner owner knows: |Γ| = 1/3 is exactly SWR 2:1.

> **Worked example:** Chapter 4's wattmeter readings, E4B06's numbers: 100 W forward and 25 W reflected. The reflection coefficient magnitude is $|\Gamma| = \sqrt{P_{refl}/P_{fwd}}$ = √(25/100) = 0.5. Then SWR = (1 + 0.5)/(1 − 0.5) = 3:1, and return loss = −20·log₁₀(0.5) ≈ 6.0 dB — one wattmeter pair, three ways to state the same mismatch, while the load absorbs what is left, 100 − 25 = 75 W. Chapter 9 takes it from here: Γ is the compass needle of the Smith chart.

**FACT:** The resonant frequency is f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)) — 50 µH with 40 pF gives 3.56 MHz and 50 µH with 10 pF gives 7.12 MHz; the resistor in those questions is a decoy, because R never enters the resonance formula.

**FACT:** One time constant τ = RC (or L/R) charges a capacitor to 63.2% of the applied voltage or discharges it to 36.8% of its initial voltage — 440 µF into 500 kΩ gives τ = 220 s (combine the paralleled components first).

**FACT:** Rectangular impedance is Z = R ± jX with +j inductive and −j capacitive — a pure capacitive reactance of 100 Ω is written 0 − j100, and 50 − j25 means 50 Ω resistance in series with 25 Ω capacitive reactance.

**FACT:** The Figure E5-1 plotting answers: 400 Ω + 38 pF at 14 MHz (X_C ≈ 300) → 400 − j300 → Point 4; 300 Ω + 18 µH at 3.505 MHz (X_L ≈ 400) → 300 + j400 → Point 3; 300 Ω + 19 pF at 21.2 MHz (X_C ≈ 400) → 300 − j400 → Point 1.

**FACT:** Reactive power means V and I 90° out of phase — ideal inductors and capacitors store energy in fields and return it, dissipating nothing — hence "wattless, nonproductive power," and only resistance consumes real power: 1 A through 100 Ω + j100 Ω dissipates I²R = 100 W, not 141 or 200 W.

### Key Takeaways

- At resonance X_L = X_C and the reactances cancel: series RLC drops to minimum impedance with maximum line current and V and I in phase; parallel RLC rises to maximum impedance with minimum input current but maximum circulating current inside the loop.

- The resonant frequency f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)) ignores R entirely — the printed resistor is a decoy; series Q = X/R, parallel Q = R/X, and half-power bandwidth is BW = f₀/Q.

- At series resonance the voltages across L and C can each reach about Q times the applied voltage — the reason tank capacitors arc.

- One time constant τ = RC (or L/R) charges a capacitor to 63.2% of the applied voltage or discharges it to 36.8% — combine the paralleled parts before you multiply.

- Admittance Y = G + jB in siemens is the inverse of impedance: conductance G real, susceptance B imaginary; polar conversion reciprocates the magnitude and negates the angle. ELI the ICE man settles lead and lag, and θ = atan((X_L − X_C)/R) settles the angle — positive leads, negative lags.

- Rectangular impedance is Z = R ± jX with +j inductive and −j capacitive; polar form is |Z| = √(R² + X²) at θ = atan(X/R).

- Figure E5-1 is a chart, not a circuit — compute the reactance, pair it with R, and plot: 400 − j300 is Point 4, 300 + j400 is Point 3, 300 − j400 is Point 1; the negative-resistance points are auto-wrong.

- Only resistance consumes real power — P = I²R — while reactive power is V and I 90° out of phase, stored and returned; and a wattmeter gives |Γ| = √(P_refl/P_fwd), so 25 W reflected of 100 W forward means Γ = 0.5, SWR 3:1, and about 6 dB of return loss.
