## 6. Components and Devices at Extra Depth

Open your parts drawer and you already know the cast — diodes, transistors, ICs, coils, crystals. The Extra exam asks the drawer to get honest. What is the junction inside a transistor actually doing when it is reverse-biased, and why does that shut the current off? Why do three nearly identical FET symbols, differing only in a small arrow, name three different devices? Why does a Schottky diode beat a plain junction diode in a switching supply, and why does the venerable DIP package vanish from designs above HF? The answers are component physics at exactly the depth the pool tests — no textbook band theory, just what makes each part behave. In this chapter you'll learn the semiconductor junction from the doping up, the whole diode family, digital logic families and their symbols, magnetic cores and crystals, MMICs and modern packages, and the optical devices. This is pool subelement E6 — Circuit Components: 68 questions in six groups, E6A through E6F, six of which will be on your exam. Three of the pool's ten figures live here too — E6-1, E6-2, and E6-3 — and their six questions are pure symbol-reading you can learn mechanically.

### The semiconductor junction and the transistor family

Book 3 gave you the working model — a diode conducts one way, a transistor is two junctions sharing a thin middle layer, forward bias turns a junction on. That refresher line is all you need from there, because the Extra pool goes underneath the model to the doping itself.

Start with pure silicon. Every silicon atom bonds to four neighbors with its four valence electrons, and at rest there are few free charges to move. **Doping** changes that deliberately. Add a trace of a five-electron **donor impurity** — phosphorus, say — and each donor atom contributes a free electron the crystal does not need for bonding. That is **N-type** material, whose majority carriers are electrons (E6A02). Add a three-electron **acceptor impurity** instead — boron — and each acceptor leaves a bond one electron short: a **hole**, which wanders the crystal as a mobile positive charge. That is **P-type** material (E6A04). The pool swaps the pair in the distractors, so pin the direction once: donors add free electrons (N), acceptors add holes (P).

Press the two types together and you have the PN junction. Near the boundary, electrons from the N side drop into holes on the P side and both leave circulation, forming a thin strip — the **depletion region** — that holds only the fixed ions the departed carriers left behind: negative acceptor ions on the P side, positive donor ions on the N side. The charge of those fixed ions opposes any further flow. Now bias the junction. Forward bias, positive to the P side, pushes majority carriers toward the junction; the depletion region narrows and current flows as carriers cross and recombine. Reverse bias, positive to the N side, pulls holes and electrons away from the junction; the depletion region widens and essentially no current flows (E6A03). That one mechanism — reverse bias pulls carriers away from the junction — is the keyed answer and the mental picture behind half this subelement.

{{fig:ch06-depletion-region}}

The figure draws those three states side by side. At rest, the depletion region sits at the boundary holding only fixed ions — negative on the P side, positive on the N side. Under forward bias the battery pushes the majority carriers toward each other, the region thins, and current crosses. Under reverse bias the battery pulls the majority carriers back, the region thickens into an insulator, and the crossing arrow is crossed out. Every semiconductor question in this subelement is that picture with a different label: the LED's forward drop, the varactor's bias-tracked width, the JFET's reverse-biased gate.

The material matters as much as the junction. Gallium arsenide's electrons move more freely than silicon's — higher electron mobility — and that speed is what microwave circuits pay for, so GaAs is the semiconductor of choice up there (E6A01; E6E01 asks the same fact from the MMIC side). Silicon owns the frequencies below microwaves because it is cheap and well behaved, not because it is fast.

Now the transistor families, where the pool's favorite comparison lives. A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) is current-controlled: base current drives collector current, and the base-emitter junction is an ordinary forward-biased diode that draws real DC current. A field-effect transistor is voltage-controlled: its gate is either insulated from the channel by a thin oxide (the MOSFET) or isolated by a reverse-biased junction (the JFET), and in both cases essentially no DC gate current flows. That is why a FET's DC input impedance is far higher than a BJT's (E6A05) — the gate barely loads whatever drives it, which is why FETs front-end receivers and voltmeters.

Two FET behaviors earn their own questions. A **depletion-mode** FET is built with its channel already formed, so it conducts source-to-drain with zero gate voltage — you must apply bias to turn it off — while an **enhancement-mode** device has no channel until the gate voltage creates one, so it stays off at zero bias (E6A09). And the MOSFET's weakness is the same oxide that makes it strong: the insulated gate is a tiny capacitor whose insulation a static spark can puncture, so many MOSFETs carry gate-protection Zener diodes, and what those Zeners protect against is **static damage** — not overheating, not bias referencing (E6A12).

Three BJT numbers close the physics. **Beta** is the transistor's current gain, the change in collector current with respect to the change in base current: $\beta = \Delta I_C / \Delta I_B$ (E6A06). A forward-biased silicon base-emitter junction drops about 0.6–0.7 V, and that voltage — not a resistance — is the sign a silicon NPN is biased on (E6A07). Study the distractor construction: the wrong answers offer 6–7 *volts* and both magnitudes in *ohms*, a pure unit-and-magnitude swap. And the **alpha cutoff frequency** is where the grounded-base current gain has fallen to 0.7 of the gain obtainable at 1 kHz (E6A08); "beta cutoff frequency" is an invented distractor.

> **Worked example:** How much can one junction drop tell you? A forward-biased silicon junction holds about 0.6–0.7 V (E6A07), so anywhere a base sits at a known voltage, the emitter sits one drop lower. In the redrawn Figure E7-2 — chapter 7's linear regulator — the Zener holds the pass transistor's base at 12.7 V, and the emitter follows at $12.7 - 0.7 = 12$ V, which is the regulated output. One fact, two chapters: the junction drop you learn here sets the output voltage there.

Doping, depletion, FET versus BJT, and the three numbers — that is pool group E6A, minus its two figure questions, which get their own treatment next.

### Reading the symbol sheets: the six FETs of Figure E6-1

Figure E6-1 shows six circled transistor symbols in two rows of three, numbered beneath — top row 1, 2, 3, bottom row 4, 5, 6. Every symbol shares one skeleton: a circle around a vertical channel bar, the drain lead leaving the top right (D), the source lead leaving the bottom right (S), gate leads entering from the left. The differences are three small marks, and three independent rules resolve every option the pool offers.

{{fig:ch06-pool-fig-e61}}

- **Rule 1 — JFET or MOSFET?** Look at where the arrow sits. On the JFETs — symbols 1 and 6 — the arrow sits ON the gate lead, which runs continuously into the channel bar, because a JFET's gate is a junction. On the MOSFETs — symbols 2 through 5 — the gate is an insulated plate drawn parallel to the bar with a gap, and the arrow moves inside the circle onto the substrate-source connection (note the connection dot tying it to the S lead).

- **Rule 2 — N-channel or P-channel?** Read the arrow's direction wherever it sits: arrow pointing IN toward the channel is N-channel; arrow pointing OUT, away from the channel, is P-channel.

- **Rule 3 — one gate or two?** The dual-gate parts carry two gate leads labeled G2 over G1 — symbols 4 and 5. A dual-gate MOSFET is a cascode in one package: G1 takes the signal, G2 takes AGC or a fixed bias.

Walk the sheet with the rules. Symbol **1** is a JFET (arrow on the gate lead) whose arrow points away from the channel — a **P-channel junction FET**. Symbol **2** is a single-gate MOSFET with the internal arrow in — N-channel. Symbol **3** is the same construction with the arrow out — P-channel. Symbol **4** is the dual-gate MOSFET with the arrow in — an **N-channel dual-gate MOSFET**. Symbol **5** is the dual-gate with the arrow out — P-channel. Symbol **6** is a JFET whose gate arrow points into the channel — N-channel.

> **Worked example:** Identify the device: a gate lead enters from the left and touches the channel through a filled arrowhead pointing **away** from the channel; the gate is not insulated. Rule 1: arrow on the gate lead = JFET, not MOSFET. Rule 2: arrow pointing out = P-channel. So symbol 1 in the redrawn Figure E6-1 is a P-channel junction FET — E6A11's keyed answer. Now add a second gate: two insulated gate lines labeled G2 over G1 with the internal arrow pointing in = N-channel dual-gate MOSFET — symbol 4, E6A10's keyed answer.

The two exam questions key to opposite corners of the sheet: E6A10 (N-channel dual-gate MOSFET) wants symbol **4**, and E6A11 (P-channel junction FET) wants symbol **1**. Look at the option pools — E6A10 offers {2, 4, 5, 6} and E6A11 offers {1, 2, 3, 6} — every option is one rule-flip away from the answer, which is why the three rules applied in order never miss.

### The diode family

You met the plain junction diode in Book 3; the Extra pool collects the specialized cousins, roughly one question each. Learn every device by the single property that makes it worth manufacturing.

The **Zener** is the backwards workhorse: operated in reverse breakdown, it holds a constant voltage drop across varying current — exactly what a voltage reference or shunt regulator needs (E6B01). The **Schottky** replaces the PN junction with a **metal-semiconductor junction** (E6B08), paying off in a low forward voltage drop and fast recovery, with no stored-charge tail to clear. That makes it the choice as a VHF/UHF mixer or detector (E6B06) and as an efficient power-supply rectifier (E6B02) — and pin the reason precisely: the rectifier advantage is the **lower forward drop**, less $I \times V$ burned as heat, NOT a higher reverse breakdown. "Much higher reverse voltage breakdown" is the distractor. The **LED** runs its junction in the light-emitting direction, and its forward voltage is set by the semiconductor **band gap** (E6B03) — which is why red and blue LEDs drop different voltages. The **varactor** is a diode used as a **voltage-controlled capacitor** (E6B04): reverse-bias it and the depletion width, hence the junction capacitance, tracks the bias. The phrase is the tell; the distractors — tunnel diode, silicon-controlled rectifier, Zener — are other devices entirely. The **PIN diode** sandwiches an intrinsic layer between the P and N regions; its RF resistance is set by its forward DC bias current (E6B11), and its low junction capacitance makes it a good RF switch (E6B05) — a DC-controlled RF resistor, the part inside countless antenna switches and attenuators. The **point-contact diode** — the cat's whisker of crystal-radio fame — is an RF detector, not a high-voltage rectifier (E6B09). And every junction diode shares one failure mode: excessive junction temperature. Current kills, but it kills via heat (E6B07).

One device per property, one property per question — that is pool group E6B, except the Schottky's symbol, which lives on the next sheet.

You will meet most of these in your own shack. The Zener sits in every regulated supply, holding its reference while the load current wanders. The Schottky rectifies the switching supplies that replaced the old iron transformers, and its fast recovery is why it can keep up at hundreds of kilohertz where a junction diode would still be clearing stored charge. Varactors tune electronically — a voltage-controlled oscillator is an oscillator whose tank capacitor is a diode — and PIN diodes switch and attenuate RF inside antenna tuners, transceivers, and T/R switches, all steered by a few milliamps of DC. Learn the parts once and you will recognize them on every schematic you open.

### Reading the symbol sheets: the eight diodes of Figure E6-2

Figure E6-2 draws eight diode symbols in two rows of four, numbered beneath — top row 1–4, bottom row 5–8. All eight are horizontal two-lead devices built on the same core, a filled triangle pointing into a cathode bar, with decorations that name the species.

{{fig:ch06-pool-fig-e62}}

Walk them in order. Symbol **1** adds a curved bow sweeping across the triangle near the cathode, like a second capacitor plate — the varactor-style symbol. Symbol **2** shows two triangles point-to-point sharing a capped central bar — a bidirectional, back-to-back pair. ### Digital ICs and logic families

The digital group opens with a device that is analog at heart: the **comparator** compares an input against a threshold voltage and flips its output state when the input crosses that threshold (E6C02). Real inputs are noisy, and a bare comparator balanced at its threshold would chatter — rattling back and forth as noise wiggles the input across the line. The cure is **hysteresis**: positive feedback gives the switch two slightly different thresholds, one for rising and one for falling, so once the output commits, noise alone cannot un-commit it (E6C01).

Then the logic states and families. **Tri-state** logic adds a third output state — beyond 0 and 1, a high-impedance state that effectively disconnects the output — so many devices can share one bus, each speaking only when enabled (E6C03). The memory chips on a shared data bus all listen to the same wires, and only the enabled chip drives them; the rest vanish electrically. It has nothing to do with ternary math; the third state is "not connected." Of the listed families — Schottky TTL, ECL, NMOS, CMOS — **CMOS has the lowest power consumption** (E6C05), because its complementary pairs draw essentially no current except while switching: one transistor of the pair is always off, so the only significant current is the brief pulse as the output changes state. CMOS also switches its input at about **half the supply voltage** (E6C06): a mid-rail threshold means a noise spike has a long way to travel before it can flip an input, and that wide margin is the noise immunity the question credits. The distractor claims "twice the power supply voltage," a threshold no input signal could ever reach — half, not twice. **BiCMOS** marries the two transistor worlds on one die: the high input impedance of CMOS with the low output impedance of bipolar drivers (E6C04). Notice that E6C04 offers "All these choices are correct" as a decoy — the keyed answer names the impedance combination, because the other clauses (much less expensive, immune to electrostatic damage) are false. Learn the content, never the pattern: in this pool "all these choices" is sometimes keyed and sometimes bait. A **pull-up or pull-down resistor** connects to the positive or negative supply to establish a defined voltage when an input or output is otherwise an open circuit (E6C07) — floating lines drift and gather noise, so the resistor decides their resting state. And the FPGA: a field-programmable gate array is configured in a **hardware description language**, HDL (E6C09), which describes the logic fabric itself rather than a program running on it — machine and assembly language belong to the distractor's world.

Comparators, tri-state, families, and the resistor that keeps inputs honest — pool group E6C, with its three gate-symbol questions next.

### Reading the symbol sheets: the six gates of Figure E6-3

Figure E6-3 shows six logic gate symbols in two rows of three, numbered beneath — top row 1–3, bottom row 4–6. Two inputs enter from the left (a single input on 5 and 6), one output exits right. Reading them is a 2×2 matrix plus a triangle pair.

{{fig:ch06-pool-fig-e63}}

- **Body shape names the family.** The flat-left, round-right "D" body is the AND family: symbol **1 = AND**. The curved-input, pointed-output body is the OR family: symbol **3 = OR**. The triangle is the buffer/inverter pair, symbols 5 and 6.

- **A bubble on the output negates.** Add the small output bubble and the family inverts: **2 = NAND** (D body with bubble), **4 = NOR** (OR body with bubble), **5 = NOT** — the inverter, a triangle with a bubble. The bubble-less triangle **6** is the buffer, passing its input along with fresh drive.

Three exam questions, three positions: E6C08 (NAND) keys to **2**, E6C10 (NOR) keys to **4**, and E6C11 (NOT) keys to **5**. E6C11's option pool — {2, 4, 5, 6} — deliberately mixes the bubbles of the AND and OR families with the two triangles, so the discriminator on the inverter question is the triangle body first, then the bubble. Body tells you the family; bubble tells you the negation; triangle tells you the one-input pair.

### Magnetics and crystals

Group E6D steps away from semiconductors to the parts that steer magnetic fields and keep time.

Start with the crystal. **Piezoelectricity runs both directions**: a piezoelectric material generates a voltage when it is mechanically stressed (E6D01) AND physically flexes when a voltage is applied to it (E6D03) — two questions, one two-way effect. A quartz crystal is that effect cut and polished into a resonator, and its electrical model is a **series RLC "motional" arm** — the mechanical resonance seen electrically — **in parallel with a shunt capacitance** from the electrodes and strays (E6D02). That two-branch model is why a crystal has both a series and a parallel resonant frequency a hair apart.

Then the cores. An alternating magnetic field induces circulating **eddy currents** in any conductive core, and those currents burn power — so transformer and inductor cores are built from thin **laminations** insulated from one another, breaking up the current paths and reducing eddy-current loss (E6D04). That is why a mains transformer's core is a stack of varnish-coated sheets instead of a solid block: a solid core would be a one-turn shorted secondary, wasting power as heat. A core's **permeability** determines the inductance a given winding produces (E6D06): more permeability, more inductance per turn. Here the pool sets its favorite two-material trap and tests both directions. **Ferrite** has the higher permeability, so a ferrite core needs **fewer turns** for a given inductance than powdered iron (E6D05) — but **powdered iron** has the better **temperature stability** of its magnetic characteristics (E6D08). Ferrite wins the turns count; powdered iron wins the stability. The distractors trade the two materials' strengths, and aluminum shows up as a decoy in the stability question — it returns in E6D11's slug question below. One more ferrite job: small **ferrite beads** slipped over the input and output terminals of a transistor HF amplifier are the common VHF/UHF parasitic suppressors (E6D09) — a tiny lossy choke that keeps an amplifier from singing at ten times its design frequency.

Shapes and slugs finish the group. A **toroid** confines most of its magnetic field inside the core (E6D10) — the winding closes on itself, so the field has nowhere to leak: less stray coupling into neighboring circuits, less pickup from them. That is why toroids dominate RF inductors and broadband transformers. **Slug tuning** moves material through the field: a ferrite slug adds inductance, but inserting **brass** — conductive yet non-ferromagnetic — *decreases* the inductance (E6D11), because eddy currents in the brass buck the field out of its volume. And **saturation** is the failure at the far extreme: the core is operated at excessive magnetic flux (E6D12), permeability collapses, and the inductor stops being much of an inductor.

One numbering note before you drill: E6D runs D01 through D06 and D08 through D12 — E6D07 was withdrawn by the pool's third errata in September 2025 for having more than one correct answer, and the group was never renumbered, so the gap is intentional, not a question you need to hunt down. Crystals, cores, toroids, and slugs are pool group E6D.

### MMICs, packages, and SMT

At the top of the frequency range, components stop being parts you solder and start being small transmission-line systems — group E6E is how the industry copes.

Materials first, because the pool asks them twice. Gallium arsenide's higher electron mobility — the same fact E6A01 keys for microwave transistors — suits it to UHF and above (E6E01). Push higher still and **gallium nitride** supports the highest MMIC operating frequency of the listed materials (E6E03). MMIC — **monolithic microwave integrated circuit** — is the building block those materials serve: a complete amplifier on one chip, engineered to present **50 ohms in and 50 ohms out** with controlled gain, low noise figure, and constant input and output impedance over its specified frequency range (E6E04, E6E06). You do not design around an MMIC; you drop it into a 50-ohm line. Cascade three of them for more gain and every junction is still 50 ohms — no retuning, no matching network, which is the whole sales pitch. Bias reaches the common type **through a resistor and/or RF choke connected to the amplifier output lead** (E6E08) — the choke passes DC and blocks RF, and the distractor swaps input for output, so read the stem twice. Between chips, the usual MMIC interconnect is **microstrip** (E6E07), a printed trace over a ground plane — the transmission line a circuit board can manufacture. And one number to pin for the receiver chain: a typical low-noise UHF preamplifier noise figure is **0.5 dB** (E6E05). Noise figure measures how much hiss a stage adds beyond the theoretical minimum, so lower is better — half a dB means the preamp contributes almost no noise of its own. The distractors are −10 dB, 44 dBm, and −20 dBm — dBm values are power levels, not noise figures at all. The trap is units again.

Packaging is the other half of the story, and the physics is chapter 5's parasitics wearing new clothes. Above HF, every millimeter of lead is inductance and every pad is capacitance, so **surface-mount** parts — tiny bodies soldered flat to the board — have the least parasitic inductance and capacitance (E6E09). The follow-up is a legitimate all-of-the-above (E6E10): at RF, surface-mount technology means smaller circuit area, shorter board traces, and less parasitics, and each clause is independently true. The counterexample is the venerable **DIP**, the **dual in-line package** with two rows of pins on opposite sides of the body (E6E11) — the only through-hole part in a list with PLCC, BGA, and SOT (E6E02) — and the reason it disappears at UHF is its **lead length** (E6E12), not dielectric loss.

> **Worked example:** Why does the DIP vanish above HF? Its centimeters of lead length are parasitic inductance — chapter 5's lesson that straight wire becomes an inductor as frequency climbs. At UHF the "same" resistor or IC is no longer the same component, because the package's own reactance swamps the datasheet behavior. Surface-mount parts, with millimeter leads and smaller loops, keep their datasheet behavior — exactly what E6E12 and E6E09 key.

Materials, the 50-ohm block, and the packages — that is pool group E6E.

### Optical devices

The last group is where electronics meets light, and the pool's whole game is a boundary: **photovoltaic** devices *generate* electricity from light; **photoconductive** devices merely *change resistance* in response to it. Keep the two verbs apart and eleven questions fall.

The generating side. **Photons** are the particles absorbed in a photovoltaic cell (E6F01) — light arrives as particle energy, and each absorbed photon can knock an electron-hole pair loose. The **photovoltaic effect** is the conversion of light to electrical energy (E6F04). PV **efficiency** is the fraction of the light falling on the cell that is converted to current (E6F09). The common power-generating material is **silicon** (E6F10) — your rooftop panel is the same element as your CPU — and a fully illuminated silicon photovoltaic cell produces about **0.5 V** open-circuit (E6F11), a junction voltage set by the physics, not by the cell's size. Big panels are series stacks and parallel banks of half-volt cells.

The sensing side. Light **decreases** the resistance of photoconductive material (E6F02): absorbed photons create carriers, and more carriers conduct better — the photocell in a photographic light meter and the sensor that dims your shack's display both read light as a falling resistance. The common photoconductive material is a **crystalline semiconductor** (E6F06 — where "All these choices are correct" is a decoy; polyphenol acetate and argon are not it). The **optoisolator** packages the boundary in one part: an LED and a **phototransistor** facing each other across an insulating gap, so the signal crosses as light and no current can. That is why optoisolators sit between solid-state control circuits and 120 VAC switched circuits — they provide **electrical isolation** (E6F03, E6F08), not impedance matching and not a low-impedance link: a fault on the mains side has no copper path back into your microcontroller. The **solid-state relay** builds the switch itself from semiconductors — a device that implements the functions of an electromechanical relay with no moving parts (E6F07); the distractor, a transistor driving a mechanical relay coil, describes a hybrid, not an SSR. And the **optical shaft encoder** detects rotation by interrupting a light source with a patterned wheel (E6F05) — count the light pulses and you know the angle, which is how your radio's tuning knob talks to its synthesizer.

Photons in, current or resistance out — pool group E6F, and with it the whole subelement.

### Key Takeaways

- Donor impurities add free electrons (N-type); acceptor impurities add holes (P-type) — and reverse bias pulls carriers away from the junction, widening the depletion region until essentially no current flows.

- A FET's gate is insulated (MOSFET) or reverse-biased (JFET), so it draws no DC current — that is the whole input-impedance advantage over a BJT — and a depletion-mode FET conducts at zero gate voltage while an enhancement-mode device does not.

- A forward-biased silicon base-emitter junction drops about 0.6–0.7 V; beta is the change in collector current over the change in base current; alpha cutoff is where grounded-base gain falls to 0.7 of its 1 kHz value.

- The diode family in one line each: Zener = constant voltage reference, Schottky = metal-semiconductor junction with low drop and fast recovery, LED drop set by the band gap, varactor = voltage-controlled capacitor, PIN = current-controlled RF resistance, point-contact = RF detector.

- CMOS sips the least power and switches its input at about half the supply voltage; tri-state adds a high-impedance output state for bus sharing; FPGAs are configured in a hardware description language.

- Ferrite wins the turns count, powdered iron wins temperature stability; a toroid keeps its field inside the core; brass in a coil decreases inductance; laminations fight eddy currents; saturation is excessive flux.

- MMICs are 50 ohms in and 50 ohms out with bias fed through the output lead, a good low-noise UHF preamp runs a 0.5 dB noise figure, and surface-mount wins at RF because parasitics shrink — the DIP's lead length is what kills it at UHF.

- Read the three pool figures by their tells: E6-1's arrow position (on the gate lead = JFET, internal = MOSFET), direction (in = N, out = P), and gate count; E6-2's squared S-hooks = Schottky; E6-3's body-plus-bubble matrix.

**FACT:** Donor impurities add free electrons (N-type), acceptor impurities add holes (P-type), and reverse bias pulls carriers away from the junction and widens the depletion region so no current flows.

**FACT:** Beta is ΔIc/ΔIb (current gain), a forward-biased silicon base-emitter junction drops about 0.6–0.7 V, and alpha cutoff frequency is where the grounded-base current gain falls to 0.7 of its 1 kHz value.

**FACT:** CMOS has the lowest power consumption of the listed logic families and its input switches at about half the supply voltage (hence wide noise margins); BiCMOS combines CMOS's high input impedance with bipolar's low output impedance.

**FACT:** Piezoelectricity is two-way — the material generates a voltage when stressed and flexes when a voltage is applied — and a quartz crystal models as a series RLC motional arm in parallel with a shunt capacitance.

**FACT:** A typical low-noise UHF preamplifier noise figure is 0.5 dB.
