## 7. Practical Circuits

Eight of the fifty questions on your exam come from this chapter — subelement E7, the biggest in the pool: ninety-nine questions in eight groups, and every group gets a seat. This is where chapter 6's components become circuits. The transistor becomes an amplifier with a class letter and a bias scheme; resistors, capacitors, and inductors become filters with family names; the Zener diode becomes a regulated power supply; and the analog-to-digital converter becomes a radio that is mostly software. In this chapter you'll learn the digital building blocks, the amplifier conduction ladder, the filter and matching networks, linear and switching regulators, the modulator and detector circuits, DSP and SDR fundamentals, the op-amp gain law, and the oscillators and synthesizers that make stable frequencies — and you will learn to read the three pool schematics, Figures E7-1, E7-2, and E7-3, which eleven of the ninety-nine ask about directly. The good news: for the pool's largest subelement, the math is small. Nearly everything here is vocabulary plus pattern recognition, and the arithmetic that does appear — five op-amp gains, one regulator dissipation, one bit count — is drilled below with the pool's own numbers.

### Digital Building Blocks: Flip-Flops, Counters, and Gates

The flip-flop is the atom of digital memory: a bistable element, meaning it has two stable states (E7A01), and it holds whichever one it is in until the next clock edge commands a change. The J-K and T varieties differ in their control inputs; here what matters is what one does to a pulse train. Clock a toggle flip-flop and its output changes state once for every two input pulses — a single flip-flop divides the frequency of a pulse train by 2 (E7A03). Cascade stages and the division ratios multiply: N stages divide by 2ⁿ, each stage halving what the previous one handed it.

> **Worked example:** Dividing by 16 means finding which power of 2 equals 16, and $16 = 2^4$ — so four flip-flops do it (E7A04). The distractors, 6 and 8 and 16, punish any guess that isn't the exponent.

Chain those divide-by-2 stages and you have also built a counter: the cascaded outputs, read together, tally input pulses in binary, which is why the words "divider" and "counter" keep trading places in digital catalogs. A decade counter wraps the same idea around a count of ten: it emits one output pulse for every 10 input pulses (E7A02). Notice the verb — it counts. Both decoder distractors in that question describe a display driver, the chip that would turn a count into seven-segment digits, and that is a different job with a different name. The counter tallies; the decoder translates for the display.

The multivibrator family sorts by how many stable states it has. An astable multivibrator has none: it continuously alternates between two states with no external clock signal (E7A05) — the classic blinker and clock-source circuit, free-running forever at a rate set by its R-C timing. A monostable has one: a trigger switches it temporarily to its alternate state for a set time, and then it returns on its own (E7A06) — a one-shot, good for stretching an irregular pulse to a fixed width. The bistable member is the flip-flop you started with. And when the pool says positive logic, it means the higher voltage represents a 1 and the lower voltage a 0 (E7A11) — the convention every datasheet assumes unless it says otherwise.

Gate truth tables round out the group, and you already read the symbols in chapter 6. A NAND gate's output is 0 only when all of its inputs are 1 (E7A07) — an AND gate with the answer inverted, so it holds 1 until every input agrees. An OR gate's output is 1 if any input is 1 (E7A08). The XNOR — exclusive-NOR — produces 0 when exactly one input is 1, which makes it an equality detector: its output is 1 precisely when its two inputs match (E7A09). That question's option D is the XOR truth table, the classic swap, because XOR is 1 when exactly one input is 1 and XNOR is its exact opposite — read the stem twice before you answer. And a truth table itself is simply a list of inputs and the corresponding outputs for a digital device (E7A10), the master reference every one of these gate questions reduces to.

That is the E7A group — eleven questions, E7A01 through E7A11.

**FACT:** A flip-flop is bistable (two stable states) and one stage divides a pulse train by 2 — N cascaded stages divide by 2ⁿ, so dividing by 16 takes 4 flip-flops — and a decade counter emits one output pulse per 10 input pulses.

### Amplifier Classes and Figure E7-1

Book 3 taught the transistor amplifier in one line: bias the device so a small input signal steers a larger current, and the output is a bigger copy of the input. The class letter says what fraction of the signal cycle the active device actually conducts, and that fraction sets the trade between fidelity and efficiency. Conduct all the time and the output is faithful but the device idles hot; conduct in short pulses and the efficiency soars but the waveform needs help to look like the input again. Learn the ladder and most of this group falls off it.

Class A conducts the entire 360 degrees of the cycle, with the operating point set about halfway between saturation and cutoff (E7B04) — maximum linearity, worst efficiency, because the device draws its full standing current even with no signal. Class B conducts exactly 180 degrees: each device in a push-pull pair handles one half of the waveform and rests through the other. Class AB splits the difference — each active element in a push-pull class AB amplifier conducts more than 180 degrees but less than 360 degrees (E7B01), and the "exactly 180 degrees" choice sitting right beside the keyed answer is the class-B trap. Class C conducts less than 180 degrees, in short pulses at the peaks: very efficient and thoroughly nonlinear, so a class C stage needs a tuned output circuit to reconstruct the sine wave. Run an SSB signal through one and the result is signal distortion and excessive bandwidth (E7B07) — class C belongs to FM and CW, where nothing about the information lives in the amplitude. Class D abandons linear operation entirely: it is an amplifier that uses switching technology to achieve high efficiency (E7B02), efficient because the device sits at saturation or cutoff — fully on or fully off — most of the time (E7B08), dissipating little either way. Precisely because its output is a switched waveform full of harmonics, a class D stage requires a filter at its output to remove the harmonic content (E7B03).

You have met the whole ladder in the wild already. The small-signal stages in a receiver run class A because they can afford the idle current; your SSB linear runs class AB because linearity is the whole point of SSB amplification; FM and CW finals get away with class C; and class D turned up first in high-power audio and now drives transmitters that are essentially power DACs. The exam doesn't ask where the classes live — it asks you to recite the ladder — but the ladder is easier to keep straight when each rung has a face.

Three practical questions round out the group. Unwanted oscillation in an RF power amplifier is cured with parasitic suppressors and/or neutralization (E7B05) — the suppressor damps the unintended VHF feedback path, and neutralization feeds back an equal-and-opposite sample of the output to cancel the path through the device itself. A grounded-grid amplifier has low input impedance (E7B06) — you drive the cathode, and the driven electrode looks like a low impedance, which suits the configuration to matching a coax feedline. And the emitter follower, the common-collector stage, keeps its input and output in phase (E7B09): no inversion, just current gain and a voltage gain of nearly one, which is why it serves as a buffer between a delicate oscillator and a hungry load.

Now read the first of the pool's three schematics — the redrawn Figure E7-1, a single-stage NPN transistor amplifier.

{{fig:ch07-pool-fig-e71}}

Start at the base. R1 drops from the positive rail to the base node, and R2 runs from the base node to ground: the pair forms a voltage divider that pins the base's DC operating point regardless of the transistor's particular beta — voltage divider bias (E7B10). Move to the emitter. R3 sits between emitter and ground and carries the full emitter current, so if the transistor tries to conduct harder, the emitter voltage rises, the base-emitter junction relaxes, and the current settles back — that local negative feedback is self bias (E7B11). Here is the classic misread the question banks on: C3, the capacitor in parallel with R3, is the emitter bypass, and it bypasses R3 for AC only, so the stage keeps its DC stability without giving up AC gain. E7B11's "emitter bypass" distractor names C3's job, not R3's — say that sentence to yourself before you mark the answer. Finally, identify the topology: signal enters at the base through coupling capacitor C1, leaves at the collector through C2 — both capacitors pass the signal while blocking the stage's DC from the circuits on either side — and the emitter is common to both the input and output loops, which makes it a common-emitter amplifier (E7B12). The unlabeled resistor in the collector lead is the load the amplified signal develops across.

That is E7B — twelve questions, E7B01 through E7B12.

**FACT:** The amplifier conduction-angle ladder: class A conducts the entire 360° (operating point about halfway between saturation and cutoff), class AB each push-pull device conducts more than 180° but less than 360°, class B exactly 180°, class C less than 180° (high efficiency, nonlinear — on SSB it produces signal distortion and excessive bandwidth), and class D is a switching amplifier efficient because the device sits at saturation or cutoff most of the time and requiring an output filter to remove harmonic content.

### Filters and Matching Networks

Book 3's filters were single L-C sections with gentle skirts. The Extra exam names the networks by their shapes and the filter families by their trade-offs, and the three-way discrimination at the center of this group is one of the most reliable marks on the exam.

First the topologies, named for the letter the schematic resembles. A low-pass Pi-network has a shunt capacitor at the input, a shunt capacitor at the output, and a series inductor between them (E7C01) — the four choices permute the L and C positions, so sketch the Pi once and the answer draws itself. It is the classic tube-amplifier output tank, matching the high plate impedance down to 50 ohms while low-passing the harmonics. Flip the arrangement and the behavior flips: a T-network with series capacitors and a shunt inductor is a high-pass (E7C02), because the series capacitors block the lows and the shunt inductor shorts what remains of them to ground — the same skeleton as the antenna tuner on many a desk. Add a series inductor at the output of a Pi and you have a Pi-L network — literally a Pi-network with an additional output series inductor (E7C07) — built for greater harmonic suppression (E7C03), which is why it follows the power amplifier in so many transmitters. Whatever the topology, the job of a matching network is the same: cancel the reactive part of an impedance and transform the resistive part to the desired value (E7C04) — chapter 5's conjugate match, done with L's and C's.

Now the family ladder. A Butterworth filter is maximally flat — no ripple anywhere in the passband, at the price of a gentle cutoff slope. A Chebyshev filter accepts ripple in the passband in exchange for a sharp cutoff (E7C05). An elliptical filter goes furthest: an extremely sharp cutoff with one or more notches in the stop band (E7C06). Flat, rippled, notched — say the ladder once and both questions are free. The trade is honest physics: every decibel of skirt steepness beyond Butterworth is bought with response you gave up somewhere else, ripple in the passband or unevenness in the stop band.

{{fig:ch07-active-filters}}

The specialists each own a niche. Helical filters — coils resonating inside shielded enclosures — are the common band-pass and notch filters at VHF and UHF (E7C08), the silver cans inside your mobile rig's front end. A crystal lattice filter is a low-level filter built from quartz crystals (E7C09) — the heart of an SSB transceiver's IF selectivity, and "low level" is the keyed phrase; it shapes signals, not power. Cavity filters serve repeater duplexers (E7C10), passing the transmit frequency while notching the receive frequency only a few hundred kilohertz away so one antenna can do both jobs at once. And shape factor — the ratio of a filter's bandwidth deep in the skirt to its bandwidth near the passband edge — measures adjacent-channel rejection (E7C11): the more rectangular the response, the closer the neighbor you can reject.

That is E7C — eleven questions, E7C01 through E7C11.

### Power Supplies, Regulators, and Figure E7-2

Book 3's power supply was transformer, rectifier, filter capacitor, done. This group adds regulation, and the pool cross-quotes two near-twin stems you must keep separate. A linear regulator holds the output constant by varying the conduction of a control element (E7D01): the pass transistor behaves as a continuously readjusted series resistance, wasting the difference as heat. A switchmode regulator instead varies the duty cycle of pulses fed to a filter (E7D02): the control element is fully on or fully off, and the fraction of each cycle spent on sets the output after the filter smooths the pulses. Conduction versus duty cycle — the distractors quote each stem into the other's question, so pin the pair now. The switcher's payoff is size and cost: high-frequency switching allows much smaller transformers and filter components for an equivalent amount of power (E7D10), because magnetics and filter parts shrink as the operating frequency rises — which is why the lightweight supply on your bench is a switcher and the heavy one is linear.

The supporting cast, quickly. A Zener diode is the stable voltage reference (E7D03). A three-terminal regulator — the 7812 in your parts drawer — is a series regulator (E7D04), its control element in series with the load. A shunt regulator works by loading the unregulated source (E7D05), diverting current around the load rather than interrupting it. Dropout voltage is the minimum input-to-output differential a regulator needs to stay in regulation (E7D12) — an input-minus-output figure, not an absolute minimum input and not an output sag. At switch-on, a step-start circuit lets the filter capacitors charge gradually (E7D15), sparing the rectifiers and the line fuse the inrush of a near short circuit. Equal-value resistors across series filter capacitors equalize the voltage across each capacitor, discharge the capacitors when the supply is off, and provide a minimum load — all three clauses independently true, so "all these choices are correct" is honestly keyed (E7D14). A solar-panel inverter converts DC to AC (E7D11). And battery operating time is amp-hour capacity divided by average current (E7D09).

> **Worked example:** A 10 Ah battery running a 2 A average load: 10 ÷ 2 = 5 hours (E7D09's formula — capacity ÷ current, nothing more).

Now the second schematic — the redrawn Figure E7-2, which turns a raw +25 V rail into a regulated +12 V output.

{{fig:ch07-pool-fig-e72}}

Read it left to right. C1, the big 4000 electrolytic, is the brute-force filter smoothing the rectified input rail. R1 feeds current from that raw rail down to the base node, where D1 — the Zener in the circle — holds the node at its Zener voltage. Q1 is the series-pass transistor: its collector sits on the +25 rail, its emitter delivers the +12 output, and its base rides on the Zener's reference, so the emitter follows one base-emitter drop below D1's voltage — chapter 6's 0.6 to 0.7 V, which is how a Zener a notch above 12 V yields a +12 output. Q1's purpose is to control the current to keep the output voltage constant (E7D06) — and notice the distractor: nothing in this circuit chops anything, because chopping is the switchmode move. C2, the second 4000 electrolytic, sits in parallel with D1, and its job is to bypass the rectifier-output ripple around the Zener so the reference stays clean (E7D07) — a regulator's output can be no steadier than its reference, and any ripple that reached the base would ride straight through to the emitter. C3, the little 0.01 capacitor, kills fast output transients, and R2 is the load. The whole circuit is a linear voltage regulator (E7D08): conduction control, not duty cycle.

The same figure's numbers power the group's calculation question.

> **Worked example:** In the redrawn Figure E7-2, Q1 drops the raw 25 V to the regulated 12 V while passing the 1 A load current, so it dissipates $P = (V_{in} - V_{out}) \times I_{out}$ = (25 − 12) × 1 = 13 W (E7D13). The dissipation uses the voltage difference from input to output, never the input or output voltage alone — and thirteen watts in one transistor is why that device wears a heat sink.

That is E7D — fifteen questions, E7D01 through E7D15, the largest group in the subelement.

**FACT:** Figure E7-2's regulator answers: Q1 controls the current to keep the output voltage constant, C2 bypasses rectifier-output ripple around D1, and the circuit is a linear voltage regulator — and series-regulator dissipation is P = (Vin − Vout) × Iout = (25 − 12) × 1 = 13 W in Q1.

### Modulation and Demodulation Circuits

This group is the modulator-and-detector zoo, and most of it sorts into three pairs. First, making FM. An FM phone signal is generated by reactance modulation of a local oscillator (E7E01): the reactance modulator hangs a voltage-variable capacitance on the oscillator's tank circuit and pulls the frequency with the audio, producing PM or FM (E7E02). Modulate the oscillator, never "the final amplifier" — that distractor is the trap, because by the final amplifier the frequency must already be decided. On receive, the discriminator detects FM (E7E03), converting the frequency swings back into audio, amplitude for frequency.

Making SSB is the second pair: a balanced modulator followed by a filter (E7E04). The balanced modulator mixes the audio with the carrier oscillator and cancels the carrier itself, leaving double-sideband suppressed-carrier energy; the filter then removes one sideband. Modulator cancels carrier, filter removes sideband — one clause each, in that order.

The third pair is emphasis. Pre-emphasis boosts the higher audio frequencies at the transmitter (E7E05), where they would otherwise suffer most against channel noise; de-emphasis in the receiver rolls them back down, restoring the overall frequency response and keeping compatibility with phase modulation (E7E06). Transmit boosts, receive restores — the stems swap the two ends, so pin which end you are reading about before answering. And the vocabulary word: baseband is the frequency range the message occupies before modulation (E7E07) — your 300 Hz to 3 kHz of voice before anything moves it to RF.

Mixers and detectors finish the group. A mixer outputs the two input frequencies plus their sum and their difference (E7E08) — four products, and the filter downstream keeps the one you want. Push the input levels too high and the mixer generates spurious mixer products (E7E09), extra sums and differences of the inputs and their harmonics landing where nobody planned for them. Then the three-detector sort, which the pool tests three separate times. The envelope detector handles AM by rectification and filtering of the RF (E7E10) — a diode strips off one half of the waveform and a capacitor follows the peaks, tracing the envelope the name describes. The discriminator handles FM, as above (E7E03). And SSB needs a product detector (E7E11), which mixes the incoming sideband with a locally regenerated carrier and puts the audio back at baseband — the same circuit that makes CW audible as a beat note.

That is E7E — eleven questions, E7E01 through E7E11.

### DSP and the Software-Defined Radio

A software-defined radio moves the radio's work — mixing, filtering, demodulation — into arithmetic. Two architectures lead here. The quadrature design inside most SDRs splits the amplified RF into two mixers driven by the same local oscillator 90 degrees apart, producing the I (in-phase) and Q (quadrature) channels, and digitizes each; because the two channels together preserve the signal's phase as well as its amplitude, the software can tell which side of the local oscillator a signal sits on — image rejection by arithmetic — and can reconstruct any modulation the math can describe. Direct sampling skips the mixers entirely: an ADC digitizes the incoming RF right off the front end — no local oscillator, no mixer at all (E7F01). Inside the software, an adaptive filter is the one that removes unwanted noise from a received SSB signal (E7F02), adjusting its own coefficients to null the interference, while the Hilbert-transform method is the one that generates an SSB signal, by combining signals in quadrature phase relationship (E7F03, E7F04). The distractors swap "adaptive" and "Hilbert" between those two jobs — adaptive subtracts noise, Hilbert builds sideband.

{{fig:ch07-sdr-iq}}

Sampling has laws. Nyquist's: to reproduce a signal, sample at a rate at least twice the highest frequency component (E7F05) — 15 kHz of content needs a 30 kHz sample rate, and anything faster buys margin, not extra fidelity. Undersample, and a component above half the rate doesn't vanish — it folds back down into the passband as an alias, a false signal at a false low frequency. The FFT is the transform that converts time domain to frequency domain (E7F07), the arithmetic behind every panadapter waterfall you have ever watched. Decimation reduces the effective sample rate by removing samples — keeping every fourth sample divides the rate by four — and before the samples go, a low-pass anti-alias filter must remove the high-frequency components, or they reappear in the lower-rate stream as exactly those false low-frequency components (E7F08, E7F09). Filter first, decimate second; the order is the answer.

The pool also tests the parameter split twice, with the distractors trading places between the two stems: the sample rate sets the maximum receive bandwidth (E7F10), while the reference voltage and the sample width in bits set the minimum detectable signal (E7F11). Rate is bandwidth; bits are sensitivity.

> **Worked example:** An ADC with a 1 V reference that must resolve 1 mV needs 1,000 codes; 2⁹ = 512 is too few and $2^{10} = 1024$ covers it, so the answer is 10 bits (E7F06) — 8 bits, 256 codes, is the trap. The same arithmetic in Nyquist form: to capture a signal with components up to 15 kHz, sample at 30 kHz or faster (E7F05). And decimating a 1 MS/s stream by keeping every fourth sample gives 250 kS/s effective — with everything above 125 kHz filtered out first, per E7F08 and E7F09's rule.

The FIR filter closes the group. A finite-impulse-response filter can delay all frequency components equally — linear phase, constant group delay (E7F12) — so every part of the waveform emerges the same number of samples later and the shape survives the filtering, which is why FIRs dominate audio and data paths where phase matters. The filter is a chain of delay elements with a multiplier at each stage, the taps provide the incremental delays (E7F13), and more taps give a sharper filter (E7F14) at the price of more computation per sample.

That is E7F — fourteen questions, E7F01 through E7F14.

**FACT:** Sample rate sets an SDR's maximum receive bandwidth, while the reference voltage and the sample width in bits set the minimum detectable signal — 1 V range at 1 mV resolution needs 1000 codes, so 10 bits (2¹⁰ = 1024).

### Op-Amps and Figure E7-3: One Formula, Five Answers

The operational amplifier is a high-gain, direct-coupled differential amplifier (E7G12) with very high input impedance (E7G03) and very low output impedance (E7G01): it senses the difference between its two inputs and drives its output to amplify that difference by an enormous open-loop factor. Its named imperfections are tested too. Input offset voltage is the differential input voltage needed to bring the open-loop output to zero (E7G04) — the small error a real device shows where an ideal one would show none. Gain-bandwidth is the frequency at which the open-loop gain falls to one (E7G06); the ideal op-amp, by contrast, has a gain that does not vary with frequency (E7G08). And when an op-amp audio filter rings — bursts of oscillation riding on transients, the bane of shack-built CW filters — the prevention is to restrict both gain and Q (E7G05), because the pair together is what pushes the stage to the edge of stability.

Now the third schematic, the redrawn Figure E7-3 — the inverting amplifier, and the best return-on-formula in the entire pool.

{{fig:ch07-pool-fig-e73}}

Read the topology before the numbers. R1 carries the input signal to the inverting (−) input; RF feeds the output back to that same node across the top of the triangle; the non-inverting (+) input is grounded. One formula answers all five of the figure's questions: the voltage gain is the feedback resistor over the input resistor, with a minus sign.

> **Worked example:** The gain law is $A_v = -R_F / R_1$, and every number comes straight off the redrawn Figure E7-3. RF = 470 and R1 = 10: the magnitude is 470/10 = 47, and the question asks for absolute gain, so 47 (E7G07). With 0.23 V in and RF/R1 = 10,000/1,000 = 10: the output is −10 × 0.23 = −2.3 V (E7G09) — the minus sign is the answer here and +2.3 V is the trap, because this is the one question of the five that asks for output voltage rather than absolute gain. 68 k over 1.8 k is 37.8, so approximately 38 (E7G10); 47 k over 3.3 k is 14.2, so approximately 14 (E7G11). Compute the ratio exactly, because every distractor is an arithmetic slip. And the fifth question: add a capacitor across RF and the feedback impedance falls as frequency rises, so the stage gives high frequencies less gain — a low-pass filter (E7G02).

> **The math, if you want it:** Why feedback resistor over input resistor? The open-loop gain is enormous, so with feedback connected, the inverting input is driven to sit essentially at the grounded non-inverting input's potential — a virtual ground. The input current through R1 is then just Vin/R1, and since essentially no current enters the op-amp's input itself, that same current must flow through RF, developing $V_{out} = -(R_F/R_1) V_{in}$ at the output. The minus sign is the inversion the configuration is named for.

The two stems in this group carry the pool's published typos: E7G02 prints "the circuit in E7-3" with no "Figure," and E7G07 prints "Figure E73" missing its hyphen — you will see them exactly that way on the exam, and they are quoted exactly that way in the Exam Focus below. The redraw here keeps the official Figure E7-3 caption.

That is E7G — twelve questions, E7G01 through E7G12.

**FACT:** The inverting op-amp gain law is Av = −RF/R1: RF 470/R1 10 → gain 47; −(10,000/1,000) × 0.23 V → −2.3 V out; 68 k/1.8 k → ≈38; 47 k/3.3 k → ≈14; and a capacitor across RF makes the stage a low-pass filter.

### Oscillators and Frequency Synthesis

The oscillator family has three common members, and the pool invents three fake ones: the real names are Colpitts, Hartley, and Pierce (E7H01) — Taft, Fenner, and Beane exist only in the distractor column. What distinguishes the real ones is the feedback path. The Colpitts takes its positive feedback through a capacitive divider (E7H04); the "tapped coil" choice sitting beside it is the Hartley's signature, there to catch anyone who memorized the pair as a blur. The Pierce runs its feedback through a quartz crystal (E7H05). And a crystal runs on its specified frequency when you give it the specified parallel load capacitance (E7H12) — the pF figure in the crystal's datasheet is part of the resonant circuit, not a suggestion, and the wrong load pulls the frequency off its marking.

Drift discipline comes in two flavors the pool keeps separate by raiding each question for the other's distractor. Microphonics is oscillator frequency change caused by mechanical vibration (E7H02) — the chassis gets bumped and the frequency wobbles — and the cure is mechanical: isolate the circuit from its enclosure (E7H07). Thermal drift is the other flavor, and its cure is NP0 capacitors (E7H08), the near-zero-temperature-coefficient dielectric whose value barely moves with heat. Mechanical problem, mechanical fix; thermal problem, NP0 fix — E7H07's "use NP0 capacitors" distractor is E7H08's keyed answer, so keep the two cures on their own shelves.

Synthesis is where oscillators meet arithmetic. A phase-locked loop is an electronic servo loop (E7H03): a phase detector compares a divided-down sample of the output against a stable reference oscillator, a low-pass loop filter smooths the detector's error voltage, and that control voltage steers the voltage-controlled oscillator until the divided output matches the reference exactly. While the loop is out of lock the detector produces a wandering beat; as the VCO is pulled toward the right frequency the beat slows, and at lock it stops — the two phases march together and the output is exactly N times the reference. Because N is a number you choose, a PLL can perform frequency synthesis; because the control voltage tracks any frequency wobble the loop chases, it can also perform FM demodulation (E7H06).

{{fig:ch07-pll-block}}

The direct digital synthesizer builds the waveform from numbers instead: a phase accumulator steps through phase angles at each clock tick, a lookup table turns each phase value into amplitude values representing the desired waveform (E7H10), a DAC makes the stream analog, and a low-pass anti-alias filter strips the sampling artifacts (E7H09). No loop, no VCO — just fast arithmetic with a clock as its only reference, and the clock's rate caps how high the output can go. The DDS's characteristic impurity is spurious signals at discrete frequencies (E7H11), deterministic artifacts of the finite arithmetic rather than broadband noise. Finally, at microwave frequencies the stability references are GPS-disciplined references, rubidium references, and temperature-controlled dielectric resonators — each clause independently true, so "all these choices are correct" is honestly keyed (E7H13).

That is E7H — thirteen questions, E7H01 through E7H13, and the end of the subelement.

### Exam Focus

Subelement E7 — Practical Circuits, groups E7A through E7H — puts 99 questions in the pool, and your 50-question exam draws 8 of them, tied with subelement E9 for the largest draw. The ten below span all eight groups, including three of the eleven figure questions; the two E7-3 stems carry the pool's published typos, quoted exactly as printed.

> **E7A04** How many flip-flops are required to divide a signal frequency by 16?
> A. 4
> B. 6
> C. 8
> D. 16
> **Answer: A** — Each stage divides by 2, and 16 is 2⁴ — four stages, not sixteen.

> B. Exactly 180 degrees
> C. The entire cycle

> B. Emitter bypass

> C. A passive op-amp filter

> **E7D13** Which of the following calculates power dissipated by a series linear voltage regulator?

### Key Takeaways

- One flip-flop stage divides a pulse train by 2, so dividing by 16 takes four stages; a decade counter emits one output pulse per ten input pulses, and it counts rather than decodes.

- The conduction ladder: class A conducts the whole 360°, class AB more than 180° but less than 360°, class B exactly 180°, class C less than 180°, and class D switches between saturation and cutoff behind an output filter.

- In the redrawn Figure E7-1, R1 and R2 are voltage divider bias, R3 is self bias (C3 is the emitter bypass, for AC only), and the stage is a common-emitter amplifier.

- Butterworth is maximally flat, Chebyshev trades passband ripple for a sharp cutoff, elliptical adds stop-band notches; a matching network cancels reactance and transforms resistance.

- A linear regulator varies conduction while a switchmode varies duty cycle — and Q1 in the redrawn Figure E7-2 dissipates (25 − 12) × 1 = 13 W.

- Product detector for SSB, discriminator for FM, envelope detector for AM; pre-emphasis boosts highs at the transmitter and de-emphasis restores them at the receiver.

- Nyquist says sample at twice the highest component; the sample rate sets the receive bandwidth while bits and reference voltage set the minimum detectable signal, and anti-alias filtering comes before decimation.

- Av = −RF/R1 answers all five Figure E7-3 questions; Colpitts uses a capacitive divider, Hartley a tapped coil, Pierce a crystal; a PLL is a phase detector, loop filter, VCO, and stable reference in a servo loop.
