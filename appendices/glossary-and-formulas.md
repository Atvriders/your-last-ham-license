## Appendix B: Glossary & Formulas

This appendix is for looking things up, not for reading straight through. The glossary defines every term the book uses — 502 of them — in the same plain language as the chapters: a chapter may say more about a term, but never something different. The formulas section gathers the Extra course's formula set, and every formula carries a plain statement plus a worked example built from the question pool's own numbers. The notation notes at the end explain the conventions the two sections share.

### Glossary

Every term in the book, A to Z, one plain line each.

| Carrier | The unmodulated RF signal onto which information is impressed. |
| Cathode | The diode electrode current exits in the forward direction; the package end is often marked with a stripe. |
| Cavity filter | A high-Q resonant-cavity filter — the building block of repeater duplexers. |

| Circulator | A one-way RF junction device; properly terminated on a transmitter output, it isolates the amplifier from returning energy. |

| CMOS | The digital IC family that beats TTL on power consumption. |

| Comparator | The IC that flips its output state when the input crosses a threshold voltage. |
| Conductance (G) | The real part of admittance; unit siemens. |
| Conductor | A material that carries current easily because it has many free electrons. |
| Constellation diagram | The display of a digital signal's possible phase and amplitude states for each symbol. |
| Contest exchange | The minimum information that scores a contest contact: call sign, signal report, plus the sponsor's defined fields. |
| Control operator | The licensed amateur designated by the station licensee to be responsible for the station's transmissions. |
| Control point | The location at which the control operator function is performed. |
| Controlled environment | The RF-exposure category for people aware of and able to control their exposure (occupational-level limits; may apply to household members). |
| CORES | The FCC's COmmission REgistration System, where you register to get an FRN. |

| Cross-modulation | Modulation of a desired signal by a strong undesired one — a symptom of poor dynamic range. |
| Crystal lattice filter | A narrow band-pass filter for low-level signals, built from quartz crystals. |
| CSCE | Certificate of Successful Completion of Examination — the VEs' proof you passed, valid 365 days for element credit. |
| Current | The flow of electrons in a circuit; unit ampere. |
| Cut numbers | CW contest shorthand where letters stand in for digits (N for 9, T for 0) — "599" sent as "5NN." |
| Cutoff | The fully-off endpoint of a switching transistor's operation. |
| Cutoff frequency | The half-power point of a low-pass filter. |
| CW | Continuous wave — a carrier keyed on and off; simply another name for a Morse code transmission. |
| D region | The lowest ionospheric region — the daytime absorber of the low HF bands, fading at night. |
| dBd | Antenna gain relative to a half-wave dipole. |

| dBm | Decibels relative to 1 milliwatt (0 dBm = 1 mW) — the unit of receiver noise floors and link budgets. |
| DC (direct current) | Current that flows steadily in one direction. |
| DDS (direct digital synthesis) | A frequency-generation technique giving variable frequency with crystal-oscillator stability. |
| Decade counter | A counter emitting one output pulse per 10 input pulses. |
| Decibel (dB) | A logarithmic ratio unit: +3 dB ≈ double power, +10 dB = ten times power. |

### Formulas

This is the book's complete formula set — nothing more is ever asked of you. Each relation comes with a plain statement and a worked example using the question pool's own numbers.

**Ohm's law: $V = I \times R$.** Voltage equals current times resistance — the push equals the flow times the opposition. Rearranged, the same law reads I = V / R and R = V / I, so any one of the three quantities falls out of the other two. (Carried over from Books 2 and 3.)

> **Worked example:** A resistor has 12 volts across it and 1.5 amperes flowing through it. You want R, so R = V ÷ I = 12 ÷ 1.5 = 8 Ω.

**The power law: $P = V \times I = I^2 \times R = V^2 / R$.** Three forms of the same DC power law — pick the one that matches whichever two quantities the question hands you. Only resistance dissipates real power.

> **Worked example:** 1 ampere through 100 ohms: I² × R = 100 watts — and the j100 ohms of reactance in series with it adds zero real watts (pool E5D11).

**The wavelength shortcut: $\lambda = 300 / f$.** Wavelength in meters equals 300 divided by frequency in megahertz — the pool's own approximation of wavelength = speed of light ÷ frequency with c ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s, never an exact identity. (Carried over from Books 2 and 3.)

> **Worked example:** 300 ÷ 14.10 ≈ 21.3 meters — a full wavelength at the frequency behind pool E9F06's half-wave line-length question.

**The prefix ladder.** Metric units climb by rungs: pico (10⁻¹²) → nano (10⁻⁹) → micro (10⁻⁶) → milli (10⁻³) → base unit → kilo (10³) → mega (10⁶) → giga (10⁹), each rung a factor of 1,000 from its neighbor. Moving toward a smaller unit, multiply; moving toward a larger unit, divide.

> **Worked example:** The E5C figure questions are the pool's own drill: a 38-picofarad capacitor at 14 MHz (pool E5C10), an 18-microhenry inductor at 3.505 MHz (pool E5C11).

**Decibels, defined: $dB = 10 \log_{10}(P_2/P_1)$.** Ten times the base-10 logarithm of the power ratio — first-class exam math in this book. The anchors still do most of the work: double power ≈ 3 dB, four times power ≈ 6 dB, ten times = 10 dB, one hundred times = 20 dB.

> **Worked example:** A factor of two is 10 × log₁₀(2) ≈ 3 dB. A 1 dB loss leaves 10^(−0.1) = 0.794 of the power. And the net +2.8 dB of gains and losses in pool E9A02 is a power factor of 10^(0.28) ≈ 1.9.

**Inductive reactance: $X_L = 2\pi f L$.** Frequency in hertz, inductance in henries, reactance in ohms — an inductor's opposition rises in direct proportion to frequency.

> **Worked example:** The pool's 18-microhenry inductor at 3.505 MHz (pool E5C11): 2π × 3.505×10⁶ × 18×10⁻⁶ ≈ 396 Ω — about 400 Ω, the step that puts the circuit at Point 3 on Figure E5-1.

**Capacitive reactance: $X_C = 1/(2\pi f C)$.** Frequency sits downstairs now, so a capacitor's opposition falls as frequency climbs.

> **Worked example:** The pool's 38-picofarad capacitor at 14 MHz (pool E5C10): 1 ÷ (2π × 14×10⁶ × 38×10⁻¹²) ≈ 299 Ω, about 300 Ω. The 19-picofarad capacitor at 21.2 MHz (pool E5C12): ≈ 395 Ω, about 400 Ω.

**Resonant frequency: $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$.** The frequency where an inductor-capacitor pair resonates falls straight out of the two component values — resistance never enters it.

> **Worked example:** 50 microhenries with 40 picofarads (pool E5A02): 1 ÷ (2π × √(50×10⁻⁶ × 40×10⁻¹²)) ≈ 3.56 MHz. With 10 picofarads instead (pool E5A10): ≈ 7.12 MHz.

**Resonance itself: $X_L = X_C$.** Where the two reactances are equal they cancel: in a series RLC circuit the impedance falls to its minimum, in a parallel RLC circuit it rises to its maximum (pool E5A03–E5A08). A concept row — no numbers to work.

**Half-power bandwidth: $BW = f_0/Q$.** The sharper the resonance, the narrower the bandwidth. Rearranged as Q = f₀ ÷ BW, it is pool E4B08's way of reading a resonant circuit's Q from its frequency response.

> **Worked example:** 7.1 MHz with a Q of 150 (pool E5A11): 7.1×10⁶ ÷ 150 ≈ 47.3 kHz. And 3.7 MHz with a Q of 118 (pool E5A12): ≈ 31.4 kHz.

**Circuit Q: series $Q = X/R$, parallel Q = R/X.** In a series circuit, Q is the reactance of either component divided by the resistance; in a parallel circuit, the resistance divided by the reactance (pool E5A09's definition). A loaded circuit always runs a lower Q than an unloaded one.

**Resonant voltage rise: $V_L = V_C \approx Q \times V_{applied}$.** In a series resonant circuit the voltages across the inductor and the capacitor can each run about Q times the applied voltage — resonance is the cause (pool E5A01), and raising Q raises those internal voltages (pool E5A13).

> **Worked example:** With pool E5A02's numbers — 1,118 Ω of reactance at resonance against 22 Ω of resistance — Q ≈ 51, so 10 volts applied puts about 510 volts across the coil.

**Rectangular impedance: $Z = R \pm jX$.** Resistance plus a signed reactance: +j for inductive, −j for capacitive — the j marks the 90-degree rotation between resistance and reactance.

> **Worked example:** A pure capacitive reactance of 100 ohms is written 0 − j100 (pool E5C01), and 50 − j25 means 50 ohms of resistance in series with 25 ohms of capacitive reactance (pool E5C06).

**Rectangular to polar: $|Z| = \sqrt{R^2 + X^2}$.** Resistance and reactance add as perpendicular vectors, never straight sums; the phase angle θ = atan(X/R) tells how far the circuit leans reactive.

> **Worked example:** 50 − j25 ohms (pool E5C06): √(50² + 25²) = √3,125 ≈ 55.9 Ω, at atan(−25/50) ≈ −26.6° — written 55.9 ∠−26.6° Ω.

**Polar to rectangular: R = |Z|cos θ, X = |Z|sin θ.** The reverse trip — magnitude and angle back to resistance and reactance. Same family as the row above: a concept row.

**Series RLC phase angle: θ = atan((X_L − X_C)/R).** Net reactance over resistance. A positive angle is inductive — voltage leads the current; a negative angle is capacitive — voltage lags.

> **Worked example:** X_L = 250 Ω, X_C = 500 Ω, R = 1,000 Ω (pool E5B07): atan(−250/1,000) = −14.0°, voltage lagging. X_L = 100 Ω, X_C = 300 Ω, R = 100 Ω (pool E5B08): atan(−200/100) ≈ −63°, lagging. X_L = 75 Ω, X_C = 25 Ω, R = 100 Ω (pool E5B11): atan(50/100) ≈ +27°, leading.

**Admittance: $Y = 1/Z = G + jB$.** Admittance mirrors impedance in siemens: take the reciprocal of the magnitude and change the sign of the angle (pool E5B03's rule). Conductance G is the real part; susceptance B is the imaginary part (pool E5B02, E5B05, E5B06, E5B12).

> **Worked example:** 50 − j25 Ω — that is, 55.9 ∠−26.6° Ω — becomes 1/55.9 ≈ 0.0179 S at the negated angle: about 17.9 millisiemens ∠+26.6°.

**Real versus reactive power: $P = I^2R = VI\cos\theta$.** Only resistance consumes real power; the reactive share Q = V × I × sin θ is stored and returned, never dissipated.

> **Worked example:** A 100-ohm resistor in series with a 100-ohm inductive reactance, drawing 1 ampere (pool E5D11): 1² × 100 = 100 watts — the j100 term adds zero.

**Time constant: $\tau = R \times C$.** For a capacitor, τ in seconds is resistance times capacitance; for an inductor, τ = L / R. In one τ a capacitor charges to 63.2% of the applied voltage, or discharges down to 36.8% of it (pool E5B01's definition).

> **Worked example:** Two 220-microfarad capacitors and two 1-megohm resistors, all in parallel (pool E5B04): 440 µF × 500 kΩ = 220 seconds.

**Reflection coefficient: $\Gamma = (Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L + Z_0)$.** From impedances, the mismatch ratio at the load; from the directional wattmeter instead, |Γ| = √(P_r/P_f) — the square root of reflected over forward power.

> **Worked example:** 100 watts forward and 25 watts reflected (pool E4B06's numbers): √(25/100) = 0.5.

**SWR from the reflection coefficient: $SWR = (1 + |\Gamma|)/(1 - |\Gamma|)$.** Always 1 or greater, always written with the larger number first.

> **Worked example:** |Γ| = 0.5 from the row above: (1 + 0.5)/(1 − 0.5) = 3 — a 3:1 SWR.

**Return loss in dB: $-20\log_{10}|\Gamma|$.** Ratios of amplitudes take 20·log₁₀ where ratios of power take 10·log₁₀.

> **Worked example:** |Γ| = 0.5: −20 × log₁₀(0.5) ≈ 6.0 dB of return loss.

**Power absorbed by the load: $P_{load} = P_{fwd} - P_{refl}$.** Forward minus reflected — what the wattmeter's bookkeeping leaves in the load.

> **Worked example:** 100 watts forward, 25 watts reflected (pool E4B06): 100 − 25 = 75 watts.

**Noise floor versus bandwidth: $\Delta dB = 10\log_{10}(BW_2/BW_1)$.** Receiver noise starts from the −174 dBm thermal floor in 1 Hz at room temperature and scales with bandwidth: widen the filter and the noise floor rises by ten times the log of the bandwidth ratio.

> **Worked example:** Widening from 50 Hz to 1,000 Hz (pool E4C06): 10 × log₁₀(1,000/50) = 10 × log₁₀(20) ≈ 13 dB.

**dBm to watts: $P(W) = 10^{(dBm - 30)/10}$.** dBm is decibels relative to 1 milliwatt — 0 dBm = 1 mW, 30 dBm = 1 W — so subtract 30 and divide by 10 to land on the watt exponent.

> **Worked example:** A −100 dBm minimum discernible signal (pool E4D14): 10^(−13) watts = 0.1 picowatt.

**Link budget.** Received power is transmit power plus the transmit and receive antenna gains, minus every loss and the path loss — pure dB bookkeeping: P_rx = P_tx + G_tx + G_rx − losses − path loss.

> **Worked example:** +40 dBm out, 6 dBi transmit gain, 3 dBi receive gain, 100 dB path loss (pool E4D13): 40 + 6 + 3 − 100 = −51 dBm.

**Link margin.** What remains after the receiver's noise floor and the required signal-to-noise ratio take their share: margin = P_rx − MDS − required SNR.

> **Worked example:** −89 dBm received, a −103 dBm minimum discernible signal, a 6 dB required SNR (pool E4D12): −89 − (−103) − 6 = +8 dB.

**ERP and EIRP.** Effective radiated power is transmitter power output times ten to the (gains − losses)/10: ERP = TPO × 10^((gains − losses)/10). Sign discipline first — add the gains, subtract the losses — and use dBd gains for ERP, dBi gains for EIRP.

> **Worked example:** 150 watts out, 7 dBd antenna gain, 4.2 dB of combined feed line and duplexer loss (pool E9A02): 150 × 10^(2.8/10) ≈ 286 watts ERP. Pool E9A06's chain works out to ≈ 317 watts ERP, and E9A07 — worked with the dBi gain — to ≈ 252 watts EIRP.

**Gain references: $dBd = dBi - 2.15$.** Gain over a half-wave dipole runs 2.15 dB below gain over an isotropic radiator — dBi is always the bigger number, and the reference is always named.

> **Worked example:** An antenna with 6 dB of gain over an isotropic radiator (pool E9A12): 6 − 2.15 = 3.85 dBd.

**Voltmeter input impedance.** A meter's ohms-per-volt sensitivity rating times its full-scale voltage gives the input impedance it presents to the circuit: Z_in = (Ω/V) × full-scale voltage.

> **Worked example:** A 20,000 ohms-per-volt meter on its 10-volt range (pool E4B02): 20 kΩ/V × 10 V = 200 kΩ.

**Half-wave line length: $L \approx 150 \times VF / f$.** Physical length in meters for an electrical half wavelength, frequency in megahertz — the wavelength shortcut with the velocity factor applied, because the wave slows in the line's dielectric.

> **Worked example:** An air-insulated parallel-conductor line (VF ≈ 1.0) at 14.10 MHz (pool E9F06): 150 × 1.0 ÷ 14.10 ≈ 10.6 meters.

**The transformation table: λ/2 repeats, λ/4 inverts, λ/8 converts.** A half-wave line repeats its termination at the input end; a quarter-wave line inverts it — short becomes open, open becomes short; an eighth-wave line becomes a reactance — shorted turns inductive, open turns capacitive.

> **Worked example:** The pool's own set: a shorted half-wave line shows a very low impedance (pool E9F04); a shorted quarter-wave line shows a very high impedance (E9F09), an open quarter-wave a very low one (E9F12); a shorted eighth-wave line is an inductive reactance (E9F10), an open eighth-wave a capacitive one (E9F11).

**Quarter-wave Q-section: $Z_0 = \sqrt{Z_{line} \times Z_{load}}$.** The matching line's characteristic impedance is the geometric mean of the two impedances being joined — the same λ/4 transformer relation rearranged reads Z′ = Z₀²/Z.

> **Worked example:** Matching a 100-ohm feed point to 50-ohm coax (pool E9E06): √(50 × 100) ≈ 70.7 Ω — the nearest standard line impedance, 75 Ω, does the job.

**Inverting op-amp gain: $A_v = -R_F/R_1$.** The gain of Figure E7-3's circuit is set by the feedback and input resistors alone, with a minus sign for the inversion — the output is V_out = −(R_F/R_1) × V_in. The pool's best return-on-formula: one circuit, four resistor pairs.

> **Worked example:** R_F = 470 Ω and R_1 = 10 Ω (pool E7G07): gain 47. R_F = 10 kΩ, R_1 = 1 kΩ, with 0.23 volts DC in (pool E7G09): −10 × 0.23 = −2.3 volts out. 68 kΩ over 1.8 kΩ (pool E7G10): ≈ 38. And 47 kΩ over 3.3 kΩ (pool E7G11): ≈ 14.

**Series-regulator dissipation: $P = (V_{in} - V_{out}) \times I_{out}$.** A linear regulator burns the voltage it drops times the current it passes — the heat the heatsink must handle (pool E7D13's definition).

> **Worked example:** 25 volts in, 12 volts out, 1 ampere of load current: (25 − 12) × 1 = 13 watts.

**Battery operating time.** Capacity in amp-hours divided by the average current drawn (pool E7D09's definition).

> **Worked example:** A 10 amp-hour battery at a 2-ampere average draw: 10 ÷ 2 = 5 hours.

**ADC resolution: $2^n$ levels for n bits.** Each added bit doubles the number of input levels the converter can distinguish.

> **Worked example:** 8 bits encode 2⁸ = 256 levels (pool E8A09). Resolving 1 millivolt across a 1-volt range takes 1,000 codes, so 10 bits — 2¹⁰ = 1,024 — is the minimum (pool E7F06).

**Nyquist sampling rate.** To be accurately reproduced, an analog signal must be sampled at least twice as fast as its highest frequency component (pool E7F05's rule).

> **Worked example:** A 15 kHz signal needs a sample rate of at least 2 × 15 = 30 kHz.

**Modulation index: $index = \Delta f / f_{mod}$.** Peak deviation divided by the modulating frequency. Its worst-case sibling, the deviation ratio, uses the maximum deviation and the maximum modulating frequency.

> **Worked example:** 3,000 Hz of deviation at a 1,000 Hz modulating frequency (pool E8B03): index 3 — likewise 6 kHz at 2 kHz (pool E8B04). Deviation ratio: 5 kHz at 3 kHz (pool E8B05): 1.67; 7.5 kHz at 3.5 kHz (pool E8B06): 2.14.

**CW bandwidth: $BW \approx 4 \times WPM$.** Faster keying widens the signal — about four hertz of bandwidth per word per minute of speed.

> **Worked example:** 13 WPM (pool E8C05): 4 × 13 ≈ 52 Hz.

**FSK/data bandwidth.** Approximately 1.2 times the frequency shift plus the baud rate: BW ≈ (1.2 × shift) + baud.

> **Worked example:** A 4,800-Hz shift at 9,600 baud (pool E8C07): 1.2 × 4,800 + 9,600 = 15.36 kHz.

**Far-field power density: $S = ERP/(4\pi R^2)$.** The shape of an MPE evaluation: the ERP spread over the surface of a sphere of radius R, so exposure falls with distance squared. Flagged as enrichment — no E0A question requires this calculation; duty cycle and time-averaging then lower the effective exposure further.

> **Worked example:** (Enrichment — no E0A question requires this calculation.) Your antenna system radiates 100 watts ERP and the lot line is 10 meters away: 100 ÷ (4π × 10²) ≈ 0.08 W/m² (0.008 mW/cm²) — comfortably under the MPE limits, whose strictest uncontrolled value is 2 W/m² (0.2 mW/cm²) across 30–300 MHz, and conversational-SSB duty with 30-minute uncontrolled averaging cuts the effective exposure further still.

### Notation & Units

**V and ×, and the pool's own words.** This book's prose uses V for voltage and × for multiplication (V = I × R), exactly as Books 2 and 3 do. The 2024–2028 Extra pool states electrical quantities in words — "a 400-ohm resistor and a 38-picofarad capacitor" — and prints complex impedances and angles in ASCII ("0 - j100," "90 degrees"). Verbatim pool quotes always keep the pool's own typography, so a quote never conflicts with the prose convention.

**Complex-number conventions.** Impedance is written rectangular (Z = R ± jX: +j inductive, −j capacitive) or polar (|Z| ∠θ — degrees always). The book uses the engineering j, never i, with the house minus sign and the j attached to the number ("400 − j300 Ω"), and polar form with the angle marker ("55.9 ∠−26.6° Ω"). Admittance mirrors impedance in siemens (Y = G + jB), its polar angle negated.

**Unit case is load-bearing.** Write kHz with a lowercase k, MHz and GHz with a capital M or G, and always a capital H; mA, µV, pF, nF, and kV follow the same prefix case rules — never "KHZ," "mhz," or "Mhz." Subscripts are set as subscripts: X_L, X_C, f₀, Z₀, P_fwd, P_refl, S21, S11.

**The working constants.** The speed of light is c = 3×10⁸ m/s = 300,000 km/s, the working value; frequency and period are reciprocals, f = 1 / T. dBm is decibels relative to 1 milliwatt (0 dBm = 1 mW). Ratios of amplitudes take 20·log₁₀; ratios of power take 10·log₁₀.

**The hobby's customary units.** Antenna lengths come out in feet from the 468/f-style formulas and are always approximate; transmission-line lengths come out in meters (the pool's own E9F06 unit); propagation paths are in miles; coax loss is quoted in dB per 100 feet; antenna gains are in dBi or dBd with the reference always named. SWR is always written with the larger number first — "3:1," never "1:3."

**Displayed math.** Inline expressions use the same math style as the chapters, at most one math span per paragraph; the renderer supports subscripts, Greek letters, roots, and fractions — the whole formula set above.
